Chapter Two Hundred Ninety-Eight

The Breakup

Friday 22:05 → Saturday 09:47 a chairman’s office after the phones go quiet · a Myeongdong alley with the engine ticking · a CCM conference room with the blinds down · a Gangnam apartment with no standards · a hillside villa, lights low · and one doorbell that took too long to answer one dinner eaten three ways · one president dealing chips to his own group · one smile keeping a file behind its back · one guest room with thin walls · and one bouquet of white roses that never made it upstairs

Park Ji-yeon spent the evening the way the devoted spend evenings at altars: installed on the office sofa, tablet on her knees, thumbs at war with some puzzle game, and every third glance lifted over the screen to the man behind the desk, where a stack of acquisition paperwork was being disassembled decision by decision.

Quiet work bored her senseless, historically. Watching him do it did not. There was a whole vocabulary in the way he squared a page before setting it aside, and she was learning to read it, and the learning itself felt like a privilege.

Past ten, he stretched until his spine reported in.

“Mm… aaah~”

She bounced upright off the sofa and came over at a delighted trot.

“Darling? All done?”

“All done.” He caught her by the waist and kissed one smooth cheek, then the other, weighing them. “Come on. Something delicious.”

“Delicious” switched on every light in the building.

“Yes! Yes yes —” She had his arm and was towing him doorward before the sentence landed. “Darling, ppalli, ppalli~”

✦ ✦ ✦

Myeongdong, a side street the tourists hadn’t found yet, and the black Maybach drawn up in the dark like a rumor with license plates. The secretary had gone in for the order. The wait was supposed to be four minutes.

She spent the first three of them in his lap.

Kneeling across him in the wide back seat, arms linked behind his neck, kissing him with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman who has nowhere better to be and knows it. The pale pink off-the-shoulder dress had given up on structural integrity somewhere around the second minute; lamplight from the alley did what it could with her shoulders, which was plenty.

His hands made a slow survey of her (the long clean line of her thighs, the narrow architecture of her waist) and he conducted it with the contented attention of a man rereading a favorite chapter.

Heels on pavement, approaching, crisp as a countdown.

She broke the kiss by a reluctant half-inch, eyes gone bright and swimmy, and floated him a wink of pure banked trouble before sliding sideways off his lap. Then a glance downward at the state of his trousers, and the blush arrived on schedule, and she hid her mouth behind one hand and laughed.

“Uncomfortable? Good. That’s what you get for bullying me.”

“Noted.” He adjusted the trouser leg with the gravity of a diplomat. “Then tonight, no begging for mercy. Fair warning.”

Her smile stopped where it was and audibly changed gear. No… begging? That clause was drafted by someone who had never met her at her limits. Perhaps she should just apologize now, while the apology was still cheap—

A knuckle rapped the window. Soo-bin, backlit by the alley, holding stacked takeout boxes and radiating professional blindness.

“Get in. We’re going up the hill.”

“Ne~”

Boxes stowed on the passenger seat, engine woken, the city unwinding behind them. Jiyeon sniffed the air like a small predator who has located the herd.

“Soo-bin-ah. What is in there?”

“Woodari ganjang gejang. The Myeongdong one. Famous.”

“Famous how?”

“Top-five-gejang-house famous. A-grade roe from Yeonpyeong-do, cured low and slow in a soy sauce the family won’t discuss; the meat sets like jelly, the roe like jam, and over rice it has ended friendships. A Japanese food writer wrote it up like a religious site. It’s been on the food programs. Half the industry has made the pilgrimage, some of them in disguise.”

“And the other boxes?”

“Grilled pork belly and beef. Four portions.”

Four portions. For three people. Jiyeon heard the arithmetic and blessed it, staring holes into the boxes with watering mouth, and Soo-bin watched her in the mirror: the small solemn greedy face of a woman being taken somewhere delicious, and put her foot down, and the hill began.

✦ ✦ ✦

Across the river, five women in evening coats came up out of the elevator at CCM and were met by the president’s secretary, whose face had clearly been having a long night of its own.

“He’s waiting for you.”

Kwang-soo stood at his window with his back to the door. When he turned, the face was composed to neutral, an official portrait of a man, but underneath the calm, banked and pressurized, the rage showed through like heat shimmer.

“All here. Sit.”

“President, you called us back, so—” Eun-jung began, and his hand rose and closed the subject.

“You’ve seen the news. LOEN has acquired CCM.” His gaze went along the row of them, unhurried. “Anything anyone would like to say?”

Qri studied her own knees. Soyeon and Hyomin found separate and fascinating regions of the carpet.

“We only saw it tonight,” Eun-jung managed. “It’s… sudden.”

“Sudden.” The word came out with a laugh filed off it. “CJ has been trying to drop this company for a year (I was the one negotiating to keep it), and LOEN cut the line mid-river. Yes, the boy writes songs. Two of them, apparently, are enough to buy a company he knows nothing about.” He came around the desk. “What does CCM do for him? It’s a gift. Gift wrapping, ribbon, a company inside: for a girlfriend. And you believe a man who makes purchases like that for a woman can operate one?”

The question needed no witness. He softened by half a degree and moved closer, the veteran statesman now, voice dropping into the register that had closed a thousand deals.

“Every one of you, I built. There is no T-ara today that isn’t standing on years of my blood. New owners arrive. How will they treat you? Does that boy know the first thing about running talent?” His eyes settled and held. “Eun-jung. You were the first one through this company’s door. You tell me. What do we do?”

Her chest tightened. Qri’s coaching was still warm in her ear (idiots, all of us), but the president’s gaze had weight, and the weight had history in it.

A fingertip touched the hem of her sleeve. Once.

“President.” A breath, taken properly. “We’re artists. The business of the company is beyond us. We follow the company’s arrangements.”

Something went out behind his eyes, and his attention slid sideways to Qri and priced her in one motion.

“The company’s arrangements. The company is LOEN’s now. You’ll follow Song Ji-ho?”

“President, that isn’t what we—” Soyeon started.

“Then what is it?” He was no longer softening. “I know about Jiyeon and Song Ji-ho. You think the mountain has a soft spot for you now? You are still under boycott. When LOEN takes over, will they spend real money clearing five names, or will they shelve five names and pour every resource into one? You’ll all be set dressing in the fairytale. Have any of you costed that out?”

Nobody had. The silence said so.

He let it sit, then eased back into the warm register, moving behind the desk, a man concluding a toast.

“My arrangement with CJ isn’t finished. After the acquisition I remain president, in the short term, at minimum. While I sit in this chair, you have my protection. Your comeback is already in planning. I am working on the matter of your names. But protection is a two-way street, and I need to see where you stand.”

Qri heard it land exactly as she’d predicted: chips. He meant to walk into the new era holding five of them.

“What would you need from us?” Eun-jung asked, bravery arriving late but arriving.

“Nothing operatic.” He sat. “When LOEN sends people to take over, you cooperate. Pleasantly, completely. But day to day, your schedules, your activities: those run through me. A united group is the one card I can still play. Show me the card is real.”

“But—” Boram’s voice came out small. “Won’t that just sharpen the fight? Between you and them?”

“I know my measures.” One glance, and Boram folded back into the sofa. “Who stood in front of you when the whole internet was baying? I did. The company changes hands, and this is the week you choose to forget it?”

The room arrived at its deadlock. Eun-jung sat with the guilt settling on her like weather. Qri kept her eyes down and her conclusions to herself: the man had shielded them, yes; he had also been shielding the asset, and the difference had never once mattered to him.

And Park Hyomin, whose face had spent the entire meeting in respectful trouble, was running a completely different conversation inside.

Follow you? Into a shrinking room, behind a man whose own buyers stop returning his calls? President, with respect: the door just opened onto the largest music platform on the peninsula (promotion, charts, distribution, everything CCM scratches for), and you’re asking me to hold your coat while you argue with it. No. The smart money introduces itself early. Defection wants sincerity, and sincerity wants something to trade.

She let the silence season a moment longer, then broke it, sweet and bright, exactly on the beat the room needed.

“President, he raised every one of us himself~ Of course we trust you most. What are a few new owners next to that?”

Qri’s head came up one inch too fast, one inch too late.

“Then here’s what I need.” His whole face warmed toward Hyomin like a plant toward a window. “Starting tomorrow, you—”

✦ ✦ ✦

Gangnam, a private bar with the lights at half. Bottles lay across the table in the arrangement of a lost battle. Oh Jong-hyuk was deep in the country of the drunk, sprawled slack against the sofa, eyes fogged. Across from him sat a tall model, a friend of Kim Tae-hyung’s; her name had been supplied once and had not survived the soju.

“Oppa~ one more?”

She leaned in with the glass, perfume and alcohol arriving ahead of her. He squinted at the face hovering near his, and the face wavered and was briefly, ruinously, someone else’s: the gentle one, the patient one, the one who had learned his coffee order at nineteen.

“No more.” An irritable flick of the hand, dismissing the ghost along with the glass.

She wasn’t offended. She set the glass down and moved nearer instead, one fingertip drawing slow circles on his chest.

“Oppa’s in a mood~? Shall we go somewhere quieter? I’m very good at company.”

Kim Tae-hyung caught the drift from two seats down, performed a heroic bit of eyebrow semaphore, and stood.

“Bathroom.”

The door clicked. In the dim, the model settled onto Jong-hyuk’s lap as if the seat had been reserved.

And the last frayed cord of two years’ discipline parted without any sound at all. Why should he be the tied one? Always the schedule, the group, the waiting; she hadn’t even come to the gate. Here was a woman who asked nothing and offered company, and the arithmetic of the drunk is never wrong in its own courtroom.

“Let’s go.”

The dorm room, later, was dark and graceless about it. He took the last two years out on a stranger, and the stranger accommodated him with professional economy, and when it was over he was asleep before his breathing could settle. She sat up against the headboard, found her cigarettes, and considered the shape in the sheets beside her.

No good, this one. Money like this is earned against the conscience.

The Maybach took the hill road unhurried, walled gardens and a few late cicadas, and let itself into the villa garage a little before midnight. Soo-bin came behind them with the boxes, a torchbearer at a very small, very fragrant procession.

“HOME!” Jiyeon was out of her heels before the door finished opening, barefoot on the cold marble, hopping from foot to foot toward the dining room. “Quick quick quick, I’m DYING—”

“Table’s fine, Soo-bin-ah. And you’re eating with us.”

“Oh, I couldn’t, I’ll just—”

“You’re eating.”

The tone closed the matter with a soft click. Soo-bin bowed to the inevitable and fetched a third setting.

Woodari · Myeongdong · collected by the secretary · after midnight

The gejang — A-grade hen crab from Yeonpyeong-do, married into a soy the house will not describe, held at low temperature until the meat sets translucent and the roe turns to a red-orange jam that stains the rice on contact; praised in print by a Japanese food writer, filmed twice, and credited with ending at least one friendship

Grilled pork belly and beef — four portions’ worth, edges lacquered, still holding their smoke; the correct answer to any question the gejang raises

House rule · the chairman pours, and the guest who claims she isn’t staying eats first, before she can resume the argument

Served with a red wine nobody photographed, on a night three people will remember for different lengths of time.

Jiyeon fell on the crab with the reverence of the starving and the table manners of a happy raccoon. He stopped her hand once, gently, to pour the wine (“One glass first. The crab isn’t going anywhere.”), and she pouted the full pout and picked the glass up anyway, and the three of them touched rims, and the evening loosened its knot.

“This gejang… it’s SO good—” A crab leg vanished; her eyes closed into the satisfied squint of a cat in a sunbeam. “Soo-bin-ah, eat! Why are you just watching us!”

“I’m eating, I’m eating.” She took a piece of the pork belly and chewed it slowly, a woman pacing herself for a long evening of other people’s happiness.

Two glasses in, Jiyeon turned pink at the cheekbones and generous with her feelings, and hung off his arm.

“Darling. You worked SO hard today. I’m toasting you.”

“You should drink less.” He ruffled her hair and drank the toast with her anyway.

Soo-bin’s two glasses lived loudly in her face. She checked the time, rose, began the goodbyes…

“Hoejang-nim, it’s late, I should—”

“You’ve been drinking. How are you driving?” One glance, no argument in it anywhere. “Stay tonight. The guest room’s made up.”

“…Then I’ll trouble you, Hoejang-nim.”

Jiyeon, well past tipsy, had migrated onto his shoulder and was addressing the room from there.

“Darling. I’m sleeeepy—”

“Guest room, second floor, left. Help yourself to anything. Come on, you.” The last part was for the weight on his shoulder, already half a sleepover.

“Ne~”

He had the bath running before he noticed the audience. She stood in the doorway on the fog of steam, watching him test the water temperature with her wrist instead of his own, eyes gone soft-focus and unblinking.

“What is it?”

Answer: none. She crossed the tiles on a sailor’s line and put both arms around him from the front, face pressed to his chest.

“Wash together… okay?”

The mirror lost its reflection to the steam. Water found every inch of her that the dress had claimed back in the alley, and the pale pink stayed crumpled on the floor where the apricot had once stayed in a hotel hallway; some garments in this story exist to be evidence.

She went up on her toes against the slick of his shoulder and kissed him first, again, as if firsts were a collection she was building. The heat of the water and the cold of the tile took turns with her, and she shivered her way through both without once letting go of his neck.

From the bath to the sink. From the sink, dripping a dotted line, to the bed.

He laid her down into the pillows and her hair made a dark fan of itself, and she looked up at him through the last of the wine and remembered a black car and a trouser leg adjusted with diplomatic gravity, and the laugh escaped before she could card it.

“What’s funny?” He bent to her ear and worried the lobe, gently, once.

“It tickles—” She twisted away, laughing at a different pitch now.

The room warmed by degrees past what the thermostat had to do with.

“Darling… it’s late… we should sleep~”

“Didn’t you say it served me right?”

She had no sentences left. What she had was a glare, soft as pastry and about as menacing, and she aimed it up at him, and watched it fail, and knew it had failed, and suspected it had been designed to.

The house settled. The small hours arrived and stayed. Long after the last of the water sounds, a weight rearranged itself against his side and found its notch.

She was too spent to lift a finger and lifted her head anyway, and set a small kiss on the point of his chin, precise as a stamp on a document she meant to file forever.

“Good night, darling.”

“Good night.” His arm cinched; his mouth found her forehead; the lamp went.

One floor down and one wall over, Chae Soo-bin lay in the guest room staring at a ceiling she was too polite to name, while the house’s late plumbing conducted itself, faintly, in surround. She turned over. She pulled the quilt over her head. She conducted, privately, a brief reevaluation of the phrase make yourself at home, and slept.

✦ ✦ ✦

The T-ara dorm came home late and split into its separate silences.

Qri sat on the edge of her bed with the bedside lamp on and the president’s voice running on a loop under her hair. The meeting had gone almost exactly as she’d mapped it (the doctrine had held, the girls had held), and it was the almost that sat wrong. She picked up her phone, opened the thread with Jiyeon, typed four words, read them, and deleted them one by one. Tomorrow, with a clear head. Threads started at midnight had a way of ending in daylight with compound interest.

Next door, Soyeon lay staring at the ceiling, running the president’s arithmetic against her own pulse. Set dressing for Jiyeon’s fairytale. Was that the plan for the other four of them? She turned it over twice and put it down. Jiyeon wasn’t built that way. She’d known her for years. She just wasn’t. The faith was manual, willed, load-bearing.

Across the dorm, Park Hyomin lay face-down on her bed with her chin on her folded arms, wearing the faint smile of a woman whose portfolio had just improved. The old man honestly believed he was still steering. She scrolled a year of LOEN’s public filings with the brightness of the screen in her eyes, filing away the org chart, the business lines, the music-platform numbers: a courier’s map of where to carry what she knew. Delivery, tomorrow. Sincerity required proof, and proof required legwork.

Eun-jung sat against her headboard with the lamp on, holding two truths with both hands and dropping both, repeatedly. The president’s voice, with its fifteen years of receipts. Qri’s fingertip on her sleeve, with its one clean instruction. She had parroted the doctrine and meant it and felt like a traitor anyway, and no arrangement of the feelings made them sit flush. She sighed at the lamp until the lamp won, and turned it off.

And Boram, under her blanket, listened to a passing car and made her request of the universe modestly: whatever came next. Please just let it not be worse.

After midnight, the dorm went quiet and stayed that way, which none of them believed yet.

✦ ✦ ✦

Saturday came up gray and soft over the city, and Park So-yeon woke before her alarm with the residue of too many dreams and none of them recoverable. No new messages. Fine. Enough of phones.

Go and talk to him. Face to face, like adults. It’s one conversation.

She washed, drew on light makeup with a steadier hand than she expected, and put on the new dress, the one bought weeks ago for a lunch she’d pictured happening after a homecoming. On the way she detoured to the florist, and came out carrying white roses, his favorite, wrapped in paper the color of morning.

The knot in her chest rode along unbuckled. She told it the same thing at every red light: a normal visit. Two people who love each other, talking. The roses agreed on her behalf.

At his building she stood at the door with the bouquet held the way one holds an apology one has rehearsed, and pressed the bell, and waited.

And waited.

The door opened at last on a man wearing last night as an outfit (hair in open rebellion, breath a distillery), and Kim Tae-hyung was still assembling his face when recognition arrived and finished the job sobriety had failed to do.

“You—”

Behind him, unhurried, a woman in the previous evening’s clothes strolled out of the hallway, installed a kiss on Tae-hyung’s cheek with the ease of a stamp on a frequent flyer’s card, looked Soyeon over once, top to bottom, no hurry, and click-clicked away toward the elevator.

The elevator doors closed with a sound like a struck match.

The cold arrived in Soyeon’s face before her mind had approved it. She looked at Tae-hyung. She looked past him, at the door standing half open on a dark hallway. Something in her, some instrument tuned over a thousand days, began to sound before anything in the room made a sound.

“Soyeon-ah, that is, listen, he—”

He moved to fill the doorway with his body, which was the wrong arithmetic; she moved him like weather and went in, straight down the hall, to the door she knew, which was not locked, which she opened.

The room did what such rooms do. Oh Jong-hyuk, bare to the waist, one arm thrown over a woman as bare, both of them sheeted to the waist and sleeping the stone sleep of the thoroughly drunk. The floor wore last night’s evidence in two genres of clothing. The air kept the bar’s receipts: soju, perfume, the aftermath of the one thing there was no mistaking.

Her hands closed on the bouquet until the paper crackled and her knuckles went the color of the wrap.

She knew the face in the bed. Small-time model, party-circuit regular, a face that had smiled at her at least once across some industry table, which was its own kind of sentence.

The impulses arrived in a rush and ranked themselves: scream, wake them, take the roses by the stem and lay them across his sleeping face stem-first. She stood in the doorway and let the whole parade pass through her.

And let it go by. Because the aftermath was already written: the noise, the neighbors, the names. If she made the scene, the story would not be his. The story would be hers: the woman of T-ara, screaming in a doorway. Ugly is a debt the wronged still have to pay themselves.

“Soyeon-ah.” Tae-hyung, wretched, from the hallway. “He was drunk, he didn’t… last night, the company news, he—”

She was already leaving. Past him, through the hallway, out the door and into the corridor, fast, past brisk, into the pace of a person outrunning something that had already caught her.

“Soyeon!”

His voice hit the stairwell behind her. It might as well have been the elevator music.

In the lobby she did the last thing with the roses: held them over the bin by the doors and opened her hands. White scattered onto black liner, petals bruising on impact, paper folding into itself: a bouquet that had been for a man who no longer existed, disposed of at the scene.

In the car, both hands on the wheel, she shook. Windows up, doors shut, engine off: a sealed jar with one person inside it. The shaking ran its full course, and then, finally, the tears came through in force.

She folded over the wheel and sobbed, loudly, ugly, both hands still at ten and two as if some discipline might still be salvaged from the morning. The sound stayed in the car, muffled by glass and upholstery, shut in with her where no one could audit it. Like her chest: closed up, and aching, and full.

Why? Was she not good enough? Was two years not enough? He enlisted four months in. Four months! And she had waited like it was a job she’d applied for, and the whole industry had lined up to make the waiting harder, and she had never once, not one card, not one number, not one late reply. Two years of clean hands. And this is what the thousandth day bought.

The phone buzzed against the cup holder. She lifted her tear-blurred eyes to it: Qri’s name, patient and punctual, a friend doing the arithmetic of a worried morning.

She watched it ring itself out. Right now she could not have borne a single human voice. Not even a kind one. Especially not a kind one.

In the bin by the lobby doors, the white roses were already going gray at the edges, keeping the only appointment they had left.
