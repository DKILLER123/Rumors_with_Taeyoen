Chapter Ninety-Three

The Wager

Meeting Taeyeon’s eyes — the bewilderment in them, the hurt, the thin thread of pleading — something wrenched hard in Ji-ho’s chest.

It was true: they had never formally started dating.

But the temperature in this room had every alarm in his head ringing. This posture — over one blurry photo? No. There was something else.

What is it? Where were we careless — where’s the handle they’re holding—

“We—” Taeyeon tried again, voice dry, fighting for composure. “We are not toge—”

“We’re dating.”

A calm voice cut straight through hers.

Ji-ho stepped forward and took Taeyeon’s hand.

Her head snapped around; she stared at him in open disbelief.

He stood there, entirely level, and said it again, one word at a time.

“Yes. Taeyeon nuna and I are seeing each other.”

Her mind whited out.

What is he saying? WHY—

She almost pulled her hand back. Almost protested — it’s not like that, we like each other but we never actually began — and then she saw the resolve in his eyes, and the way Kim Young-min’s face had gone from stone to colder stone, and a terrible premonition closed over her.

“Good.” Lee Soo-man did not look surprised. He only sighed — deep, heavy, disappointed — and the sound of it landed on Taeyeon’s heart like a dropped weight.

He didn’t look at Ji-ho again. He reached across the desk, picked up a tablet, unlocked it, opened a video, and turned the screen toward them.

Dim footage. The company production room, from the ceiling corner. Timestamp: last night, very late.

Two people in the frame.

Taeyeon against the wall, on her toes, arms wound around Ji-ho’s neck. His head bent to hers. The two of them — kissing. Unhurried. Unmistakable.

No audio. None needed. That degree of closeness torched every possible “sunbae mentoring a hoobae” defense on contact.

The color went out of Taeyeon’s face completely — paper-white, bloodless. Cold swept her body; a fine tremor started in her shoulders.

They knew. The company already KNEW.

Last night, two people drowning in half a month of missing each other had both forgotten the same small thing: production rooms have cameras.

The scandal broke; the company traced their habits; their habits led straight to that room; the room kept receipts. So the questioning just now had never been a question at all. It was a test — of whether they would tell the truth.

And they — she had denied it. The kiss was fact; the not-dating was also fact; and in front of that screen, the distinction was ash. Ji-ho must have seen this coming — must have realized that against hard evidence, the technical truth would read as one more lie. So he’d chosen the other truth. The one their hearts had already signed.

Panic, shame, and the naked helplessness of being seen — all of it broke over her at once. Tears came up with no permission and blurred everything. She opened her mouth to explain — the kiss was real but they hadn’t defined it, they were only just beginning, seonsaeng-nim, please—

“Now.” Kim Young-min’s voice was refrigerated. “Is there anything left to say?”

The tears spilled. Her voice wouldn’t assemble. She looked to Ji-ho with everything collapsing in her eyes—

—and a warm, steady hand closed firmly around her cold, shaking one.

He moved a half-step forward, putting her slightly behind his shoulder, and met the two most powerful men in the building without blinking. His grip tightened: a message with no sound in it. I’m here.

She froze — then looked down at their joined hands, felt the heat and the steadiness coming through his palm, and the panic crushing her chest eased by exactly one breath.

At least this man doesn’t run. At least he stands and carries it.

Whatever happened next — this one moment of safety, she would keep.

She raised her wet eyes to his profile. Calm. Set.

He didn’t look at her. He held her hand and repeated his “lie” to the company’s two supreme decision-makers, clearly, evenly:

“As I said. Taeyeon nuna and I are dating.”

Her lips moved. In the end, nothing came out. Instead her fingers turned and locked through his — hard, like a drowning grip on the only driftwood in a storm sea. She lowered her head, done with defending, and let the tears run silently — but the worst of the shaking, hand in his, slowly stilled.

The office was a held breath. On the tablet, the paused kiss glowed on in silence — evidence no one could argue with, of a feeling no rulebook had ever fully caged.

Lee Soo-man’s gaze moved off their joined hands and up to Ji-ho’s face, unreadable and complicated. He leaned back slowly. His fingers took up their tapping again. The silence sharpened; he set the tablet down, folded his hands, drew breath to speak—

“You two. Step outside first.”

Kim Young-min had beaten him to it.

A blink of surprise from both — then a silent bow, and they left the office hand in hand.

The manager, the department head, Tiffany, and Seohyun were all waiting in the corridor. Four pairs of eyes dropped to the joined hands. Four faces went still.

Wasn’t it… just a rumor?

Tiffany’s face lost its color. Her fists closed slowly, nails biting her palms.

DaeDae and Ji-ho? How? When I’m the one who— Was it because I only ever talked music with him? Because I never gave him a real signal?

But if a man had no interest in you — who texts you until two in the morning through a schedule like his?

Shock, confusion, silence, settling over the corridor like snow.

✦   ✦   ✦

Half an hour later, they were called back in.

One person had been added to the room: the head of Legal, Kim Ah-ju.

Lee Soo-man adjusted his glasses and laid out the terms.

“The company gives you two roads.”

“One. You may continue — under a supplementary agreement. Publicly, the relationship is denied. Privately, the company will not pursue it — provided that for two years, not one frame of substantive evidence surfaces. Ever.”

“And Ji-ho — the company will proceed with your debut as planned. In exchange, you sign a new debut contract. Consider it the price of your carelessness — and the insurance the company requires to keep investing its resources in you.”

Kim Ah-ju drew a prepared draft from her folder, slid it to the desk’s edge, and read the core terms at speed.

Revised Debut Contract (Draft) · Supplementary Terms
Term

Overseas contract term extended by five (5) years — total term twelve (12) years.

Revenue

Album/digital splits, overseas activity splits, and acting commissions revised downward. Ratios reviewable against market performance after three (3) years.

Rights & Obligations

All copyrights exclusively licensed to the Company. Unconditional compliance with Company scheduling for the full term. Priority renewal right to the Company. Elevated liquidated damages.

— read aloud in under a minute; designed to be signed in under two.

Kim Young-min supplied the second road, cold and hard.

“Two. Deny the relationship. The ‘accident’ explanation goes out as the official line.”

“Ji-ho — your debut plan terminates immediately. The two of you break off completely. All contact ends. And Taeyeon — your contract is still in this company’s hands. Your solo development, your team activities — all of it runs on the company’s goodwill. If you defy us and insist on your own way—” the pause carried the whole threat, “—the company has the means, and the justification, to let you ‘rest’ for a very long time.”

“You have half an hour. Consider it carefully — outside.” Lee Soo-man waved a hand.

The manager led a hollow-eyed Taeyeon and a silent Ji-ho out of that suffocating office and into an empty meeting room next door.

The door shut. Two people. One clock.

Taeyeon stood against the wall, trembling faintly, being torn down the middle by the arithmetic.

Option one? He signs a twelve-year chain. His talent, his future — padlocked, because of me. How do I watch him walk into that fire?

Option two? His debut dies. And we— we—

Even thinking the word break up dragged a dull blade through her chest. And seonsaeng-nim’s last line — the one about “resting” — sat in her like an icicle. She could not drag Girls’ Generation down. Could not fail the members. And could not — could not — picture a future with Ji-ho subtracted from it.

Which road? Both of them end in a cliff.

“Nuna.”

His voice pulled her up out of the spiral. He crossed to her and drew her gently in.

She didn’t resist. Face buried in the hollow of his shoulder, tears soaking silently into his collar, she could feel the steadiness of his arms — and, under her cheek, a heartbeat every bit as heavy as her own.

He closed his eyes, chin resting on her hair, breathing in the scent of it.

Road one: slow poison. Trade the future’s freedom and every split for a debut and permission to love her in the dark.

Road two: let go of a woman’s heart — and know that hearts released like that are almost never returned.

Bend, and sign the chain? Or give her up?

No.

His mind was sprinting now, hunting the gap that almost certainly didn’t exist. The songs sitting in the company library. Tiffany’s and Taeyeon’s faces last night when “I Got a Boy” and “Growl” hit their ears. And the fact — the bedrock fact — that Lee Soo-man and Kim Young-min were, before anything else, businessmen.

Would they throw away Taeyeon? Cornered — maybe. Given a better option — never.

A long moment. His eyes opened. Decision made.

He bent his head and kissed the cold tears from her cheek — they tasted bitter — and then his lips found hers, gentle at first, then deepening as she answered without meaning to, trading back and forth the breath they’d each been missing for half a month.

A century, or a second. He eased back, arms still around her.

He looked into her tear-blurred, still-beautiful eyes, and said, with complete seriousness:

“Nuna. Let’s date. For real.”

She shivered — one bright flash of light in her eyes, drowned immediately by a deeper pain. She shook her head slowly, voice sanded raw.

“Not like this— don’t sign that contract. We— we could—”

She couldn’t finish. The two syllables of break up refused to leave her body.

He bent and stopped the rest of the sentence with a soft kiss.

“Trust me.” Forehead against hers. “There might be a third road.”

Under her blank, faintly hoping stare, he took her hand and pulled her toward the door at a run.

“Where are we going?”

“The production room.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Thirty minutes later, Ji-ho pushed open the sajang-nim’s door again, Taeyeon half a step behind him.

Against two gazes — appraising, edged with impatience — he wasted no words. He walked to the desk, worked the tablet, and pulled up the two full demos uploaded to the company library last night: “Growl” and “I Got a Boy.”

Plus one more, finished eleven minutes ago: “Rooftop.”

“Sajang-nim. Director. Please hear these three songs first.”

Neither humble nor insolent. The two men traded a glance — irritated by the presumption, but the boy’s track record and their own ears outvoted the irritation. They put on the headphones.

The music started.

“Growl” seized the ear in four bars — that infectious hook, the taut rhythmic engine, the swagger of youth on the edge of rebellion. Tailor-cut for a rising EXO.

“I Got a Boy,” with its shape-shifting structure and headlong genre collisions and its banner-sized declaration of a woman’s own terms — not a conventionally pretty song, but as a transformation gambit, a genuinely intriguing one.

And then “Rooftop.” Lyrical rock threaded through warm, healing guitar work, a melody with hooks that would not let go.

It stopped Lee Soo-man cold.

Thirty-plus years of producer’s instinct rendered the verdict almost instantly: all three of these had enormous potential. He looked up sharply.

“This ‘Rooftop.’ You just uploaded it? Now?”

“Ne. Took about thirty minutes — it’s a rough demo.”

Thirty minutes. A song like that in thirty minutes?

Is he even human?

The two executives looked at each other, and the same thought passed between them: if you’d told us yesterday you’d uploaded “Growl” and “I Got a Boy” — and that you write like THIS on demand — the whole morning goes differently. A creative engine this fast and this consistent? A quiet relationship was suddenly a very absorbable cost. Take the scandal heat, ride it into a debut, soften the splits a little—

A pity the morning’s wildfire had left nobody any bandwidth to check the song library.

The office had gone very quiet. Ji-ho knew the window when he saw it, and laid his plan out clean:

“I propose a wager. ‘Growl’ — I give to SM. Free. For EXO’s comeback. Not one won of upfront fee.”

Kim Young-min’s eyebrow jumped. Lee Soo-man didn’t move.

“The terms: if ‘Growl’ fails to hit the company’s own internal definition of a smash during EXO’s comeback — single-digit chart positions, album sales, measurable lift to the group, broad cultural footprint, whatever specific bar you set — then for ‘I Got a Boy’ and ‘Rooftop,’ I take the industry-minimum one-time production fee. Flat buyout. I waive all long-term royalty participation.”

“But if—” his voice came down harder, “—the song clears the bar: then for all three songs, my production fee is benchmarked to my market rate as of each song’s release date — plus long-term royalty participation on streaming and digital sales.”

Field Notes

The producer’s ladder, for context: the industry pays a one-time production fee plus long-term royalties. A no-name rookie’s songs are worth so little that buyers tack on a small buyout fee and take the publishing outright. Land a few hits and the one-time fee jumps, plus ten to twenty percent royalty participation. Prove you can build whole albums — direct recording, define a sound — and you’re asking twenty to thirty. And thirty to fifty percent? That’s the summit, reserved for the handful of names that move markets: Black Eyed Pilseung, Shinsadong Tiger, PSY, Park Jin-young.

On paper, the wager favored the rookie — a nobody’s song would never normally touch EXO or Girls’ Generation, not with Yoo Young-jin and KENZIE in the building. But “Growl” was a milestone hiding in a demo, and “Rooftop” was a chart-legend waiting for its year. Lee Soo-man wanted them. Badly.

A considering pause. Lee Soo-man raised his head and looked at the young man in front of him.

“A wager like this comes with conditions. Name them.”

“Correct.” Ji-ho nodded and laid them down. “First: I voluntarily withdraw from SM’s debut plan, and we terminate my trainee contract.”

“Second: SM does not obstruct or interfere, in any form, with Taeyeon and me seeing each other privately — and does not retaliate against her resources or threaten her with shelving because of it.”

“In return, Taeyeon and I guarantee: for two years, the relationship stays absolutely private.”

And then Taeyeon stepped forward — one brave step — and bowed. Ninety degrees.

“Seonsaeng-nim. Sajang-nim.”

“I love Ji-ho, and I don’t want to lose him. And I love Girls’ Generation — I gave it my whole youth.”

“So I will keep this promise. I will absolutely keep it.”

“Please.”

The office fell silent again — but the silence had changed in kind: no longer suppression, now calculation.

Lee Soo-man’s fingertip resumed its slow tick against the desktop. Taeyeon’s contract has barely more than a year on it. Push her to scorched earth and she walks at renewal — a catastrophic loss. The shelving threat had always been pressure, not plan — exactly as the boy had read it. And the boy himself had just put two warhead-class songs on the table as chips.

Kim Young-min was running his own numbers. “Growl” is a lead single. “Rooftop” is a classic. Handed to EXO, the return dwarfs the whole value of one solo artist’s contract — no solo act outearns a top boy group. The comeback is next year; even if the kid has a name by then, that’s ten, twenty percent participation. If the song hits, that fee buys a lock on a future top-tier producer — cheap at the price. And if it doesn’t hit—

It will hit. He knew music well enough to know that much. With SM’s production and EXO’s engine under it, the only open question was the size of the crater. Set the “smash” bar high, then — half a million albums, ten music-show wins—

As for the boy himself: an unmanageable, over-opinionated rebel like this OUTSIDE the SM system might honestly be better for company discipline than inside it.

And the romance? As long as it stays invisible, it’s nothing. This industry is full of love-you-to-death couples who dissolve within two years of real schedules. And if they DON’T dissolve — look at the two of them — maybe the boy ends up writing Taeyeon’s solo albums. Also not a loss.

At length, Lee Soo-man spoke, eyes drilling into Ji-ho.

“You’re that certain? That these songs will hit?”

Ji-ho met the gaze without a flicker.

“I’m certain they’re good songs. Whether they hit — the market decides. That’s what makes it a wager. And I trust SM’s judgment and SM’s operations too much to believe you’d bury genuinely great work.”

Kim Young-min looked at Lee Soo-man. One more exchanged glance — and this one carried something new: the loosening that follows a favorable ledger, and a complicated flicker of respect for the nerve and the strategy of the young man standing in front of them.

“The details go to Legal for proper drafting.” Lee Soo-man’s tone made it a yes. “But before that — both of you, hear me. From this moment, through signing, through the next two years: this so-called relationship of yours must be completely invisible. One whisper — one — and everything is void, and the consequences follow.”

Taeyeon’s hand clamped Ji-ho’s, palm damp — but the boulder that had been crushing her chest since morning finally, finally shifted off a first inch of daylight.

She looked at his calm profile.

He’s blinding.

Ji-ho bowed slightly.

“We understand.”

And about “Growl” — he had absolute confidence. A year remained before EXO’s comeback: a year to drive his market rate into the twenty-to-thirty bracket. SM thought it was getting a freebie; he intended to make them bleed top-producer rates by settlement day. The harder “Growl” hit, the more he’d earn — and the louder the song itself would advertise exactly one name: the one on the credits.
