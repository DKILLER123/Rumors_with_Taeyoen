It’s Late Enough to Stay the Night

Goo Ha-ra slept like she did everything else: flat out, and without the smallest amount of guilt about it.

Song Ji-ho lay on his side and looked at her for a while. The morning was coming in the far side of the curtain and doing nothing to flatter anybody, which is the sort of light a bedroom is supposed to be able to survive. Her breathing had gone long and even about four minutes ago. There was the beginning of a smile still sitting on her mouth, the residual expression of some dream she had not yet come back to be annoyed about.

Her hair had made a mess of itself across the pillow in every direction at once, and a piece of it had been pulled forward by her own movement and was lying over her forehead.

He reached over and put it back behind her ear. His fingers were still warm from the bed and her skin was cooler than they were, and at the touch of them she moved without waking: turned her face a little into his palm, settled, and kept on breathing.

A cat — a large, expensive, difficult cat that had let him in out of the rain and would deny it later with real feeling.

He let his hand stay there for two seconds more than the moment needed, and then he got up.

Her phone went twice on the bedside table while he was pulling a shirt over his head. The screen stayed lit for a moment with the name of her manager across the top of it and a message underneath about a fitting, and then went dark, and then lit again with a third one, and then went off, because he had turned the phone face down.

There was no argument in that, and none was available. The fitting was at eleven. She had been up since five-thirty in a car park in front of a beef restaurant, and she had gone to bed at something past four with the intention of sleeping until the afternoon, and the least a man can do is take the telephone away from it.

She had rolled over at some point while the water was running, and when he came out of the bathroom with his hair still going dark at the ends she was lying face down across the whole of the bed with one arm up over the pillow, the cover down at her waist, and her back entirely open to the room.

That was the thing about her that still got to him, and it was not the shape of her at all — everybody in the country had already seen that, in somebody else’s photograph. It was the two blades of her shoulder standing up under the skin at the top of her back, and the small dip between them, and the way the bone finished and the spine started. Butterfly bones, a woman had once called them in an interview, and had been laughed at for it by every other woman on the panel, and had been copied without credit by four hundred magazine headlines within the year.

He went back and pulled the cover up over both of them and stood there for one more look, and then he turned the light off at the door.

The suit he did not put on is the argument for the outfit. It is still on the rail in a room where four people ate breakfast at eight, and it was approved for him by three people who have never once been thanked for it. He has chosen instead what he can sit in for eleven hours, which is the decision of a man who has already arranged the day so that nobody will be taking a photograph of him standing up.

In the hall downstairs the house was doing its morning quietly: the cook at the island with a cloth over a bowl, one dishwasher running, a gardener’s radio somewhere out beyond the glass. Somebody had laid two places on the small table — the correct number for a house of one when he has guests — and had laid them in the wrong room.

“Eat in the kitchen,” he said. “There’s nothing for it here.”

He was given a bowl of rice, a fried egg, and something with sesame in it that he did not ask the name of, and he stood at the counter and finished all three of them in four minutes — which the cook took as a compliment, and which went into the diary the staff keep about him without any of them having decided to keep one.

At the door he stopped and came back for the key card off the hook, and stopped again, and said into the hall:

“Nobody in that room until I come back. If she asks, I have gone to do something boring.”

He had not gone to do something boring. Everybody in that house knew the difference and none of them would have said so out loud, because at the ages of the two women sleeping upstairs the difference between the two is a matter of luck.

The black Mercedes was at the gate with its nose out and its engine ticking over quietly, and Chae Soo-bin was in the driver’s seat with both hands at ten and eleven and her eyes on the mirror, and she had been sitting there long enough that the mirror had started to be the only part of the car she could honestly say she was watching.

She had the window down two centimeters. It was not cold enough for that. It was the window of a person who had been reading something on her phone and had shut it in a hurry.

He went round the back rather than to the front, and she was out and holding the rear door before he had crossed the nose of the car, and he got in, and dropped the bag on the seat beside him, and the car took him the way it takes anybody: a small, expensive settling.

“The office,” he said.

“Shall I take the—” She had the two routes in her head already — one of them eleven minutes longer, with four fewer lights — and she had chosen between them before she woke up at five-forty this morning. “The bridge, then.”

“The bridge is fine.”

She got back in, shut her own door with two fingers, and put the car into the road without any of it being hurried — because a woman who has been sitting still for eight minutes does not become slow on purpose unless she has thought about it.

In the mirror, for exactly one second, she looked at his face.

There was nothing on his face that she had not seen on a hundred other mornings, and it was not a face she had any business checking on at five-fifty in the morning, and she turned back to the road with both hands flat on the wheel and drove the car like a person driving a car.

Seoul at the end of September goes to work in a single continuous movement that nobody has ever organized. The office blocks along the river let their people out onto the pavement in a steady stream, and the stream had coffee in it, and the coffee was being drunk by people walking at a pace set by a timetable and not by a human body. A line of students in the same grey jackets went along the wall of a school in threes and fours, loudly, with their bags on one shoulder, and the older boy at the front of the group was holding a paper bag of something fried and sharing it backwards without looking.

There was a woman putting a sticker over a shop shutter. There was a man asleep upright on the metro bus in the second lane over, with his forehead against the glass and the glass doing nothing for him.

Song Ji-ho lay back against the seat and shut his eyes.

Second album. Seven songs, all of them in English, and the whole of it to be finished in fifteen days, and two of those days are already gone.

The first video he had made for himself was in a rack on the fourteenth floor of this building, and it was in nineteen hours and forty minutes of pieces, and the version that would exist in public had to come out of that by tomorrow or by the day after, depending on how long it took him to be unsatisfied with it.

He had directed other people’s songs. That was not the same sentence and he knew the difference well enough not to say it out loud: a seventeen-minute film for a ballad in a laundry in Mangwon-dong, in somebody else’s borrowed time, in a year when there was nobody in the country who had heard his name at all. Nobody had asked him to do that one. He had done it because the girl in it had been told she would be paid and had not been, and because there was no one else in the room who knew where to put a light.

“Hoejang-nim.” She had the schedule out, and read the top of it rather than reciting it from memory — one of the reasons he had kept her. “There’s nothing on the building’s calendar until eleven. He opened one eye at the mirror.

“I did say that.”

“I put the words in the right order for a person who would be hurt by the wrong one,” said Chae Soo-bin, and did not look back at the mirror, and drove.

The bridge came up, and the city went out to the side of it, and then the river was under them, grey-green and heavy and moving far more slowly than anybody crossing it ever believes.

He did not look at it. He had looked at it from this car on nine hundred mornings and would look at it on nine hundred more, and on the ninth hundred and first, tonight, it would be black.

The LOEN building took the left of its own block and had a service yard on the right, and in the yard a man with a clipboard was already looking at the wrong end of a car being unloaded, and the black Mercedes went past him without stopping, which is a thing that makes a man with a clipboard tell four other people about it at lunch.

In the lobby there were eleven people, and by the time the doors of the lift had shut there were four of them standing and two of them with their phones down.

“Good morning, Hoejang-nim,” said the one who had spoken first, for all of them, in the tone of a person who has rehearsed being casual about it.

“It’s early,” said Song Ji-ho, which was the only answer any of them got, and which the four of them discussed in the canteen at nine — as evidence of something.

The lift went to the top of the building and stopped one floor short of it, because the fourteenth floor has its own door and its own card, and the card is the sort that has to be held in place rather than waved, and the corridor on that side is carpeted in a grey so dark it reads as black except where the ceiling lights fall on it.

There were six rooms along that corridor. Three of them belong to post-production, one to music, one to a lawyer who is never in it, and the one at the end, at the corner, with two doors, was a store room until this year, and is now the reason the fourteenth floor has the only window it has.

He had had it built to his own specification, which had taken eleven weeks and had been argued about in three separate meetings by people whose job descriptions did not include the word “monitor.”

The main panel is a reference display that cost more than the car in the yard, and the second one is for the timeline. The desk is deep enough that a keyboard, a notebook and a coffee can exist on it at the same time without any of them being moved.

The chair — the only thing in that room everybody agrees about — had arrived on a trolley in a crate at four hundred thousand won, and two grown men had both asked to be the ones who assembled it.

The door closed behind him. The latch is a heavy one; it makes a sound like a car door and it is the reason the room feels like a room.

And then there was nothing in it but a man, a wall of frames, and the first of nineteen hours.

He put the bag down, did not sit down, and stood in front of the second monitor with his hands in his pockets and looked at the empty timeline, which is a thing a person does once per project and never explains.

Then he sat, and the machine came up under his hands, and the folders were where they had been left.

The post team had done the first pass of the archive while he was in America, and had done it properly, which is a sentence he would not have expected to write about that particular department. There was a bin for every location and inside it a bin for every day, and inside that, cameras: A through F, and a seventh for the drone, and an eighth for the two phones that had been used for one shot at the harvest dinner because he had wanted the crowd’s own angle and there was no way to get a camera into the middle of it.

Eighteen hundred and six files. Nineteen hours forty-one minutes. Nine terabytes on the shelf behind him and a second copy on a shelf in another building, because he had read what happened to a man who kept one copy.

Everything in the folders was labeled the way it had been shot: scene, set-up, take, and then a small letter for the ones the assistant had marked. He went through the first two bins at speed, opening, holding, closing, with the audio down and then off entirely.

There was a piece of paper in his left hand, and he wrote the numbers down on it in pencil, because the alternative is a keyboard shortcut, and a keyboard shortcut is how a man ends up keeping a take he should not keep.

By the sixth bin the paper was full and he had the shape of the thing in front of him, and by the eighth he had stopped being quick about it.

Because the eighth was the crush pad.

The panel hands over the vocabulary and the reflexes. It does not sit in the chair. Twenty-one hours of a woman in a vat of grapes has to be watched one time by the person who is going to be blamed for it, and that is a task the System has no line item for.

The knowledge was there and had been there for months, sitting in him like a language he had spoken as a child and had no excuse for not remembering: what a cut wants to be landed on, where a dissolve is a lie and where it is the only honest thing available, how a shot of a face can be made to wait half a second and what that half second will cost the audience in their chest.

What he did not have was the second half of it, the part that only comes from doing the thing badly in public. The room he had built had arrived before the experience that would have filled it, and there was no way to buy that at any price, and he had just learned it — on the first morning of the first video he had ever made for himself, in a room that took eleven weeks to build.

He pulled the first file out of the eighth bin and put it on the timeline, and started at the beginning, and this time did not watch it at double speed — which is how a rough cut is arrived at by a man who intends to be blamed for it.

There are two kinds of rubbish in a set of rushes and only one of them is a surprise.

The first kind is technical, and technical rubbish is easy to be brave about. A shot where the focus puller missed the rack by the width of a finger and the face went soft in the middle of a line nobody was going to hear. A hand-held shot where somebody breathed at the wrong moment and the horizon swung like a ship. A take with a wind buffet on the mic so bad that the girl in it might as well have been singing into a fan. Out. Out. Out. Nine minutes of a man walking toward a camera in beautiful light, and the light was beautiful and the walk was nothing, and the whole nine minutes went into a bin he renamed REJECTS, which is the only bin in the project with a name that makes anybody feel something.

Twenty-six per cent of nineteen hours, gone, in three hours, without a single one of the files being watched twice.

“That’s a monster,” said the assistant editor at eleven-fifteen, coming in with a printout of the ingest log, because he had left the outer door open for exactly eight minutes. “How much of it is going to survive?”

“Twelve minutes.”

“Twelve minutes of what?”

“Of what I keep.”

The boy looked at the wall of the room, and at the chair, and at the second monitor with its black timeline across the middle of it, and did the arithmetic of a working life on a man who had been in the building for two hours and had not yet asked him to fetch anything.

“Can I—” he began.

“You can go and eat,” said Song Ji-ho, “and if anybody asks where the rough cut is, the answer is that you do not know. That is not a metaphor and it is not a joke.”

The door closed. The latch did what latches do in that corridor.

He put his hands flat on the desk and looked at a frozen face for a long time.

There is a kind of person a camera is glad to find. It is not beauty — and it is not talent, either, both of which a competent crew can photograph to death in a morning. It is the total absence of an argument between the woman and the lens: nothing being pushed, nothing being sold, and the whole of her attention going into the thing she is doing instead of into what the thing looks like. It is the reason a director can be ruined by an unrepeatable take and cannot make another one of it, and the reason he is going to have to spend the next three months of his life with the frame at 00:46:19:22 in front of him.

He had come over to say something about tomorrow’s call time and had found her at the end of the table with her boots off and a glass she was not drinking out of, looking at the fire with the flat, unguarded face of a person who has been on display since four in the afternoon.

She turned her head when he was still nine paces away. There was no reason to: the ground was gravel, and he was not trying to be quiet, and nothing in the script was due to happen for another eleven hours.

The firelight went across her face as she turned, and for the length of one breath her eyes were the brightest two things in the whole valley, and then she smiled and asked him if he was going to tell her to put her shoes back on.

Camera A had been struck. Camera C was on a jib with a boy asleep at the base of it. The only thing on the lawn that was looking at her was a young man from the second unit with a body monitor slung over his shoulder, who took the shot because the shot was happening, and handed the card over at the wrap without a word, and did not ask for anything for it, and is still working on the fourteenth floor.

It is forty-one frames. The exposure on it is wrong by two thirds of a stop and it drifts out of true at the end. It is in the video, at 01:58:07, and nobody has ever once complained about it.

Empties, then. The B-roll that had been shot for transitions and had been treated all week as a box of spare parts.

The mist in the rows at six in the morning, with the sprinklers on and nobody in the frame but a dog. The sun going down behind a ridge and the whole of the valley turning the color of something in a photograph you have had too long. The balloon’s burner lighting the inside of the envelope from within for two seconds every eight, over and over, on the way up, a thing nobody had planned and which was now the closest thing in the material to a heartbeat.

He laid the sunset across the front of the good part, so that the happiness had an end written into it while it was still happening, and you could not say what had changed until you thought about it. And he took out every hard cut in the second act and replaced it with a cross-dissolve, and then took half of those dissolves out again and let the shots breathe into each other at a length that had no business working and did.

A hard cut is a decision. A dissolve is a memory. The song is a man remembering, and the whole of the middle of it had been edited like an announcement.

Everything good in a memory arrives already graded: a shade too warm, soft at the edges, and not one frame of it accurate.

That was the last thing he changed before he looked up and found out what time it was.

At eight in the evening he looked at the clock in the corner of the second monitor and could not, for a moment, make it agree with the light in the corridor outside the door.

Ten hours. Lunch had been a bowl of something brought up by a production assistant at half past one and eaten at the bench in four minutes with the left hand while the right hand was on the wheel of the timeline, and the bowl had gone back down cold on the corner of the desk, and the boy had said nothing about it, and the rest of the day had gone into the frame without being interrupted once.

His neck was the first thing to send in a report: a hard, hot line from the base of the skull down into the shoulder on the right, where he had been holding his head eleven degrees off square for eleven hours. His eyes had gone dry and silly, so that the edges of the monitor doubled and the letters on the toolbar drifted a little when he looked at them directly, and blinking was an activity rather than a reflex.

He rubbed the bridge of his nose, rolled his neck once, heard it, and did not stop to enjoy the sound of it, because the last thing was the one thing that mattered and it could not be done by a man leaning back in a chair.

Music.

The board had been cut to picture. Now the picture had to be cut to the record, and the difference between those two sentences is the entire job — and the only reason a person who owns a building full of people does it himself at nine at night rather than leaving it to somebody on a rate.

He put the reference cans on, and opened the stereo session, and played “Stay With Me” from the top for what he had long stopped counting.

There is nothing about a song you wrote that you still get to hear for the first time. Every entry of it was already in his hands before the first note arrived: where the drum steps in under the second verse, where the bass goes out on its own for a bar and comes back late on purpose, and where the vocal climbs up to the top of the thing and has to be allowed to stand there alone with nothing under it.

C major, and no tricks in the key; two hundred and ten seconds; a line that starts on E4 and finishes on E6, and the whole arrangement built so that the last chorus is the first moment in the song where anybody is allowed to be loud.

He tapped markers in as it went, one per beat at the joins, and then played it again with the picture against them and moved the whole of act one forward by three frames, and then back by one.

Written along the bottom of the board, in the same pencil as everything else in this room: A MUSIC VIDEO IS NOT A SONG WITH PICTURES ON IT. IT IS THE SAME ARGUMENT, MADE TWICE, AT THE SAME SPEED.

The rule he had been taught by nobody and had worked out in a laundry in 2012 is that a lyric is a hand on the audience’s collar, and a cut at the end of a lyric is that hand letting go. Put the picture change anywhere else in the bar and the line goes soft. Put it on the accent and a man in a room in Seongsu-dong who has never met either of them will feel something he cannot name and will think it is the weather.

So the frame at 00:46:19:22 came up on the word that needed it, and the shot of the valley from the basket held for the length of a breath more than the rhythm wanted, because the rhythm is not the only thing in the room that is right.

The first one lands on the fire: her turning, the light going across her face, forty-one frames of a thing that was not written. The second one lands on the morning after, on a push-in that starts at the empty pillow and finishes at a window with the sun through it, and it has to arrive there exactly as the word “need” is let go, or the whole of the third act is a man looking for somebody in a postcard.

The shot on the pillow had been three seconds when the picture was cut to itself. He made it three and twelve.

It is the whole difference between a man noticing that she is not there and a man having noticed it for a while and being too tired to do anything about it.

He moved the length of the shot by half a second, then by three further frames, and then took one back, and sat there while the machine did what it was told, and let the sun come up over the sill at the exact pace the melody goes down.

Too slow and it sits there like a wall. Too quick and it is an effect.

Then the picture had to be finished, which meant the frame itself: the crops, the repositioning, and the grade.

He went through it shot by shot and moved the subject off the middle and onto the line he had been arguing with his own director of photography about for six days in California: her face, her eyes, the turn of her mouth, always at the place a person’s eye wants to arrive at, and never at the place a camera defaults to. Two shots got a crop; one of them was a beautiful wide of the crush pad that had been framed for a wall and not for a timeline, and cutting a tenth off both sides made the girl in the middle of it unmistakable without making the valley any smaller.

One shot could not be fixed and he left it alone, which took more discipline than fixing the other nineteen, because it was his own favourite in the whole set of rushes and it was in the wrong aspect for the story it was in.

The grade was a different job and he did it after he ate, because a man who grades on an empty stomach makes a picture look like the inside of a bad restaurant at closing time.

He built it warm and low in the first pass, the way he had said he would on the plane: saturation taken down a notch all through, the highlights rolled off rather than clipped so the sky kept its shape and the white cloth at the dinner stayed white for as long as it was on screen, and the shadows let go of their detail in a way that is very difficult to do and extremely obvious when somebody else has done it.

Then he split it by act, because a single look across two hundred and ten seconds is a music video made by a committee.

The meeting goes up in the middle and forward in the warmth, sunlight with the smell of it still in, and the skin of everybody in it a little too good to be true, which it is allowed to be, because the shot is somebody else’s memory and not the news.

The balloon gets diffusion: a softness at the inside edge of every bright thing, as though the light has to be persuaded out of the frame. It is a trick.
