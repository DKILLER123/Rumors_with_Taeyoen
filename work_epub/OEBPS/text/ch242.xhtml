Chapter Two Hundred Forty-Two

Why Emperors Favor the Cunning Minister?

August 9, 2013 · afternoon LOEN Entertainment · the chairman’s office five files land on a CEO’s desk, a manager gets a department — and a name is spoken aloud for the first time

With the understanding reached, Ji-ho took his leave of SM.

Even as founder and largest shareholder, Lee Soo-man would need time to walk the deal past SM’s shrewd, well-fed executives. But that was his problem, not Ji-ho’s. By next week, the five people he wanted would be reporting to LOEN.

Back at the company, he sent for CEO Cho Young-chul.

The CEO was mid-meeting when the summons arrived — and wrapped the agenda with a speed that surprised even himself. His state of mind these past weeks had been a complicated weather system. On one front: quiet relief. The new owner had no appetite for micromanaging daily operations; the job was the same job, plus one reporting line. On the other front: dread with no expiry date. A man does not buy a company to change nothing. The silence since the takeover had hung over him like a suspended sword — and now, at last, the sword was moving. The good news: no more guessing. The bad news, as it turned out: the guess he’d never have made.

“A GIRL group?” He heard the words and doubted his own ears. “Hoejang-nim — you’re certain you said girl group?”

“Correct. Next year, LOEN debuts its own girl group.”

The CEO wrestled his face into order and lost on points.

“Hoejang-nim, our management division’s condition — I reported it to you myself. Fewer than ten trainees in total, none showing exceptional promise. The training infrastructure is effectively blank — vocal and dance instructors are hired ad hoc from outside. To debut a group from THIS foundation—”

“Which is exactly why I’m giving it to you to arrange.” A folder came across the desk. “Profiles of five trainees. They report to LOEN next week.”

Cho Young-chul opened the file. One page in, his pupils tightened.

INCOMING TRANSFER · FIVE TRAINEES — CONFIDENTIAL

Personnel summary · effective next week

Bae Joo-hyun b. 1991 · SM trainee 4 yrs · leader, visual, center, main rapper, lead dancer, sub-vocal

Kang Seul-gi b. 1994 · SM trainee 6 yrs · main dancer, lead vocal

Son Seung-wan b. 1994 · SM trainee 1 yr · main vocal

Park Soo-young b. 1996 · SM trainee 3 yrs · sub-vocal, sub-rapper

Kim Ye-rim b. 1999 · SM trainee 1 yr · sub-vocal, sub-rapper, lead dancer, maknae

His brow knotted. Two of them with barely a single year of training?

“Hoejang-nim — these trainees. What’s their actual level?”

“This is the reserve core of SM’s NEXT girl group. Draw your own conclusions.”

The knot in the CEO’s forehead unwound itself in stages. Twenty-plus years in this industry — he knew precisely what those words weighed. SM’s pre-debut core class was the scarcest resource in the entire trainee ecosystem. The only remaining question was the impossible one:

“Hoejang-nim — how in the world did you get SM to LET GO of them?”

“Lee Soo-man and I made a trade. The details aren’t your concern. What concerns you: from today, these five are LOEN trainees. Except for Yeri, all of them are effectively debut-ready — what they need is systematic integration and polish. Which brings me to your tasks.”

The CEO’s spine straightened.

“Speak.”

“First: within one week, an apartment for their dormitory. Strict security, real privacy, close to the company, a private bedroom for each of them. Don’t agonize over budget — but don’t be absurd either.”

“Understood.”

“Second: establish a Trainee Management Department.”

He rose and walked to the window as he spoke.

“LOEN’s trainee system is a blank page, and that’s unacceptable. One week: a complete departmental plan on my desk — staffing, budget, training framework. One month: the department is functioning.”

The CEO wrote fast. A week for the plan, a month to operational — brutal tempo. But the tone wasn’t consultative; it was dispositive. Which meant it would be done, or else.

“Third.” He turned back from the window. “For the training curriculum itself, we learn from SM. I’ve agreed an exchange program with Lee Soo-man — we can send people annually to study their system. That needs a dedicated owner.”

His gaze traveled to the doorway, where a familiar figure had been standing quietly.

“Dong-hwan. Come in.”

Ryu Dong-hwan entered with a small bow.

“Hoejang-nim. Sajang-nim.”

“Dong-hwan has been with me over a year. Careful hands. Knows what to do and — more importantly — what not to do. His competence I trust completely. The Trainee Management Department: its formation and its operation are his. Sajang-nim, you’ll give him full support.”

Cho Young-chul blinked once, recalibrated instantly, and bowed to the new org chart.

“Of course. My full cooperation, Director Ryu.”

“Hoejang-nim, this—” Dong-hwan had visibly not seen it coming. “I worry my abilities aren’t—”

“Then fail, and go back to being my manager.” The tone was mild as tap water. “But succeed, and this is the boldest line on your résumé: the man who BUILT LOEN’s trainee system from bare ground. That credential is worth more than any number of years standing next to me.”

Dong-hwan stood silent for a few seconds, running the scales — then bowed his head, solemn as a vow.

“Understood. Hoejang-nim — I’ll give it everything I have.”

Which was, of course, the design. Ryu Dong-hwan was one of the very few people who knew where several bodies were buried — the trainee department was simultaneously a test, a reward, and a deepening of the bond. Loyalty compounds when it’s paid interest.

He turned back to the CEO.

“And accelerate the film & television production division. LOEN cannot live on platform rents forever — content is the future. Writers, directors, producers, crews: build the roster step by step. Submit whatever budget it takes; I’ll approve it personally.”

The CEO quietly marveled. Girl group, trainee academy, screen production — the young chairman wasn’t adjusting LOEN; he was converting it, wholesale, from a music platform into a full-spectrum entertainment house. He asked nothing further.

“Understood, Hoejang-nim. Plans as fast as I can produce them.”

“Then that’s all. One week: dormitory and trainee-department plan. Two weeks: the production-division plan. Problems?”

“None.”

“Go.”

The CEO rose, turned — and stopped at the door as if catching a dropped thought.

“Hoejang-nim. The five of them… have you decided what to call the group?”

Ji-ho tipped his head back slightly, let the moment stretch, and felt the smile arrive on its own.

“Red Velvet.”

The CEO paused on the name — nodded once — and closed the door behind him.

✦ ✦ ✦

Ryu Dong-hwan remained, wearing the expression of a man circling a question.

“Whatever it is — say it.”

“Hoejang-nim. Once I move to the trainee department… your manager position sits empty. What would you like—”

A fair point. He considered it briefly.

“You’ve got the experience — run the hiring yourself. A manager, and an assistant for me as well. Skills are secondary. What matters: meticulous, tight-lipped, and loyal above everything. Hand your current work over properly, then give the department your full attention.”

“Understood, Hoejang-nim. Consider it handled.”

“Mm. Go on.”

The office settled into quiet. He leaned back and watched the sky over the city dim by slow degrees.

Red Velvet. Finally — his.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the corridor, Ryu Dong-hwan walked with his heart hammering pleasantly against his ribs. From road manager to department head in one conversation. PROOF, if proof were needed: hitching yourself to the right man was ninety percent of any career. He had clocked Ji-ho as no ordinary fish the very first month — which was exactly why, regarding the chairman’s… extracurricular complexity… his policy had always been ironclad: don’t look if you can avoid it, don’t hear if you can help it, and if you accidentally saw or heard anyway — immediate, total amnesia. And now the loyalty had paid its dividend. BUJANG. DIRECTOR RYU. Just rolling the syllables through his head improved his posture.

The debt of recognition was heavier than mountains. He would make this department sing if it killed him — partly gratitude, partly the plain fact that chances like this knocked exactly once, and a man who fumbled the knock did not get a second visit from fortune.

As for recruiting the new manager and assistant — that deserved genuine care. Meticulous, discreet, loyal: mandatory, as ordered. And… one additional specification, unwritten. He had served the chairman for over a year and could state with scholarly confidence that the man had precisely one vice, and it was not gambling. These two hires would orbit the chairman all day, every day. Plain-looking candidates would be — how to put it — a failure of workplace ergonomics. If his picks pleased the chairman… heh. The trust would only deepen.

Why DO emperors dote on the cunning minister? Simple. Because the cunning minister is the only one who actually studies what the emperor likes — and takes care of it before being asked.
