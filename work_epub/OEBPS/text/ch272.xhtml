Chapter Two Hundred Seventy-Two

The New Secretary Takes Office

September 2013 · dawn Ji-ho’s hillside villa · Seoul one week abroad, one first day at a desk, and one set of keys handed over in a garden

Heavy beige curtains had been drawn so far past their overlap that the room kept its dark like a secret. Only at the seam did morning force through — one thin blade, bright enough to find the furniture, not bright enough to name it.

Ji-ho woke first.

A weight lay against him: warm, soft, entirely unconscious. Irene had slept the whole night folded into his side, one hand still fisted loosely in the front of his shirt, and waking into that warmth felt like being forgiven for something.

Her hair had come undone from whatever she had tied it into and covered both their shoulders — black silk spilled over pale skin, a few strands stuck to her cheek, rising and falling with breath she hadn’t bothered to keep quiet about.

He looked at her for a while.

Long lashes, dense enough to cast shadows. An eyelid that twitched once, twice, as if a small argument were being lost somewhere behind it. Her breathing came so lightly that he had to stop his own to hear it, and each exhale landed on his chest like a cat testing whether a hand was friendly.

He turned his head to breathe and found the whole line of her shoulder bare.

Unfair. She sleeps like that and it’s somehow my fault.

He shifted, half an inch, and the shift traveled. Sleep-warm skin against his, friction with far too much voltage in it, and every wire in him woke up ahead of his manners.

So he rolled, pulled the duvet up over both of them until only the top of her head was left outside, and lay there like a man who had made a decision.

She woke.

Her eyes opened under the covers and met his, smiling already, which was not a fair advantage to give him.

He lowered his forehead to hers. Noses bumping, once, lazily.

“Cabbage.” His voice came out low and ruined and entirely pleased with itself. “I leave for America soon, and you won’t even miss me a little?”

“It’s one week—” She turned her face away. The tips of her ears went first, then everything below them. “You— you just want an excuse to bully me!”

“Then I’ll bully you.”

“Who said you co—”

The rest went into the duvet.

✦ ✦ ✦

When Irene surfaced again, the crack of light at the curtain seam had gone from grey to white and was cutting a stripe across the pillow.

The bed held only her.

The place beside hers had gone cool. There was a shallow hollow left in the pillow and a smell on the sheets that belonged to someone else, and that was the entire evidence that the night had happened at all.

She lay there, staring at the ceiling, doing nothing.

Then she lifted an arm and laid the back of her hand across her eyes.

That idiot.

He had come home late, announced a week in the United States, and poured out so much honey — I’ll miss you, I can’t leave you, write me all day — that she, like some kind of rookie, had believed all of it, and had then been talked into everything else.

Now he was gone. And she had been left folded wrong, with limbs that no longer answered to her.

She lay another five minutes before she could sit up.

The duvet slid off. She looked down at herself, and then the heat came up her neck so fast it was almost embarrassing how little warning it gave.

She wrapped the blanket around her shoulders, found the floor with her bare feet, and went down the corridor at a run.

The bathroom was wide and bright, with a mirror long enough to hold the whole wall. She stood in front of it and looked at a woman with tangled hair, flushed cheeks, and lips kissed into a color no lipstick had any right to be, and did not entirely recognize her.

Don’t look at it. Look at the water temperature instead.

She turned the shower on and let it run until the sound filled the room, then stepped in and lifted her face into the water.

Warmth went down her spine in one long sentence. Sleep, and several memories she would be deleting later, ran off with it.

She worked shower oil between her palms until it turned to milk, and jasmine rose in the steam — her favorite, chosen months ago for reasons that had nothing to do with anyone.

Her hands were moving on their own. Then, when they passed over her stomach, they stopped.

She looked down.

Foam. Flat, taut skin, exactly as it had always been.

Which is when, uninvited and at full volume, her brain asked: what if you’re carrying a baby?

Her heart skipped, then stumbled, then started keeping time badly.

No. No, no— that’s not—

She stood under the water and counted days in her head, once, twice, to be sure she had not made the arithmetic come out convenient.

Safe days.

That idiot is not that lucky.

She breathed out for what felt like the first time in a minute, and finished washing, and came out of the shower with the panic folded up small and shoved somewhere out of reach.

Drying her hair in front of the mirror, she found that the glass had fogged into a white blur with one faint silhouette in it. She dragged a finger through the mist and looked at half a face.

Bare skin, and glowing — annoyingly so. Mouth redder than usual. Even the corner of her eye had gone soft, as though someone had edited her.

She held her own gaze for three seconds and looked away first.

Back in the bedroom, towel around her shoulders, she opened the wardrobe and began her morning’s real work: finding an outfit.

Her eyes went straight to the jeans on the left hanger. Pale blue, high waist, her favorite cut of the last fourteen months — the pair that made it look like she had been built from the knees up.

She stepped into them and pulled.

At mid-thigh, they stopped.

She blinked.

…What?

In breath. Both hands on the waistband. Heave.

Nothing. Firmly, proudly, structurally nothing.

“No way.” Her voice came out small. “Did I actually— gain?”

She let go. The jeans fell to her ankles with finality. She looked down at her legs with the personal suspicion of a woman addressing a court.

Softer. A little rounder. A little. One little. Barely a little, really.

Aish!

And whose fault was that.

Don’t lose weight, he had said. You’re uncomfortable thin — you’re prettier with a little softness. You like this. Don’t listen to anyone who tells you otherwise.

She had listened. That was the shameful part. She had listened, and relaxed, and eaten rice at eleven at night like a civilian.

Now her best jeans were a memory and her own body had apparently gone over to his side.

She flung the jeans at the hamper, dug through the entire shelf, and came out with a pair of long, wide-leg trousers she had once abandoned for swimming around her.

They fit.

She turned left. She turned right. She turned left again, in case the answer had changed.

“I’m not listening to you,” she told the mirror, with as much authority as she could arrange. “Uncomfortable to hold— then don’t hold me.”

She unscrewed the first bottle of skincare, patted toner onto her cheeks, and caught herself doing it again, softer, slower, with a face she could feel coming apart into a smile.

Damn it.

LOEN Entertainment · lobby, then the executive floor a chairman in a good mood is a hazard to everyone below him

Morning sun came through the glass curtain wall and lay down a long bright rectangle across the marble, and Ji-ho walked straight through the middle of it like a man who had slept nine hours and lost an argument with no one.

“Good morning.”

He said it to a junior marketer struggling with two towers of sample boxes, and said it smiling, and she stopped mid-stride and bowed so hard the boxes slid.

“G-good morning, Hoejang-nim.”

He passed on. She kept her bow for a second longer than necessary, hand on her chest.

“Ju-yeon-ssi.” He stopped beside another employee, glanced up, and let his eyes soften. “You look well today. Did you sleep?”

“I— g-gamsahamnida, Hoejang-nim!”

She survived the sentence by roughly one syllable.

Only when the elevator doors had closed did she turn to her colleague and drop her voice to the level of conspiracy.

“What is wrong with him today—”

“What’s wrong with him?”

“He’s unfairly—” She gave up on the word and just waved at the general direction of the chairman. “—that.”

Her colleague nodded with the gravity of a witness.

At the elevator bank, on the executive floor, Shin Min-hyuk was walking toward him from the far end of the corridor with a tablet under one arm and the expression of a man who had been hoping not to be seen.

“Head Shin.” Ji-ho looked him up and down, unhurried, then let the smile show. “Good. You look handsome today.”

Shin Min-hyuk rubbed the back of his neck and laughed out loud despite himself.

“Do I? Aigoo — not a tenth of you, Hoejang-nim.”

“Flattery from the record division. I’m writing it down.” He clapped the man’s shoulder once and turned the motion into a steering, walking him toward the doors. “The overseas shoot.”

“Done. Confirmed twice.” The tablet came up, and so did the professional voice. “Gear left in advance. Locations re-scouted and signed off. Crew rolls call at six a.m. tomorrow local.”

“Good.”

Doors opened. Cho Young-chul was inside, holding the panel for him, which was either deference or age.

“Sajang-nim,” Ji-ho said, stepping in. “Morning.”

Cho Young-chul inclined his head as if a small award had been handed to him.

“Hoejang-nim. Morning. You’re in a mood today — did something happen?”

“I may simply have slept well.”

The doors began to close. Ji-ho let them get an inch from the frame, then reached out and stopped them.

“One more thing. The CCM and Starship files are yours to push. Don’t let anyone else carry them.”

“They’re already moving. Preliminary talks are closed, and the other side is answering faster than we budgeted.”

“Faster is good. Faster is expensive in the wrong direction if we’re slow, so be faster.”

“…Understood.”

He walked out, and the greetings continued a half step behind him until the doors slid shut and swallowed them.

The corner office held sunlight from floor to ceiling and one enormous desk facing the skyline. Ji-ho sat down with the city at his back and a stack of authorizations in front of him: forty-four pages, forty-one of them in need of a signature.

Two soft knocks.

“Come in.”

The door opened without noise, and Chae Soo-bin came in carrying a tea tray, set the cup down, and retreated one step to the side as if she had been trained her whole life to occupy as little of a room as the room would allow.

No clink. No shadow of a sound against the wood.

That’s new.

She had changed the way she dressed. White shirt, dark pencil skirt cut to a modest knee, hair gathered low at the back of her head in a plain knot that left her neck and the line of her jaw entirely uncovered. Nothing about the outfit tried to be interesting, which was precisely why it worked.

Right. Her first day.

He didn’t look up. He was on page eleven, and page eleven had a clause about cross-collateralization worth reading, so he read it, signed it, and reached for twelve.

Soo-bin stood where she had been placed and did not fidget.

The office kept only two sounds: paper, and a pen moving.

She watched him sign.

His jaw was set. Not hard — set — and the muscle under it moved once when a sentence needed it. Long fingers, pen held low, three quick strokes of a name on each page with the ease of a man who had signed many things that other people would spend months litigating.

She had known four versions of him. Polite on a film set. Laughing with Writer Park over coffee at eleven in the morning. Lazy in a hallway, hood up, being talked at by someone. Careful, that night in the corridor, carrying a neighbor who weighed nothing.

She had never seen the fifth.

Not this: the man who held a company the size of a district in one chair, deciding, in an ordinary hour, things that moved billions and rearranged several thousand lives at once.

There was no effort in it, which was the terrifying part — and, she noticed far too late, the attractive part.

Some time passed, in which she was not, strictly speaking, working.

And then, without her permission, her feet moved.

She came around behind the desk, reached down, and laid both hands on his shoulders.

The man went rigid.

He turned with his face already closing, and the reprimand he had assembled died in his throat when he saw who it was.

“Soo-bin-ssi?” Genuine surprise, which she took as small mercy. “What are you doing here?”

She stepped back and bowed — properly, twenty degrees, feet together.

“Hoejang-nim. I’m your secretary now.”

His eyebrow moved.

“Secretary.” He laid the pen down and turned his chair to face her fully. “You’re giving up acting?”

Her eyelashes dropped, then came back up.

“I don’t think the audience likes me,” she said, and her voice went quiet in a way that had clearly been rehearsed for a while and was still embarrassing. “The film did that much business and nobody remembers my name. They do, actually — they know it, they can even match the face — and then it’s oh, it’s you, and that’s the whole conversation.”

She smiled. It was a careful, crooked thing.

“I’ve been on day contracts for months. I wanted something steady. The posting said LOEN was hiring a secretary for the chairman’s office, so I—” A small breath. “And if a role comes along later, maybe I’ll try again.”

Ji-ho looked at her a beat longer than the answer required.

Chae Soo-bin. Years of work, a couple of second-lead credits, decent numbers, and a name that never once trended. The industry was full of this particular cruelty: the show gets hot, the person stays cold. In another timeline she would grind through three more years of near-misses before one role finally stuck and the public discovered she had been there the whole time.

Now she was standing in his office with a knot at the back of her neck and a tea tray in her hands.

Well. That’s not my business. Yet.

Her face was clean and fair and made for close-ups nobody had ordered — not the kind of beauty that stops a room, the kind that makes a room easier to be in. And her eyes, when she used them, were steady and a little soft at the edges, as though she had decided in advance not to be a problem.

A view like that, at a desk beside me, all day. Not a bad trade.

“Then it’s decided,” he said, and let his voice go easy. “You’re my secretary.”

Something in her face came on like a lamp.

“Ne!” She nodded once, hard, then stepped forward with her hands already moving. “Let me— before I was handed the desk, the outgoing secretary told me your shoulders go stiff if you sit too long.”

He looked at her hands: pale, narrow through the wrist, nails cut short for work rather than for show.

“Sit down and behave,” he said, which was not a refusal.

He settled back into the leather and turned his head slightly to one side and closed his eyes.

Her hands came back to his shoulders, and both of them understood — immediately, and with horror — that she had never done this for a living.

Her thumbs were tentative. She pressed as though apologizing in advance.

But there is a particular alchemy to having someone whose face you enjoy touch a knot under your shoulder blade, and by the fourth pass he had stopped reading the file entirely.

“Mm.”

The sound left him before he could decide whether to allow it. His shoulder tightened, then, against his plans, let go.

Her hands froze.

“Hoejang-nim—” Tightrope quiet. “—did I press too hard?”

“No.” Eyes still shut, voice dragged down at both ends. “Keep going.”

“Ne.”

This time she stopped being careful.

Her fingertips walked up from his shoulder to the nape of his neck and worked the line of it, and once — she saw it instantly and could not unsee it — a strand of her hair brushed the skin behind his ear.

His breath caught.

She could feel her own breathing landing on the side of his neck. Not perfume, either: shampoo, maybe the jasmine shower oil, something clean and warm and completely uncalculated, which was much worse.

“Harder,” he said.

She stopped for half a second, then obeyed.

Through a shirt thin enough to be a rumor, heat came up through her fingers, and the muscle under it was solid in a way that had nothing to do with a desk. Her hands went hot. Her cheeks, unaccountably, followed.

“Mm—”

She went still again, and this time it was her pulse that had the audacity.

Looking down at his profile — that frankly obscene jaw, the mouth set flat with the corners turning up a fraction, a man enjoying something and refusing to admit it in the same breath — she watched his face for one second too many and then snapped her eyes away, ears on fire.

The wall clock ticked. Two people breathed. Somebody in the corridor laughed once and went away.

She worked the knot until it thinned out under her hands, until his breathing lengthened and evened into something that might have been sleep and might have been the state people reach right before it, and she stayed close enough to see the curve of his lashes.

Why would anyone be built like this?

She sighed, quietly, inside herself, and kept pressing, and her calves had begun to ache, and she noticed that she did not want to stop.

Sun came in at an angle across both of them and did not move.

Nonhyeon-dong · a two-story detached villa a white house in the green, and a girl who does not believe it

European in taste, stubbornly white in the sun, the house sat behind a wall with the trees grown up enough to make it feel found rather than built. Trimmed shrubs lined a pebble path, two ornamental trees were in bloom, and everything about the plot had been chosen for one quality: quiet.

Goo Hara had her arm through Ji-ho’s and had already walked the ground floor three times.

“The living room gets light all day,” she said, to no one, spinning once with her hands behind her back. “And the kitchen— is that marble?”

It was. White, veined, and wide enough to argue over.

The master bedroom came with a walk-in wardrobe and a bathroom whose tub was, on inspection, large enough for two people to be lazy in.

Upstairs, the terrace ran the depth of the house. Gangnam’s skyline stood at a polite distance: visible, inaudible.

“You like it?” Ji-ho asked.

Hara lifted her face to him, bright-eyed, and then, at the last moment, careful.

“I do like it. I like it a lot. But—” Her fingers found his sleeve and started worrying it. “I live in the dorm most of the year. Buying this for me is like buying a nice umbrella for someone who owns a car.”

He took her hand. His thumb moved once across her knuckles, and she went quiet because he had done it deliberately.

“Right now you’re working, so the dorm makes sense,” he said, in the voice people use on hesitant cats. “In two years, when you want to stop, where will you live?”

She had no answer.

“It’s quiet here. Not far from the city, not in it. Good for resting.” He let a second go past before the next part. “And you’ll bring your halmeoni. You can walk her in the garden after dinner, and it’s flat enough that she won’t ask you to stop.”

Hara’s eyes changed.

“Halmeoni,” she repeated, softly, testing whether the word sounded different in a house like this.

Every call from Gwangju ended the same way — eat properly, sleep properly, don’t be stubborn. If her grandmother lived here, the calls could become dinners.

“But it’s too much,” she said anyway. “I haven’t done anything. You’re handing me a house like—”

He put his hand on her head and moved it, once, backwards, through her hair.

“You being around is the thing you’ve done.”

“You—” The color arrived at her cheeks at speed. “All you do is talk.”

“Do you want it or not?”

She bit her lip. Then her chin came up, and she nodded — small at first, then with commitment.

“I want it. But I’m changing it. The terrace goes in as a sunroom, so we can sit out there in winter. Flowers in the garden — halmeoni likes flowers. And the kitchen counter.”

“What about it?”

“It’s too white. Beige.”

“Sunroom, agreed. Flowers — pick something that forgives us. Roses, maybe, or hydrangea along the wall. Counter, beige.”

“And a low table on the terrace. And a place for two chairs. And—”

She talked the rest of the way around the house, and he listened the way he listened to a script pitch, head slightly tilted, nodding at the beats that landed, and the two of them left the afternoon light in a state of total disorder: one tall, straight shadow and one small round one, walking across the floorboards side by side.

Later the same night Nonhyeon-dong · the villa, lights down takeout cleared away, curtains left open on purpose

The containers had gone into the bin, the boxes into the hallway, and the house had settled into the specific silence of a building that has never had anyone in it at this hour.

The bedroom light stayed low. The curtain was never closed properly: a wedge of deep blue night, and three or four stars doing their best with what they had been given.

Afterward — with the kind of tired that has no argument in it — Ji-ho lay with Hara against his side, breathing still not entirely behaving, his chin resting on the top of her head, fingers going slowly through hair that had gone damp.

Her fingertip went back to drawing idle circles over his heart, and stopped there.

She lifted her head. Under the low light her eyes were absurdly clear.

“Be careful in America.”

He kissed her forehead and put her back down.

“I will. Sleep.”

Hara folded herself into the hollow of his shoulder and closed her eyes, and the villa settled once against the dark, and a long while later — just as Ji-ho had begun to believe she was asleep — she spoke into his shirt.

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm?”

“Come back early.”

The corner of his mouth gave in before the rest of him did, and his arm came around her again, and pulled.

“Okay.”

Two days later · 09:40 Incheon International Airport · private terminal a party of eight, one album’s worth of nerve, and a very long runway

The check-in desk at the private terminal was a counter the size of a lectern, and it was being dealt with by Chae Soo-bin.

Beige trench coat, hair down over her shoulders, notebook open on her forearm — she was going through the list for the third time with the ground handler, her face set to the expression of a woman who has decided that nothing will be wrong on her watch.

Ji-ho stood to one side with his passport and boarding documents in one hand, in a dark soft-shouldered suit worn over a white T-shirt, which was how he managed to look underdressed and overdressed at the same time.

Behind him and slightly to the left, Park Ji-yeon stood with a baseball cap pulled down, a mask to her cheekbones, one bright hand-luggage case, and only her eyes available to the world. Those eyes kept sliding sideways to the back of his head and firing away before they were caught.

The rest of the party was already lined up with the handler: the Planning Division’s deputy director, the man in charge of overseas business, two assistants, one bodyguard who had not smiled since the briefing.

“Hoejang-nim.” Soo-bin stepped up, closed the notebook, and looked up with the small, dangerous brightness of someone reporting a completed task. “Everything is cleared. We can board.”

“Good. Let’s go.”
