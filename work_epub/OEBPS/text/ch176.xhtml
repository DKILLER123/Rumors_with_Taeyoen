Chapter One Hundred Seventy-Six

Sulli’s Ambition

The morning after Gapado — the rented island house first light, a line held, and a plan quietly hatched under a quilt

Morning on Gapado came up unreasonably clear.

He surfaced from deep sleep first — and in the second consciousness came back online, so did all of it: the dark, the cold, the screaming, the knife-water, the strength draining out of him one stroke at a time, the freezing body going slack in his arms. His heart clenched once, hard, before his eyes had even fully opened.

Then the rest of his senses reported in.

Warmth. Soft, real, and pressed all along his front.

Sulli was still curled against him, deeply asleep. A whole night of shared body heat had chased the lethal cold out of her entirely; what radiated off her now was the healthy, pliant warmth of a young body doing its job.

The Idol Aura had scrubbed his own exhaustion overnight and refilled the tank. The Boyfriend Halo — level one, modest as it was — had spent the same hours quietly nourishing her, coaxing her metabolism along, restoring a portion of her strength. That, in truth, had been the other reason he’d insisted on holding her through the night: hypothermia mishandled today became a fever tomorrow.

One thin quilt covered the two of them. Skin against skin, nothing between.

Her breathing ran long and even, warm against his collarbone, carrying her particular sweet-warm scent tangled with the faint clean ghost of shampoo. A few strands of her hair had strayed across his arm and neck, itching in the smallest possible increments. And her whole smooth, warm body lay flush against his chest — its softness, its resilience, and the frankly unignorable pressure of certain curves, all reporting to his nervous system in high definition.

His body went rigid.

This was not set intimacy. There was no one to call “cut.” This was a private room, first light, full consciousness, the morning after a night of shared survival — and no gap anywhere.

Last night, in the crisis, there had been no bandwidth for anything but saving her. This morning, safe, every sensory detail arrived amplified — and his body, honest to a fault, responded accordingly. All he could do was curl away at the waist and endure.

He tried to ease backward a few centimeters — and discovered that the arm wrapped around his waist, even in sleep, held on with an unconscious, proprietary insistence that was not going to be negotiated with. He didn’t dare force it. Wake her too abruptly and she’d open her eyes directly into the most unexplainable staging imaginable.

While he lay there in strategic paralysis, she stirred on her own — a tiny sound in her throat, lashes trembling — and her eyes slid slowly open.

He gained his few centimeters while he could.

The just-woken gaze was still fogged, blinking without comprehension, clearly unsure of what country she was in. Then she registered the firm, warm chest in front of her. And the precise, complete degree to which she was fitted into the arms attached to it. His body heat was printing itself through every point of contact.

Memory came back like the tide reversing: the courage of the confession, the pain of the answer, the fury of the slap, the chase, the cold, the choking dark — and the grip that had never once let go in that black water, and the warmth that had walked her back from the edge, degree by degree, in these same arms.

Color climbed her face at visible speed, flooding to her ears and down her neck. Her body tensed — but did not pull away. She just lifted her eyes, and found his, equally complicated, equally cornered.

Four eyes. No exits.

The air congealed. There was nothing left in the room but two sets of breathing and the temperature and heartbeat passing through skin.

She looked away first. Tried a small experimental shift to open some distance — and the smallest friction shorted out her strength entirely, muscles going to water, which had the net effect of pressing her closer.

“Nn—”

A tiny muffled sound of surrender. She gave up, buried her face back against his chest — ears now unmistakably red — and mumbled into his sternum.

“Oppa… morning.”

“…Morning, Sulli.” His own voice came out sandpapered; he leveled it by force. “How do you feel? Still cold? Anything hurting?”

She shook her head against him, hair brushing his jaw with predictable neurological consequences.

“Not cold anymore… just — no strength anywhere.” A pause; a small addendum. “And my head’s a little swimmy.”

Concern shoved the awkwardness aside. “Probably the aftermath — the cold, the water, the shock. Don’t move around. Rest more. I’ll have warm water and something plain sent over in a bit.”

“Mm.”

She said nothing more — just lay quiet against him, listening to the steady heart under her ear, borrowing its warmth. The fear of last night still lived somewhere in her bone seams; this solid, heat-generating body was the most effective known treatment.

Silence flowed through the morning light — but not an empty one. It carried the strange new tether that surviving something together installs in two people, plus the warmth of skin, plus everything neither of them was saying.

A long while later, very softly:

“Oppa. Last night — thank you.”

For jumping. For not letting go. For this warmth, right now.

His arm tightened a little; his chin came to rest gently on her hair. “I’m the one who should apologize. None of that danger happens to you if not for me.”

“No.” She lifted her face at once, entirely serious. “I fell because I wasn’t careful. That’s not on you. And—” her teeth pressed her lower lip, “—if it hadn’t happened, I might never have known how far you’d actually go for me.”

Neither of them needed the sentence unpacked. His recklessness had shown her the truth of her own feelings — and made this strange, imperfect arrangement something she could live inside.

The sun climbed; the room brightened. He looked at the pale, absurdly lovely face inches from his and heard his own breathing change. She felt the rise and fall of his chest, the hard clean lines of him — details there’d been no capacity to notice last night, and no way to un-notice now.

So warm.

Her cheek lay over his heart, and her own pulse gave up its independence and fell into step with it. Something wordless and wanting stirred. She tipped her head back — gaze catching first on the sharp line of his jaw, then traveling up until it locked with his eyes. Their breath had merged somewhere along the way, gone warm and quick. Her eyes dropped to his mouth — the mouth she’d kissed dozens of authorized times in two months and knew by heart — dry this morning, and still completely ruinous.

An impulse rose, older and less governable than last night’s confession. She swallowed once, leaned in, closed her eyes, and pressed her lips to his.

It began featherlight, a butterfly touching down. Then contact completed the circuit, and the current ran both spines at once. He went taut for a breath — and then instinct answered for him: his mouth opened slightly, accepted her, replied. Emboldened, she grew braver, tracing the line of his lips with a small creature’s territorial thoroughness; his breathing dropped a register, and the hand at her back tightened without consulting him, pressing her in. The temperature under the thin quilt began climbing at a rate with only one known destination.

Morning light; tangling breath; a spark fully capable of taking the whole field.

And then — right at the edge where it would have stopped being a decision — his reason got a hand on the alarm.

Wrong time. Wrong place. Wrong everything.

Her body was one night removed from hypothermic shock; Halo or no Halo, she was still weak. The crew boarded a ferry in hours. He could not — would not — let it come apart here, like this, in the margins of an evacuation.

It took approximately everything he had. He wrenched his head aside and broke the kiss.

His breathing stayed rough, chest heaving, the flood pressed down by main force.

“Sulli—” The voice came out badly scorched. “No. Not now.”

She blinked up at him, dazed, cheeks blazing, lips kiss-brightened — and read, clearly, the struggle and the restraint standing in his eyes. Disappointment washed through her. And with it, unexpectedly, something like reassurance.

At least he isn’t ruled by it.

She bit her lip, said nothing, tucked her burning face into the crook of his neck, and nodded once. Understood.

He breathed the fire down to coals, patted her back gently, and made the executive decision before the situation could vote again. “We should get up. Rest properly once we’re back in Seoul.”

Carefully, he unwound her arms from his waist and neck, sat up, and lifted the quilt away. Cool morning air poured in; his overheated skin answered with a full-body prickle.

It also left him standing in the light exactly as last night’s medical protocol had required — wearing nothing at all.

“Ah—!”

A strangled yelp from the bed. Sulli went scarlet from hairline to collarbone in roughly one frame. She had, technically, encountered the concept before — through the safe glass of a screen, in videos she would deny to her grave. This was different. This was real, near, and entirely unedited: a shoulders-to-heels architecture of trained muscle, wide shoulders over a narrow waist, every line carrying the kind of unhurried power that years of discipline — and, unknown to her, a system’s quiet reinforcement — had built. And in one involuntary, catastrophic glance, considerably more information than her nervous system had rated capacity for.

Her brain emitted a single flat tone and whited out. She hauled the quilt over her head like a spooked ostrich, sealing herself in from crown to toe, leaving only two enormous wet eyes glinting at the gap — mortified, and peeking anyway.

Caught between embarrassment and amusement, he rubbed his nose, rose with all the naturalness he could bill for, and retreated to the small corner bathroom.

Cold water, twice, hard. Calm restored. He raised his head and met himself in the mirror: the left cheek still carried a faint red print — last night’s fury and heartbreak, delivered by hand. He touched it. It stung. Against the memory of that black water, it registered as approximately nothing.

He washed, brushed, pressed a cold towel to his face. Then he walked back out — making no particular ceremony of the quilt-covered surveillance post tracking him across the room — opened his suitcase, and dressed. Clean underwear, jeans, motions fluid and unbothered, as if the room were empty.

From inside the quilt, through the gap, the surveillance post observed everything: the light drawing the long lines of his legs, the flex of his back as he bent, the narrow waist. Face burning, heart hammering, entirely unable to stop. She had not previously been aware that a man putting on trousers could constitute an event.

A plain gray long-sleeve went on last, ending the broadcast — though the fabric settled close enough to keep the outline of his chest in the historical record. He topped it with a ball cap and a black mask, which incidentally buried the evidence on his cheek, and only then turned to address the quilt.

“Sulli.” His voice had found its ordinary level again. “I’ll have Dong-hwan hyung go to your manager — she’ll bring your change of clothes and a light breakfast. Official version: you came by my place early to eat. That’s all anyone needs.”

A mosquito-caliber “mm” emerged from the quilt.

He knew mercy when it was called for. He crossed to the bed, patted the approximate summit of her head through the quilt, and said, “Wait for me.” Then he let himself out and drew the door carefully shut.

The instant the latch clicked, the quilt erupted. She burst out gasping, cheeks the exact shade of a ripe apple, ears radiating measurable heat. She pressed the cool backs of her hands to her face, which changed nothing, because the projector in her head was running a loop — the bare torso, the powerful back, and that one unpardonable glance—

“Aaah! Choi Sulli, what are you THINKING!”

She clapped both hands over her face and toppled backward into bedding that still held the shape and warmth and smell of both of them, and rolled, marinating in a full spice-rack of feeling — shy, sweet, unsettled, expectant — until it nearly closed over her head.

Because something had changed for good last night. She knew it. She was no longer merely the dongsaeng who leaned on him, worshipped him, loved him in secret. Between them now stood a night with death in it, jointly survived.

Twice this man had saved her life. He had planted himself between her and her own mother. Refusing him was no longer an operation her heart knew how to perform.

It wasn’t the love she’d first wanted — whole, exclusive, hers alone. But two rescues, one redemption, and the fact of loving each other… braided together, that rope was arguably stronger than plain romance, and infinitely harder to cut.

Oppa is a philanderer. And I fell for a philanderer.

But — and here her chin set — what other woman could bring what she brought? Who else had a bond written in seawater? And those other women — could they truly, cleanly accept sharing one man with rivals? No. They couldn’t. They’d tear at it and each other.

But the me of today — CAN.

The one who walks with Oppa to the very end will be me.

In fact — the girlfriend title itself was optional, for now. She could set it down. As his “little sister,” she could stand in front of every one of his other women in broad daylight, beyond suspicion, above the battlefield — dispensing a little eye-medicine here, a little sisterly breeze there, the way a beloved future sister-in-law does. They’d all have to court her. None of them would dare keep secrets from her. Which would make her — she hugged the quilt and smiled into it — the one woman holding the whole board.

Outside, he drew a long breath of the cool, damp morning and filed the entire preceding hour under LATER. The crew tents were five minutes off; he found his manager in the yard.

“Hyung.” He kept his voice down. “A favor. Go to Sulli’s manager — have her bring Sulli’s change of clothes and something light and easy to eat. Tell her—” a beat, “—Sulli caught the sea wind last night, slight chill and low fever, she’s resting. Have the manager come along too. We eat, then take the ten o’clock ferry out.”

Ryu Dong-hwan looked at him, ran the arithmetic he was always going to run, and asked none of it. “Done. I’ll handle it clean — the company won’t hear a syllable.” He clapped him on the shoulder and added, with a weight he didn’t bother disguising, “You watch yourself too. The crew knows the etiquette, but every set has a loose mouth somewhere. Best not to be seen at all.”

“I know. Thanks, hyung.”

Dong-hwan went off to manage the aftermath. He stayed in the yard alone, looking out at the blue line of the sea.

Last night had been genuinely, stupidly dangerous. And by pure blundering accident, it had also delivered him Choi Sulli’s whole heart — sealed, notarized, and non-returnable.

Which left the ledger with two names on it still: Irene and Taeyeon. Two homebodies, two very different temperaments, both owed a slow, careful truth — and both needing to be held steady when it came.

How the road ahead went, he didn’t know. Whether it could even work, he didn’t know that either.

But the second life had made the career almost unfairly smooth. A little challenge on the heart’s side of the books — he watched the horizon and found, to his own mild disapproval, that he was looking forward to it.
