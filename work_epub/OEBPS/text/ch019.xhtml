Chapter Nineteen

Chingu

Joy had a voice like honey and a face to match — sweet-featured, a little baby fat still rounding her cheeks, a smile that could power a small town. In a dusty-pink cropped hoodie and high-waisted joggers, the hem cutting exactly at a firm young waistline, she was youth itself in athletic wear.

She skipped up with her arm looped through Seulgi’s, eyes curving into crescents.

“Becaaause — I really, really wanted to train with eonni today.”

Seul-gi gently disentangled herself, then latched onto Irene like an affection-seeking cat, a note of smug pride in her voice.

“Lots of people want to train with eonni. But I’m the only one who’s actually lasted this long~”

Soo-young’s eyes went instantly, fiercely resolute.

“I’ll last too! Watch me!”

Joo-hyun just smiled her soft smile and took a small sip of coffee, saying nothing — though having the juniors cling to her like this clearly warmed her all the way through.

Joy had entered SM through the Seoul auditions in January this year — a mere two months ahead of Ji-ho. And Irene, gentle and kind by grain, mothered every new girl who came through the company doors so thoroughly that the trainees had crowned her with the nickname “Mama Bae.” The new kids stuck to her like magnets.

Then Seul-gi’s gaze landed on Ji-ho, and surprise flickered through it. Handsome. And I’ve never once seen him in the building.

“Hello, sunbae! I’m Ji-ho — just passed the official evaluation.”

“YOU’RE Ji-ho?” Seul-gi’s small, lively eyes strained wide with astonishment. “The one they say grinds harder than eonni?! And you already passed the official evaluation??”

“That’s me — passed two days ago.”

As he answered, he took her in: black cropped hoodie, high-waisted cargo-style wide pants, 161 centimeters with proportions that made the number irrelevant. Years of childhood ballet had left her posture ruler-straight, faint abs ghosting into view when she moved — handsome and pretty at once, in that way only dancers managed.

This company is a flower field. They genuinely do not admit plain people.

“All right then. Welcome, hoobae.” She stuck out a small fair hand; he dipped politely and shook it. “Thank you, sunbae.”

“So — what year were you born?” The curiosity was open now.

“January ’94, sunbae.”

“Really?!” It came out as a squeak. Her eyes made another valiant attempt at widening, to modest effect. “I’m ’94 too! February, though.”

Then the grin bloomed, delighted.

“Seung-wan’s February ’94 as well! You two can speak banmal — be chingu~”

On the Peninsula — a society ruled top to bottom by the seniority ladder — the gap in your birth years dictated your titles, your verb endings, the very borders of acceptable behavior. Elders received honorific speech, never their bare name; juniors yielded first. But age-mates were the single sanctioned exception: they could drop into banmal, speak freely, actually say what they meant. Which was why, in Korea, people the same age became friends at almost embarrassing speed.

Wendy. The future first main vocal of Red Velvet — and its resident polyglot. Ji-ho knew precisely how valuable this friendship would someday be, and smiled warmly on cue.

“Deal. Banmal it is, then?”

Fresh off the plane from Canada, Seung-wan had roughly zero attachment to the formality ladder anyway. A cheerful nod.

“Sure!”

“Seung-wan — coffee’s on me. All of you, actually. What do you say?”

“No no no—” Hands flapping, a flush of embarrassment. “I can pay for mine!”

“Relax. We just agreed we’re chingu, didn’t we? You get the next round.”

He was already walking to the counter. He came back balancing three cups of instant coffee and handed the first to Seung-wan.

“Here.”

“Thank you… I’ll savor it properly.” She received it with both hands, eyes shining.

“And these are yours.”

Soo-young and Seul-gi traded a startled glance.

“Us too?!”

“Take them. Get me back next time — that’s the whole system.”

A moment’s hesitation, then two careful, almost ceremonial acceptances — and two private vows to repay this debt at the first opportunity.

“Thank you.”

Seul-gi felt the asymmetry most keenly. She was February ’94 too — but she was his senior, which meant no chingu shortcut for her unless they grew genuinely close later. And besides: on the Peninsula, seniors treated juniors. A junior buying for a senior was almost unheard of.

Here the cultural wiring showed its seams — because a person’s home programming never fully overwrites. Twelve years on the Peninsula, and underneath it all Ji-ho still ran on pure American generosity: if you’re buying for two, you buy for four. Splitting the group down some invisible seniority line felt, to him, actively rude. So he’d simply bought the round.

Field Notes

Field notes: the economy of goodwill, Peninsula edition.

Korean society prizes group harmony above almost all else — and a one-sided treat creates a giver-receiver imbalance that pricks at the collective sense of equality. So the default, learned young, is shared consumption: classmates splitting tteokbokki, coworkers dividing the dinner bill, friends tallying travel costs by headcount, even couples alternating or going dutch. Nobody wants to burden the other side — or be read as showing off, or buying control.

Even Girls’ Generation, close as sisters, ran birthdays on the same logic: the birthday girl treats her members to coffee — modest, level, balance preserved — rather than laying out a lavish feast. Group dinners split the bill well into their thirties, before anyone started quietly slipping off to pay first.

Which is exactly why coffee occupies the perfect equilibrium point: cheap enough to accept without weight, warm enough to mean something, and infinitely reciprocable. On the Peninsula, “let me buy you a coffee” is friendship’s optimal opening move — pressure-free goodwill, with a built-in next round.
