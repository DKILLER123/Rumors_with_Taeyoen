Troublemaker.

✦ ✦ ✦

The next door opened before the bell finished its first chime.

“Oppa!”

Sulli launched herself into Ji-ho’s arms. He caught her securely while she looped both arms around his neck, laughing with the uncomplicated delight she reserved for private reunions.

Her hair was still damp from a shower, and toothpaste left a trace of mint when she kissed him.

It had been too long since they had met without a set, manager, or flight schedule waiting nearby. She stayed close until excitement softened into contentment.

“There are too many schedules. And we’re preparing another album.” She rested her cheek against his shoulder. “But I paid off everything I owed the company. I have savings now.”

Pride lifted the final words.

Ji-ho carried her to the sofa and set her down.

“Then don’t hand them to anyone casually—not even me.”

Sulli blinked.

“I was going to say you could manage them.”

“I can offer the family fund after you read the agreement and obtain independent advice. Until then, keep your money where you control it.”

“Oppa became very serious.”

“Chairmen are occasionally required to be.”

She laughed and tucked herself against him.

“I spend too much anyway.”

“If you can bankrupt me, I’ll consider it an achievement.”

Ji-ho reached into a shopping bag and produced a small box.

“For you.”

Sulli opened it and gasped.

“They’re beautiful.”

She studied the earrings, then studied him.

“Why such an expensive gift?”

“Do I need a crisis before I’m allowed to give you something?”

Sulli’s expression changed.

“Hara eonni.”

Ji-ho exhaled.

“Partly.”

Sulli placed one finger over his lips.

“You don’t need to invent an excuse. I know what happened. Ji-eun told me you moved rooms before anything happened.”

“And after Gwangju?”

Her eyes became curious.

“That part is between you and Hara eonni until she decides what to tell us.”

Then Sulli smiled with exaggerated innocence.

“Ji-eun was the dangerous one. She kept suggesting we should teach you a lesson.”

Ji-ho frowned.

“Did she?”

“I defended you. I’m your most loyal little sister.”

“Naturally.”

“If you punish her, don’t be too harsh.” Sulli counted thoughtfully on her fingers. “One stern conversation. Maybe two.”

Ji-ho laughed.

“I’ll follow your merciful ruling.”

“And don’t tell her I suggested it.”

That, Ji-ho decided, was true friendship.

He drew Sulli closer. Their reunion became private beyond the living room, affectionate and mutually chosen; the details belonged to them rather than the page.

JYP Entertainment an old American dream and a complicated shareholder

“An American campaign?”

Park Jin-young lowered his phone.

Ji-ho was more like him than he wanted to admit. Both had been rejected by SM. Both had forced their way into the industry through songwriting and performance.

Ji-ho had already surpassed him in wealth and current influence.

Talent, Park privately insisted, was another matter.

Give me that face and I would have been unstoppable.

He touched his own cheek, sighed, and returned to the real issue.

Breaking into the United States had been one of Park Jin-young’s great ambitions—and one of his most expensive lessons.

LOEN × JYP · LOVE-HATE CORPORATE HISTORY

Investment, control, retreat, and mutual dependence

Wonder Girls“Nobody” entered the Billboard Hot 100 and the group toured in the United States, but the extended overseas push consumed capital and weakened domestic continuity.

LOEN investmentIn the novel’s corporate timeline, LOEN’s financing gave it a 25.4% position and second-largest-shareholder influence.

Control conflictJYP resisted further acquisition and reorganized through its listed-company structure to preserve management control.

Starship pivotAfter takeover discussions failed, LOEN pursued a 70% Starship stake—the real-world transaction later closed in December 2013 for approximately ₩15 billion.

Future ruptureJi-ho remembers LOEN disposing of its remaining JYP position around GOT7’s 2014 debut, adding pressure to an already fragile company.

Real corporate details and the novel’s alternate ownership percentages are distinguished where they diverge.

JYP could not afford to treat LOEN as an enemy. LOEN controlled essential digital distribution. Yet Park Jin-young could not permit any shareholder to take the company from him.

After Ji-ho acquired LOEN, Park had invited him to visit in hopes of resetting the relationship. Ji-ho accepted and then became too busy to appear.

The announcement of ECHO revived Park’s curiosity.

They had chased the same impossible market. Perhaps they still had something to say to each other.

He called LOEN again.

Ji-ho’s future memories supplied their own strategic temptation. TWICE would become the first girl group after Girls’ Generation to achieve undisputed top-tier dominance across Korea and Japan.

A joint venture might be possible one day. So might shared training, distribution, or territorial rights.

But the trainees in those rooms were not inventory to be carved up.

If LOEN and JYP built something together, it would require contracts, parental protections for minors, education safeguards, transparent revenue terms, and the trainees’ informed participation.

The money could be enormous.

So could the responsibility.
