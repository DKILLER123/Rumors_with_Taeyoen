Chapter Eleven

A Man Is Forever Eighteen

The next morning — riiing, riiing, riiing… The alarm sank into Ji-ho’s dreams like a sharpened hook and hauled him bodily out of them.

He groped blindly across the nightstand, finally landed on the phone, cracked one eye open just far enough to read the screen, and killed the 6:30 alarm. Then, with all the grace of a sloth on a Monday, he levered himself upright.

He was a portrait of the morning after: hair savaged into a bird’s nest, sleep crusted heavy at the corners of his eyes — the honest wreckage of a body that had dropped into the deepest possible sleep after being pushed far past its limits. He smelled magnificent, too, in the worst way. Last night the drowsiness had rolled over him like a tide; he hadn’t even had the strength to shower before crawling into bed and going under.

Experimentally, he flexed an arm. Then a leg.

They moved — smoothly. Freely. None of the leaden, scream-with-every-step muscle agony that should have followed a fourteen-hour training day.

The system. The system is absolutely ridiculous. God bless it.

He threw off the covers, shucked everything he was wearing, and padded to the bathroom, where he blitzed through the full routine — teeth, hair, shower — in under ten minutes flat. Eighteen-year-old hardware plus hot water equaled instant resurrection; he stepped out feeling like a new operating system had been installed overnight.

Past 50, the price went up: talent required, effort doubled. But progress you could see, progress with numbers on it — that was rocket fuel for the soul.

He picked up the phone and checked MeTube — and nearly dropped it.

Two hundred–plus new fans in a single day, on a platform this size, in this era — a genuinely respectable number. Ten years from now, a clip like that would’ve done six figures easily. But there’d be no follow-up upload for a while. From here to the evaluation, every waking hour belonged to training.

He dressed, shouldered his bag — two spare tees packed this time; lesson learned — and headed out.

His current lodgings were a budget youth hostel, about ninety dollars a night — bleeding money he didn’t have. But apartment-hunting required time and sanity, and he currently possessed neither. It would all have to wait until after the evaluation.

Seven a.m. sharp, same as yesterday, he walked into the company. Same rooms. Same grind.

On the Peninsula, cultural export was national strategy, and the entertainment industry it powered was a gladiator pit. Everyone ground themselves down, a dozen-plus hours a day, training until their bodies felt hollowed out — and it worked, that was the maddening part. The idols who survived to debut came out the other side as all-rounders, nearly without exception. The main vocal who danced beautifully. The main dancer who could genuinely sing. The idol-turned-actor who was, at minimum, competent. Even the actors could usually carry a tune and a routine. Competition this savage left only one road: out-grind everyone, and hope the bridge held while ten thousand others crowded onto it with you.

The practice rooms stayed serious, airless places because of it. To his quiet relief, no one hazed him — being eighteen, he was the oldest of the provisional trainees, and age still counted for something here.

That night, Irene packed up at 1 a.m., and Ji-ho exhaled a breath he’d been holding all day. At present-day SM, no one out-grinded her — she was, reliably, the last one out of the building. He trained his ritual ten minutes past her, banked the daily reward, and dragged himself back to the hostel.
