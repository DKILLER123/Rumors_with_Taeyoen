Chapter One Hundred Seventy

Coaching Sulli

March 1, 2013 · Shooting day one The office tower set a one-take opening, an elevator, and a rescue in the corridor

Scene one, in motion:

Fine sweat beading on his face, brows knit tight, eyes urgent — Seo Yeon-woo cut through the crowded street at a run, shoulders twitching side to side as he threaded the extras.

“Sorry — excuse me, coming through.”

Quick words, breath ragged from the run, a bobbed apology to each obstacle, a clean sidestep around the next.

“Sorry!”

“Coming through, coming through!”

The company doors ahead — he accelerated, half-skidded into the turnstile, fumbled the ID card from his pocket, beeped through the gate sideways, and bolted for the elevator bank, jabbing the call button in a rapid drumroll before leaning in, panting, eyes nailed to the floor indicator.

“Come on, come on — jebal — come ON.”

One designed long take, street to lobby to elevator, in a single breath — dolly tracking smooth alongside, fixed units catching the coverage.

“CUT!”

Kwak’s voice came through the floor monitors bright with pleasure.

“Excellent! ONE TAKE!”

The set exhaled as one — scattered applause from the crew. First shot of the picture, first try. The kind of omen film sets live on.

He stepped out of Seo Yeon-woo’s panic like a coat, wiped the spritzed-on sweat with a sleeve, and turned to find the director already out of his chair.

“Honestly? I was worried.” Kwak clapped his shoulder hard. “Idol training makes the camera an instinct — most idol-turned-actors can’t STOP finding the lens. They check their angles mid-scene and the performance dies on the spot. But just now? You were purely the man about to miss his clock-in. Eyes full of turnstiles and elevator numbers, nothing else. You never once knew where the camera was. That immersion, from a rookie — talent, and a good head. Both.”

“The credit’s yours, Director — and the crew’s. The set made it easy to fall in.”

“Hahaha, spare me the modesty. Camera-sense is half heaven-given, and you have it.”

Son Ye-jin had watched the take from the wings, and drifted over now, changed into her scene wardrobe — soft knit under a long camel coat, gentle smile in place.

“Genuinely natural. You caught that city-morning franticness exactly — the sweat-wipe, the rhythm of the breathing, all of it. Ji-ho-ssi, it seems the music isn’t the only place you carry talent. Director Kwak’s eye remains vicious. He’s found another treasure.”

“Thank you, sunbae-nim. I have a great deal to learn — please instruct me freely.”

A few paces behind, Sulli had her hands laced together at her chest, up on her toes, eyes flat-out sparkling. Watching her oppa collect praise from a legendary director and the nation’s queen of melodrama — the pride nearly boiled over, tangled up with something warmer. He writes songs that cut straight in. He sings and she forgets to breathe. And now, FIRST take, first scene, and the whole set applauds. The look she aimed at him said, plainly: see him? I chose him.

And at the edge of the same crowd, Chae Soo-bin felt her palms go damp. Following THAT debut take, opposite these veterans — she inhaled slowly and coached herself steady.

Kwak checked his watch, scanned his set, and swung an arm.

“The mood is GOOD — we ride it! Next setup! Ye-jin-ah, you’re on.”

“Ne, Director. Ready.”

The elevator interior. Doors sealing out the world’s noise; soft toplight; a hush with walls.

Take one:

The “sunbae from upstairs” stood composed at the back rail, elegant, gaze level. The chime sounded; the doors parted on the ground floor; Seo Yeon-woo lunged in, still blowing hard — and in the instant he registered WHO he was sharing the box with, the panic drained clean out of his face and something younger flooded in: surprise, delight, and a hopeless awkwardness.

“G-good morning.”

The stammered greeting; the smile a size too honest. She tipped her head a few degrees, returned a courteous, gentle curve of the lips and a small nod — friendliness and distance in the same economical gesture, nothing wasted.

He seemed to remember, suddenly, the state of himself — straightened the backpack, tugged the run-crumpled collar square, smoothed the jacket. Composure mattered, in front of THIS person. The box hummed. His eyes began to wander with theatrical casualness — ceiling, buttons, wall — snapping back from her direction every time they strayed too near, fingers picking at the strap. The whole crush, legible in fidgets.

Then, visibly summoning it, he turned.

“Haven’t— seen you around much lately.”

“Ne. It’s been a busy season.”

Perfectly pleasant. Perfectly closed. He absorbed that, glanced at the climbing floor numbers, throat working — and made his run at it anyway, shy and sideways:

“Um— tonight, are you free?”

She turned, mildly puzzled, waiting. He turned fully, gathering every scrap of nerve, eyes honest and terrified:

“If it’s all right, I’d like to—”

Ding.

Doors. And through them, folders hugged to her chest, crisp in office wear: Choi Seung-ah.

“Good morning!”

Bright, professional — and she stepped straight into the space between the two of them, slicing the moment in half.

“CUT!” Kwak’s brow had a crease in it. “Soo-bin-ssi — Choi Seung-ah is a sharp career woman, not a first-week intern. Your shoulders flinched on the entrance; the presence collapsed. Again.”

“I’m sorry, Director-nim! I’ll fix it!”

No harshness for a rookie’s first NG — just the standing order: “Reset, everyone. Soo-bin-ssi, relax — but find her spine first.” She breathed deep, and beside her a low voice added, warm and even: “Soo-bin-ssi. You’ve done this on stage a hundred times. This doorway is just another wing entrance. Walk in like you own the floor.” The knot in her chest loosened a turn. “Ne. Thank you. I’m ready, Director-nim.”

Take two: she entered like a closing argument — stride level, chin set, the insertion between them clean and confident.

“Good — that take works!”

The scene ran on. Three passengers, triangled against the walls; the numbers climbing; the air gone delicate.

“Yeon-woo-ssi.”

He turned at the sound of her voice, faint hope surfacing—

“I’m getting married.”

Beside them, Choi Seung-ah’s eyes went saucer-wide — the full just-overheard-the-scandal-of-the-quarter face, gaze ping-ponging between the two of them. And on him: the blood visibly leaving, pupils dilating, mouth opening on nothing. Every fidget and hope of the last ninety seconds froze solid — the confession sentenced to death before it finished being born.

Ding.

Her floor. She stepped past him, unhurried, and at the shoulder-brush moment tilted her head a few degrees:

“Too late, you know.”

Flat calm, one grain of regret buried in it — and she was gone down the corridor, out of frame.

“CUT! Beautiful!”

The director’s monitor replay confirmed it: the veteran’s control absolute; the male lead’s stunned wordlessness landing exactly; the rookie’s rubbernecking delicious. One proactive swing from the hero, answered with a wedding announcement and three words in passing — the classic opening wound that buys a decade of introversion.

And with that, the cameo was wrapped. Kwak crossed the floor beaming — “Ye-jin-ah, THANK you. You saved me real trouble today.” — “Aigoo, Director, so formal. Feed me something good next time. And keep me in mind when the right role comes.” — “First call. Always.” She made her farewells down the line, stopping last at the male lead: “A pleasure, Ji-ho-ssi. When the next album comes — I expect a signed copy.” — “My honor, sunbae-nim. Delivered in person.” A wave, a ripple of bows, a faint trace of perfume — and the set returned to work.

✦   ✦   ✦

The morning rolled on through the office-floor material: Team Leader Park lying in wait at Yeon-woo’s desk, planner rolled like a baton — “9:14! Look who FINALLY graces the company!” — the volume climbing, the improvised planner-taps to the head that Kwak called in from the monitors (“Kwang-soo — tap him with the file, twice. Ji-ho — flinch small, don’t dodge big.”) landing so well the director laughed his own take half-off. Light office comedy, minimal NGs, the morning’s quota clearing early.

Which brought the schedule to the day’s true summit: the reunion.

Scene: a partner company’s planning office. Team Leader Park escorts a recomposed Yeon-woo in for the first coordination meeting; Choi Yoon-hyuk, the counterpart lead, waits inside. Cards are exchanged — and then, from the inner door: light, quick footsteps, and Go-i rounds the corner at a trot, arms full of files, head slightly down, late and hurrying.

The card in his hand stopped mid-air. His head came up slowly, as if pulled on a wire. The camera closed on his eyes: bare recognition first — then shock — disbelief — memory flooding its banks — and finally that fine tremor of something long-buried being struck. The set’s side-light rode her as she ran, hair and flushed cheek and focused profile picked out in a soft corona, one degree too beautiful to be real. The long lens tracked his gaze tracking her — the whole world contracting to one running figure and the drum of a heartbeat.

“CUT! Ji-ho’s side — EXCELLENT. That’s ‘one glance, ten thousand years.’ Now Sulli’s coverage. Sulli — standby.”

Her single: the truth under the scene being that Go-i had schemed and searched for ten full years for exactly this man — so the shot asks for a stolen glance from the corner: pupils quaking on recognition, the whole decade surfacing in her eyes, then forcibly packed away as she makes herself run on. Kwak had braced for trouble — a young idol carrying THAT freight of interiority in one look was a tall order.

And the look itself came like a gift: her eyes flooded on cue, love and ache and welled-up patience all legible, utterly persuasive. The PROBLEM was everything below the neck. She overran her mark. Her path drifted and broke frame. Her eyeline missed his by degrees. And between takes her gaze kept sliding, traitorously, toward the lens — the idol’s reflex, fatal at thirty feet tall. NG. NG. NG. Kwak kept his voice level for the writer’s sake and the rookie’s, walking her through marks and eyelines each time — but his face was settling, and the crew’s patience had begun to shift its weight audibly.

“Twenty minutes! Actors reset; departments check equipment!”

She stood rooted where the take had died, lip caught in her teeth, eyes going red — not from the director’s face, but from the arithmetic in her own head: HE had handed her this role, and she was the one burning the crew’s hours, take after take, embarrassing herself and — worse — embarrassing him. The guilt crested; she ducked her head and made for the empty corridor corner before anyone could see it spill.

A paper cup of hot chocolate arrived under her nose, steaming.

“Oppa—” The word came out already cracked.

“It’s nothing. First film, first long-lens tracking work — marks and camera-sense wobble for EVERYONE at the start; half the professionals you admire NG’d their first sets to pieces. And listen: the director told me privately your eye-work is exceptional. The emotion is real, and THAT’S the part that can’t be taught. The rest is technique. Technique, we can fix.”

He pressed the cup into her hands.

“Drink first. Breathe.”

“But— I’m wasting everyone’s time— maybe I’m just useless—”

“None of that.” A light hand on her shoulder. “Not useless. Under-practiced. Different problem, fixable problem. Here — three tricks.”

He nodded toward the far wall.

“See the reflective strip on that fire hydrant? Don’t hunt the floor tape — the head-down search is what reads false. The camera’s over there—” a tilt of his chin, “—know it exists, then delete it. That’s not a cinematographer; that’s a block of wood with a lens.”

On-Set Coaching · The Girl in the Sun · Day 1

Three Tools for the Reunion Take

MarksNever hunt the floor tape. Catch a fixed glint — a reflective strip, a tile seam — in peripheral vision, and let the body run to it on its own.

LensKnow where the camera is, then delete it. The eyes and the chest carry exactly one thing: what Go-i feels this second about the man she has hunted for ten years. Everything else belongs to the director and the machine.

BreathFour counts in through the nose before “action,” out slow through the mouth — drop into the scene on the exhale, and hold one anchor-thought to stay inside it.

She listened with the tears drying, nodding harder at each one — the spine coming back into her by degrees.

“Mm! I’ve got it, oppa. One more time.”

Twenty minutes later, on her mark, she closed her eyes and breathed the way he’d shown her — and what she summoned wasn’t the script’s Go-i and Seo Yeon-woo at all. It was a boy who kept arriving exactly when she needed him, and herself. Close enough. Better than close enough.

“Action!”

Files hugged tight, she broke from the corner at a trot — path true, mark clean, eyes never once acknowledging the machine tracking her. The glance toward him carried the whole ten years.

“Cut! GOOD! That’s the one! Hold that state — we go once more for safety!”

The safety take ran cleaner still. One pass.

“That’s a KEEPER! Sulli — fast learner! Keep exactly this!”

The director’s face had fully thawed. She let out a breath that seemed to empty her whole body, and stole a look across the set — where a thumb was already up, and a smile behind it. The confidence flooded back in so fast it made her lightheaded. The shoot was one day old; the mountain ahead was real. But she had the best oppa in the industry standing in her corner — and Sulli, she told herself, you can absolutely do this.

After the lunch break, the crew folded back into the afternoon’s setups, tight and orderly — day one of The Girl in the Sun rolling on under early-spring light.
