Chapter Ninety-Nine

The Road to Jeonju

June 24, 2012 · Cloudy Songpa-gu — Apartment 801 & Environs a promise kept has a date on it

“Ji-ho! Could you help me carry the water?”

“OK.”

The delivery man had left the jug at the front door. Ji-ho hefted it and carried it to Bae Soo-bin’s room.

A clean, sweet fragrance met him at the threshold — something floral tangled with plain soap. Very pleasant.

Bae Soo-bin stood barefoot on the floorboards, toes round and neat under a coat of pale pink polish. Cream oversized tee, light-blue denim shorts, and a pair of long, straight, distractingly fair legs. The features almost excessively delicate, still carrying that unfinished-girl freshness; skin fine as good white porcelain. A heavy fall of chestnut waves had been gathered into a loose low ponytail with a little black lace scrunchie, stray curls escaping at her temples and neck — which made the small face, if anything, more helplessly winsome.

“Thank you SO much, Ji-ho!” She sidestepped quickly. “Come in — just set it down anywhere.”

He carried it through and swapped out the drinking water. Inside, the sweetness thickened — that particular composite perfume of a girl’s room: a whisper of fruit-and-flower diffuser, the clean ghost of fabric softener, and underneath it, her.

The room itself was determinedly girlish — beige curtains half-drawn, a small congregation of plush toys on the bed, a desk crowded with trinkets and splayed-open fashion magazines.

She hopped to the dresser light as a rabbit, pulled a drawer, and produced a gift box of conspicuous quality — matte gold, deep-red ribbon, visibly not cheap.

“Here — this is for you!” She held it out, eyes curved into crescents. “It’s a snack box my dad’s friend brought back from abroad. Really good chocolates and biscuits and sweets. For all your trouble these past days.”

The artless smile carried nothing but the pleasure of sharing, and a small apology for imposing.

“Once you’re healed, buy me one meal and we’re square.” He accepted the box. “Oh — I’ll be out of town a couple of days. Not home.”

The smile dimmed a perceptible notch.

“Oh.”

“Thanks for the snacks.” He smiled and withdrew to his room.

She closed her door with all the enthusiasm of a rained-out picnic — on a leg that showed, it must be said, not one trace of a limp.

She had, in fact, healed days ago. The lingering “recovery” was a construction project: manufacturing small reasons to owe him more.

Ji-ho considered the gift box for a moment and put it in a drawer. Then he packed the bag of Starmi gummies, grabbed the car keys, changed into something crisp, and locked up.

Bae Soo-bin listened to the door click shut.

“…Gone, then.”

A sigh. Face-first onto the bed, blanket over head. Sleep.

✦   ✦   ✦

Ji-ho dropped into the driver’s seat and typed one word into the navigation: Jeonju.

Nine sharp, he was on the expressway, scenery unspooling at a lawful, even clip. Three hours later he took the exit ramp down into her hometown.

Jeonju meant one mandatory pilgrimage: bibimbap, in the city that invented the peninsula’s definitive version — where any old storefront might be hiding the truest bowl in the country. Masked, he slipped into a restaurant called Peninsula House and ordered the signature: twelve seasonal namul over rice, house gochujang, every layer distinct on the tongue. Legitimately excellent.

After lunch: Jaman Mural Village. A wartime refugee settlement reborn as an art quarter, its walls flowing with painted fairytales — the Little Prince, Oz. Young couples queued for photos at the angel-wings mural; he climbed the stairs past them to the hilltop overlook, and stood a while over the interleaved rooftops of the hanok village and the ribbon of the Jeonju stream.

✦   ✦   ✦

8:00 PM. Full dark.

Taeyeon sat cross-legged on the living-room floor, locked in mortal combat with her younger sister over the TV remote. Tomorrow was their mother’s birthday; she’d come home early for it.

Mid-tussle, her phone lit up on the coffee table.

She released the remote instantly, snatched the phone, and was up and moving toward her room in one motion.

“…? Why’d eonni just RUN?” Kim Ha-yeon blinked at the empty space.

Kim Ji-woong stroked his chin, gaze following his sister’s closed door with entirely too much thought behind it.

“Hello?” Voice lowered — and failing completely to hide the sweetness in it.

“I’m in Jeonju.”

His voice came through the speaker warm and unhurried, like evening wind.

“Really?! When did you get in?”

“Noon. Just had bibimbap — that place you recommended. Peninsula House.”

“And? Was it good?”

“It was. Though—” a pause, “—eating alone, something was missing.”

“Missing what?”

“You. Across the table. Stealing the bean sprouts out of my bowl.”

“As IF! I would never!” The protest arrived pre-sweetened. “…So where’d you go after?”

“The mural village. Climbed up to the overlook — you can see the whole hanok village from there. And I caught myself thinking: if you were here, you’d point at some rooftop and say ‘that’s where I used to play as a kid.’”

Something in her chest went soft.

“You actually guessed it… our old house is in that view. Oppa and I used to climb onto the roof. Ha-yeon was tiny then — she’d stand at the bottom crying for us to come down.”

“Sounds like a happy place.”

“Mm… did you just wander all day?”

“Wandered and thought. Mostly about which photo spot you’d run to first, and which street food you’d force-feed me.”

She hugged her knees and buried her laugh in the crook of her arm.

“Kongnamul-gukbap first, obviously. Then hanbok photos at the hanok village — you’d look SO good in hanbok.”

Not wearing that. Ever.

“You’d look good in anything.”

“Wh— what’s with you suddenly—” Her ears went warm; her voice went suspiciously soft.

“Just the truth.” One second of silence, and when he spoke again there was something new under it. “Nuna. I’m downstairs.”

Her breath stopped.

“I checked. No reporters. Nobody watching.”

Her fingers tightened on the phone; her heartbeat went ragged; half a month of missing him crested all at once.

“—Wait for me.”

“I’m at…” — and he described the spot, and the car.

She hung up, attacked her hair in the mirror, pressed on a whisper of lipstick, and emerged from her room wearing tremendous casualness.

“I’m going out for a bit. Buying some things.”

“I’m coming!” Ha-yeon sprang up.

“Girl stuff. Be good — stay with Mom.” She pressed her sister back down by the shoulders.

Ha-yeon opened her mouth; one look from her eonni closed it.

Kim Ji-woong glanced over — his gaze snagging one extra beat on her lips — said nothing, and went back to his phone.

Cap, mask, head down, quick steps out of the complex. One scan of the street, then a jog into a quiet side lane. At its end sat an unfamiliar car; as she reached it, the door swung open from inside.

“Nuna.”

He drew her gently into the back seat. The door shut, and the world went suddenly, completely quiet.

He removed her cap, then her mask, and kissed her.

She closed her eyes, wound her arms around his neck, and answered him without reservation — half a month of distance dissolving between one breath and the next. When they finally parted, she stayed folded against him, small and warm, while his fingers traced idle lines along her back.

He fished a bag of Starmi gummies from his pocket and held it up in front of her eyes.

“Wah—” Instant illumination. “You brought gummies AGAIN?”

“I promised you a new flavor last time.”

She tore in, popped one, and her eyes narrowed into pure contentment.

“Peach.”

“Sweet?”

“Sweet.”

Nestled against him, she finally had bandwidth to inspect the car, voice gone small and cozy.

“So this is the new car?”

“Mm.”

“I… should get back.”

“Mm.”

“Tomorrow’s Mom’s birthday. I can’t get away all day.”

“Mm.”

“The day after — I can make an excuse.”

“Mm.”

“It’ll be our first real date. It can’t get discovered, and it can’t be BORING. Plan accordingly.”

“Mm.”

She puffed her cheeks and poked him in the chest.

“Can you say anything besides ‘mm’?”

He lowered his head until their foreheads touched.

“Nuna. Saranghae.”

Her heart stuttered; her nose went suddenly, absurdly stingy. She tilted her face up and kissed him first.

This one tasted faintly of peach.

Then she straightened herself out, gathered her things, and slipped from the car, hurrying home through the dark with the gummy bag clutched tight.

She pushed open the front door — and found Kim Ji-woong leaning against the entryway wall, arms crossed.

“Where’d you go?”

“Just— a walk.” The hand with the gummies migrated behind her back.

“What’s in your hand?”

“Nothing—”

His gaze settled on her visibly faded lipstick. The corner of his mouth twitched. He let it go — mostly.

A beat of silence. Then, out of nowhere:

“That kid… Ji-ho, right?”

“Yes— I MEAN no! Who? Never heard of—” She attempted to slide past him; an arm gently barred the way.

His voice dropped, and for once his eyes were entirely serious.

“Taeyeon-ah.”

“If anyone ever makes you cry — remember you have an oppa.”

A pause. A dismissive little shrug of the lips.

“That kid… doesn’t look like he could take me anyway.”

She stood rooted, eyes abruptly hot. She ducked her head.

“…Thanks, oppa.”

Then she squeezed the gummies tight and fled to her room. The door clicked softly shut.

Kim Ji-woong stood a moment in the entryway, and shook his head. Looked the kid up online. Barely adequate. …Make her happy, you.
