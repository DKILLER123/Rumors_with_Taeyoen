Chapter Two Hundred Forty-Three

The Opening to Acquire CCM

Los Angeles · a hillside villa one homecoming, two diplomas — and a company quietly placed on the table

A black Maybach glided to a halt before the villa, its polished body holding the California sun in a muted sheen.

Two bodyguards stepped out first. One surveyed the drive while the other circled the car and opened the rear door.

“Agassi.”

The woman who emerged was in her late twenties, dressed in a perfectly cut suit that made elegance look effortless. Her long hair fell loose over one shoulder. She walked toward the villa with light, decisive steps while a guard followed with her suitcase.

It was Lee Kyung-hoo’s first visit. She let her gaze travel as she entered, taking in the villa’s restrained extravagance: pale stone, dark timber, and an indoor courtyard large enough to pass for a private conservatory. Rare flowers filled the room with color and a damp, green fragrance.

Real
Lee Kyung-hoo
Entertainment Executive · CJ Heir
CJ Group
Born: January 18, 1985

A Columbia-educated strategist returning to Korea with boardroom ambition—and a carefully concealed devotion to Song Ji-ho’s music.

A woman near sixty stood among them with a watering wand in one hand. She was pleasantly rounded, plainly dressed, and so absorbed in tending a pot of orchids that she did not notice the visitor until hurried footsteps crossed the tile.

“Gomo!”

Miky Lee turned. The years around her eyes softened at once.

Real
Miky Lee
Vice Chairwoman · Executive Producer
CJ Group
Born: April 8, 1958

The visionary force behind CJ’s global entertainment ambitions, equally at home discussing family duty, political survival, and the future of Korean cinema.

“Kyung-hoo. You’re here.”

Kyung-hoo all but threw herself into her aunt’s arms.

“Gomo, I missed you so much.”

A fingertip tapped her lightly between the brows.

“How old are you now? And you still come running to be spoiled.”

“In front of you, I’m allowed to be six forever.”

Miky laughed, the sound bright enough to startle the quiet room.

“Aigoo, this child. Come inside.”

They settled on the living-room sofa. Kyung-hoo had barely sat down before she reached into her bag and produced two document folders with theatrical ceremony.

“Ta-da. Look.”

Miky accepted them, and pride slowly replaced amusement in her expression.

COLUMBIA UNIVERSITY · ACADEMIC RECORD

Presented in Los Angeles upon completion of graduate study

“You did wonderfully, Kyung-hoo. You’ve made the Lee family proud. Your father will be delighted.”

Kyung-hoo’s eyes narrowed into delighted crescents.

“I was hoping you’d say that.”

Miky examined the certificates twice more before returning them.

“A master’s degree already. Time runs too quickly. One day you’re holding my hand; the next, you’re making me feel old.”

“Old? Gomo, please. If we walked down Rodeo Drive together, people would assume you were thirty.”

“You only say such shameless things because you know I enjoy them.”

Her aunt’s good humor was exactly the opening Kyung-hoo had been waiting for. She drew a breath and approached the true reason for her visit with studied care.

“I’ve graduated. It’s time I went back to Korea and joined the company. Let me take some of the weight from you.”

The warmth vanished from Miky’s face.

“No.”

Kyung-hoo came to her feet.

“Why not? I’m part of this family too. If the family is in danger, I have the right to stand with it.”

“Not yet.” Miky’s voice was quiet, which made it harder to challenge. “Your father and I can still hold the line. Going back now would put you in the wrong place at the worst time.”

“What place could be more right than beside my father? President Park has already forced you to America. How long are we supposed to retreat? Her term is five years, not fifty.”

Miky’s palm struck the table.

“Lee Kyung-hoo!”

The name cracked through the room. For several seconds Miky’s breathing was the only sound. Then anger drained into exhaustion.

“She is not merely the president. She is Park Chung-hee’s daughter, and power gathers around that name whether we like it or not. Your father is already trapped in an investigation that could put him in prison. Tell me honestly—what could you accomplish by walking into the same fire?”

Kyung-hoo did not lower her eyes.

“Then I have even more reason to return. If Appa falls, my brother and I must be seen standing. The board, the lenders, our partners—everyone needs to know CJ has another generation ready to take responsibility.”

The answer left Miky silent.

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, concern remained, but pride had risen beside it.

“Good. That spine belongs to this family.”

Kyung-hoo’s shoulders eased by a fraction.

“So you’ll let me go?”

“You were always raised with CJ Entertainment in mind. Return and begin there. Your brother can concentrate on the group’s other businesses.”

Kyung-hoo embraced her so fiercely that Miky laughed despite herself.

“Thank you, Gomo.”

“For what? I have no children of my own. You have always been a daughter to me.” Miky patted her back. “Sooner or later, this work will be yours to carry. Better that you learn its true weight now.”

“I won’t disappoint you.”

“I know.”

Miky’s gaze drifted toward the sunlit courtyard.

“And perhaps America will prove useful. I have spent my life trying to carry Korean culture outward. Now I can study Hollywood from inside its own walls. One day, I want us to make a Korean film that reaches the Academy Awards.”

“If you decide to do it, Gomo, the Oscars should start preparing.”

“There you go again.”

“It isn’t flattery if it comes true.”

Miky shook her head, smiling.

People outside the industry knew Lee Soo-man, Park Jin-young, and Yang Hyun-suk. Fewer recognized the woman whose decisions could move an entire distribution network, build a multiplex culture, fund filmmakers, or place Korean content before a global audience.

Miky Lee had never needed the public to know her face. She preferred to build the stage.

Lunch was served in a dining room overlooking the city. Between courses, Miky began explaining the condition of Korea’s entertainment market—not as an aunt indulging a favorite niece, but as an executive testing a successor.

“I intend to sell CCM.”

Kyung-hoo’s fork paused.

“Core Contents Media? Why?”

“CJ Entertainment is already under scrutiny. CCM’s finances are a mess, and its flagship girl group has not recovered from the scandal. Keeping it inside the group gives our opponents one more weak point to press.”

“Who are you selling it to?”

“Kim Kwang-soo. He has operated the company for years. His handling of the recent crisis was poor, but his earlier record was not without merit. If he wants the business, selling it to him would be clean.”

Kyung-hoo took a measured sip of wine. Something unreadable crossed her face and disappeared.

“There is also an interesting young man in Seoul,” Miky continued.

“Interesting enough for you to remember his name?”

A small smile touched Miky’s lips.

“Ji-ho.”

Clink.

Kyung-hoo’s fork slipped against the plate.

“What is it?”

“Nothing.” The denial arrived much too quickly. She set the fork down with exaggerated calm. “I know him, of course. He reached the Billboard charts recently. I’ve listened to his music. He’s… very good.”

“Very.”

Miky studied her niece for half a heartbeat, then continued.

“When you return, find a reason to meet him. He writes, produces, performs, and understands film. Someone like that could become invaluable to CJ Entertainment.”

“Ne, Gomo.”

Kyung-hoo aimed for casual and missed by several degrees.

“I’ll still need a legitimate approach.”

“CJ invested in The Girl in the Sun. Profit settlement, investor reporting, a celebration dinner—choose whichever excuse suits you.”

“I see.”

She pressed her lips together, thinking too loudly.

“Kyung-hoo. Is something on your mind?”

“Me? Not at all.” Her eyes widened with such innocence that guilt practically introduced itself. “But I do have an idea.”

“You never needed permission to speak to me.”

“Then hear me out.”

Kyung-hoo leaned forward, and the playful niece disappeared. In her place sat a Columbia-trained strategist.

“Ji-ho arrived from Los Angeles as a Korean-American trainee. He debuted through LOEN, then acquired LOEN—all in roughly a year. Every move has supported the next. Before launching an English-language album, he used a film soundtrack to build recognition abroad, then amplified it through Instagram, YouTube, and every platform he could control.”

“Go on.”

“I think the acquisition was part of his plan long before anyone else saw it. A person that deliberate did not buy LOEN merely to sit behind a grand desk. He’s going to expand.”

Miky’s expression became progressively more curious.

She had been studying in New York. How, exactly, did she know this boy’s release strategy down to the platform?

Then Miky remembered Ji-ho’s face.

Kyung-hoo had never brought home a boyfriend. Ji-ho was younger, yes—but handsome, tall, independently powerful, and free of the poisonous complications that accompanied most chaebol marriages. If he joined the family, his talent alone could raise CJ Entertainment another level.

“Gomo? Are you listening?”

“Of course.” Miky returned with a smile she did not bother to hide. “What is your proposal?”

“If CCM must be sold, why not sell it to Ji-ho?”

There it was.

“Give me the business case.”

Kyung-hoo’s eyes brightened.

“A company with ambition grows fastest through acquisition. CCM gives him facilities, catalog rights, distribution relationships, and a roster that LOEN would otherwise need years to assemble.”

“And what does CJ receive?”

“LOEN equity.”

Miky’s gaze sharpened.

“Equity?”

“Through cross-shareholding. Ji-ho grew up in the United States and can move naturally between Korean and American markets. You continue building CJ’s Western network from here; I stabilize our position in Korea and open more of China. He becomes a bridge among all three.”

“You’ve thought rather deeply about him.”

“About the transaction,” Kyung-hoo corrected, almost primly. “LOEN controls Korea’s largest music-service ecosystem. Ji-ho is also a rare producer. If he takes T-ara, he may be able to restore the group when CCM cannot. CJ and LOEN would gain a durable alliance instead of a one-time sale.”

Miky leaned back.

Personal motives aside, the proposal had merit. Ji-ho needed CJ’s film distribution, exhibition, and production reach. CJ wanted artists capable of carrying Hallyu beyond its existing borders. A Korean-American soloist who had charted in the United States while building a career in Seoul possessed advantages few competitors could imitate.

“The structure is attractive,” Miky said at last. “But SK still holds twelve percent of LOEN and remains its second-largest shareholder. If we want influence, a token stake will not do. CCM alone is unlikely to buy us ten percent.”

Kyung-hoo’s confidence dimmed.

“Then?”

“Sell LOEN all of CCM if the valuation works. But if you want cross-shareholding, use CJ Entertainment shares.”

“CJ Entertainment?”

“Not because we volunteer them.” Miky reached over and smoothed a strand of hair behind her niece’s ear. “Because Ji-ho will ask.”

“You’re that certain?”

“He is an American citizen, backed by a path into the U.S. market and useful connections throughout Asia. That gives him options. But the brighter he becomes in Korea, the more resentment he will attract from people who distrust anyone they consider insufficiently Korean. SK is already inside LOEN. If CJ also becomes a shareholder, he gains another shield.”

“But then President Park may treat him as part of our camp.”

Miky stared at her.

This was not the passing enthusiasm of a fan. The child was already worrying about which political storms might reach him.

“Gomo, you’re drifting again.”

“President Park wants improved relations with China and stable ties with the United States. Targeting a Korean-American artist with Ji-ho’s reach would create the wrong kind of attention. He should be safe.”

“Should be?”

“As safe as anyone in entertainment ever is.” Miky’s tone hardened into instruction. “When you return, the CCM matter is yours. Do not introduce the cross-shareholding proposal too early. Learn what he wants first—and under no circumstances sacrifice CJ’s interests because you happen to admire the counterparty.”

“You can trust me. I’m supposed to inherit this business one day. Why would I damage it?”

“Good.”

Two years earlier · Columbia University

Couples filled the campus on a warm afternoon, walking too close and smiling as if happiness were an ordinary possession. Kyung-hoo watched them with an envy she would never have confessed aloud.

Her own marriage would never be ordinary. In her family, affection was welcome only after strategy had approved it. Graduate admissions, family expectations, and the looming machinery of succession pressed against her ribs until even breathing felt scheduled.

Then a song entered her headphones—light-footed, teasing, full of the uncertain warmth between friendship and love.

“Some” · written and composed by Ji-ho · performed by Ji-ho and IU.

She listened once. Then again. By evening, curiosity had become fascination. By the end of the week, she knew every stage clip, every interview, every small shift in his expression before a chorus.

She had stayed awake across time zones to catch broadcasts, collected performances, and debated styling choices with strangers who felt absurdly like friends. Now she would return to Seoul—and meet the person who had existed for so long only through speakers and screens.

Excitement fluttered beneath her sternum. So did fear.

How should she greet him? As a CJ executive? As a negotiating partner? Certainly not as the woman who had once watched the same encore twelve times because he smiled at the camera during the last three seconds.

The shower stopped. Kyung-hoo locked her phone and arranged her face.

Miky emerged, towel in hand, drops of water silvering the ends of her hair.

“Still awake? Sleep. Tomorrow we have a great deal to prepare.”

“Ne, Gomo.”

The lights went out.

Kyung-hoo lay in the dark with her eyes wide open, Ji-ho’s face stubbornly bright behind them.

When she returned, she would secure the best possible future for CJ. She would also discover what kind of man existed behind the music.

Both were legitimate objectives.

✦ ✦ ✦

Morning spilled through the curtains. Kyung-hoo woke with last night’s resolve intact, chose a simple ivory blouse and tailored trousers, applied light makeup, and went downstairs.

Miky was already at the breakfast table with a folder open beside her coffee.

“Eat. We have work to do.”

Over breakfast, she walked Kyung-hoo through CCM’s debt exposure, artist obligations, rights ownership, and the clauses most likely to complicate a sale. Kyung-hoo listened without interrupting, except when a question would expose the logic beneath the numbers.

“Before I approach Ji-ho, I want a deeper map of his holdings, financing, and expansion plans. If we understand what he needs next, we can negotiate the asset he values—not merely the asset we want to sell.”

“Correct. I’ll have a team prepare it. But remember: a good transaction is not the one that wins the room. It is the one that still looks wise ten years later.”

“I understand.”

In the study, Miky gave her a thick file of project reports. Aunt and niece spent the rest of the morning alternating between quiet reading and sharp, compressed debate, laying the first stones of a deal no one in Seoul yet knew existed.

◎ Republic of Korea Seoul LOEN Entertainment · the same morning

Ji-ho had no idea that Core Contents Media was moving toward him from the other side of the Pacific.

He was occupied with the five incoming trainees, the foundation of LOEN’s new training division, an English-language album, and the soundtrack for The Heirs. Ryu Dong-hwan was equally buried—interviewing candidates for manager and assistant while building an entire department from an empty floor plan.

Dong-hwan understood the rarity of the opportunity and attacked it with the zeal of a man constructing his own future.

Only one matter disappointed him.

Why had not a single truly breathtaking beauty applied for either position?

Somewhere in the back of his mind, Sulli’s wish list remained regrettably unfulfilled.
