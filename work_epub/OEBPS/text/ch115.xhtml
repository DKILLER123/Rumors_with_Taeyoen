Chapter One Hundred Fifteen

The Taste of Fire

Past Midnight A 24-Hour Convenience Store — Gangnam one iced americano. that was the whole plan.

The automatic door slid open on a wall of cold air. Ji-ho came in with his cap pulled low, wanting exactly one iced americano and a quiet exit.

The small hours of Seoul were still lit end to end, but this schedule was his natural habitat now — deep night was when the songs came, and the new album had kept him at the company late.

“Welcome,” the ajumma at the register said around a yawn, on autopilot.

He took an americano from the cooler, added a pack of almonds, and dug for change at the counter.

“Four thousand seven hundred won.”

He handed over a five; coins chimed on the counter — and the ajumma stopped, mid-count, and made a small “eh?”

His stomach dropped a floor.

She leaned in, squinting past the cap brim at the face beneath it.

“Young man… aren’t you… the one who sings ‘Some’? Ji-ho?”

One second of stopped time.

The handful of high-schoolers drifting the snack aisle rotated as one organism. He never even saw the approach vector — just the phone lens suddenly at his face and the flash going off.

“It’s REALLY him! Ji-ho oppa!”

“Oh my god, HERE? I met him HERE?!”

“Oppa, can you sign?? I LOVE your song!!”

“One photo! Just ONE! Please!!”

The voices came in like tide. Four, five girls compressed the checkout lane into a fan meeting; the ajumma, fully awake now, was excavating pen and paper from the drawer.

“It’s really Ji-ho-ssi? My daughter ADORES you — could you sign one for her?”

One month since debut. He’d seen the lightstick ocean from the stage, heard the screaming from behind a mic — but always in the places where “Artist Ji-ho” was scheduled to exist. This — midnight, a convenience store, civilian clothes — was the first time “Ordinary Person Ji-ho” had learned, on his own skin, what FAMOUS actually meant.

The crowd’s enthusiasm backed him into a shelf; snack bags rained.

“Careful!” — from the same girls whose lenses never wavered.

“Oppa you’re SO handsome in person!”

“I listen to ‘Some’ every single day!”

“Will you and IU eonni collab again??”

The questions stacked past parsing. He raised both hands, voice pitched over the noise:

“Please don’t push — let me say one thing first!”

They actually quieted, vibrating in place.

He took the ajumma’s pen, signed for the invisible daughter — “Thank you,” she said, cradling it like porcelain, “she’s going to lose her MIND” — and then worked through the line, one signature and one photo at a time.

“Oppa, you’re really SO handsome…”

The girls clutched phones and notebooks, faces glowing. He clocked the uniforms.

“High schoolers?”

“Ne, oppa!” A round-faced girl hugged her fresh signature to her chest. “Cheongdam Girls’ High, second year!”

“Third year’s coming, then. Genuinely hard road.” He finished the last autograph and threw the fist. “Hwaiting!”

“Oppa — HWAITING!!” — returned at four times his volume.

“Mm. Bye now.”

“Oppa, byeee!”

The door slid shut behind him on a rising bubble of audio:

“Daebak, daebak, SO handsome—”

“He’s so GENTLE, aaaah—”

“I touched his SLEEVE. I’m not washing this hand for three days!”

The muggy summer night closed around him and he exhaled from his heels.

Careless. Solo trips out were rare enough that he’d forgotten to switch on Utterly Unremarkable. Next time: full protocol.

He cinched the mask, dropped the brim, and set off toward the company at speed — then, a few steps on, couldn’t help glancing back. Behind the store’s glass, the girls were pressed to the window; catching his turn, they waved with maximum violence. He waved back — one friendly hand — and reaped a muffled shriek through the glass.

A few more steps, and his feet stopped on their own.

Across the street, the billboard wall: a full-building “Some” promotion. Himself in the black suit, eyes intent; IU’s solo panel beside it; between them, in flowing display type: “SOME” — more than a little bit of like!

He stood in the dark drinking his iced americano, looking up at a version of himself six stories tall, feeling something strange and slow move through his chest.

At the bus stop near the company — a few late stragglers on the bench, nobody looking — the ad screen replayed yesterday’s Music Core. Taeyeon in ivory lace at the MC stand, smile sugar-bright: “This week’s first place — congratulations to Ji-ho-ssi and IU-ssi, ‘SOME’!” The archived screaming echoed thinly across the empty platform. On screen: confetti, IU’s hand catching his, the side-by-side bow — and Taeyeon at the edge of frame, applauding, smile flawless.

The phone buzzed. Ryu Dong-hwan: Meeting 10 AM re: upcoming schedule. Also — too many fans at the main entrance lately. Use the garage. Before he could answer, the next one landed: “Running Man” sent a formal invite. Company wants you and IU together.

His thumb paused over the screen.

“Running Man.” THE national variety show. Just yesterday he’d caught a clip of Yoo Jae-suk grinning into a camera, joking that eight out of ten high-schoolers on any street were humming Ji-ho these days — that the cafe by his building ran “Gangnam Style” in the morning and “Some” all afternoon. And last night on the phone, Taeyeon had laughed at him down the line: “Our Ji-ho really became a big star.”

The fire is lit. It’s actually burning now.

Next morning, 9 AM: two hundred meters out from LOEN, the driver clicked his tongue.

“More of them today.”

Ji-ho looked out — and went still. What had been a scatter of waiting fans was now a small standing assembly: sidewalks packed, thirty people at a glance, lightboards blazing even in morning sun — SONG JI-HO. SOME. IU. Sunset-glow crimson and fluorescent yellow, a small sea of held light. The security ajusshi stood on the steps, sweating through his uniform, megaphone up: “Back up! Off the road! Everyone BACK—” — his voice sinking into the noise.

A sharp-eyed fan made the company car.

“Is that oppa’s car?!”

Every head turned. The crowd began to move like water finding a slope.

“It’s the company car! He’s HERE!”

“Ji-ho-ssi — straight to the garage?” The driver’s knuckles were pale on the wheel.

“Mm.”

The car swung toward the underground entrance; security held the line; a hundred phone lenses tracked the tinted glass, and he could feel the heat of the eyes straight through it. As the garage door swallowed them into the dark, the chant came through the concrete, unified and bright:

“Ji-ho oppa! Ji-ho oppa! OPPA!”

“After the Music Core finale aired, the digital charts rebounded — the All-Kill held another four hours.” He swiped. “Naver real-time top ten: you own THREE slots.”

“‘Gangnam Style’ is a monster, no argument — but the Triple Crown already rewrote the record book for solo debuts. Every network is sending collaboration requests. Endorsement inquiries have passed TWENTY.”

“Ji-ho. You’re famous. ACTUALLY famous.”

“Yeah.” He let it land. “Finally burning.”

“Fire’s good.” Dong-hwan clapped his shoulder. “But remember — fire lights the road ahead, and fire burns the man carrying it. Which one it does is up to you.”

Ji-ho walked to the window and looked down at the sea of people that existed because of him.

Four months ago: an anonymous trainee no one glanced at twice. Now: a bottle of water requires an extraction plan; his face papers the subway; his name lives on the trending board; the nation’s MC name-drops him on broadcast; a wall of lenses and eyes tracks the tint of his car windows.

The phone shivered. And kept shivering.

He took his seat. Schedules and planning decks fanned out before him.

Outside the window, faint and rhythmic, the sound of people calling his name.

Inside it, his life crossing into its next dimension.
