Chapter Thirty-Eight

Being Sick Alone Is Its Own Kind of Despair

Overheard · Subway Platform

A “Yah, Seo-yeon — LOOK at that guy. Look. LOOK.”

B “Whoa. Daebak. Is he a celebrity??”

A “Never seen him before. But with THAT face — if he were famous we’d know, right?”

B “Trainee, maybe? He’s got to be SM with that face.”

A “Speaking of SM — that Ji-ho is everywhere lately! The one who saved Sulli eonni! If he debuts I’m stanning INSTANTLY. But nobody knows what he looks like! What is SM even doing, sitting on his photos?!”

B “Didn’t they say he’s in the TTS music video? They’re TOTALLY starving us on purpose to pump the album numbers.”

Hearing his own name, Ji-ho surfaced from the reward-high and glanced toward the voices: two middle-schoolers, whispering at velocity and sneaking looks. The instant his eyes met theirs, both girls froze, combusted scarlet from cheek to ear, and spun away in a flurry of elbows.

He bit down on a laugh. And the girls had a point, actually — a face this thoroughly professionally assembled, wasted on a solo subway ride home? Criminal.

…I could go see Sulli.

Decision made. At the transfer station he changed lines, bound for her hospital.

✦   ✦   ✦

By then, Sulli had been standing at her window for the better part of an hour.

The last of the sunset had drowned; Seoul’s lights were coming up like scattered stars. The room held no sound but the monitor’s flat, patient beep… beep… — a metronome for loneliness, plucking at her nerves one tick at a time.

The final visitor of the day — the last of her members — had left that afternoon. They were all drowning in schedules; that they’d come at all was already generosity. Her manager unni had a feverish relative at home; Sulli had practically shoved her out the door herself.

As for her mother… her mother had come once. Under twenty minutes, all of it spent on three questions: when can you go back to work — are you listening to the company — will this affect the endorsements.

Sulli rested her forehead against the cold glass and closed her eyes.

The loneliness came in like tide — over the ankles first, then the knees, then rising past the chest with nothing to stop it. She thought of being small and sick: her mother simmering seafood porridge, her father reading fairy tales at the edge of the bed. Back then, being sick had felt almost like a gift — an all-day monopoly on both of them at once.

And now?

Something warm slipped down her cheek. She wiped it away fast, on reflex.

Don’t cry. Sulli, you’re nineteen. You’re an adult.

But the hollow in her chest kept dilating, and the cold kept pouring through it. She wrapped her arms around herself and whispered at the window, so quietly the glass barely fogged:

“If… if someone would just come sit with me. Even for a little while…”

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Three raps — unhurried, even — landing directly on her heartstrings.

She whipped around, pulse suddenly wild. This hour? The nurse had just done rounds. Her friends’ schedules were all accounted for; everyone was working—

Unless—

The impossible thought fired, and she was already running — nearly out of her slippers — hand on the door handle before caution caught up. What if it’s not him? What if it’s a reporter? What if—

Click.

The door swung open. Corridor light poured in around a tall, straight silhouette.

Sulli’s breath simply stopped.

A simple black shirt, sleeves rolled carelessly to the forearm over clean-lined wrists. A face like deliberate sculpture — high brow bone, deep-set eyes, the outer corners tilted faintly upward — and on his lips, still, the berry stain of a professional’s brush, restrained and lethal against cool-toned skin. And above all of it, those dark eyes, resting on her now with unhurried warmth.

“Evening, Sulli.”

Lower than on the phone. Warmer. The kind of voice that skipped the ears entirely and reported directly to the sternum.

Her mouth opened. Nothing came out. Her brain had been wiped to a single phrase, scrolling on loop —

“…So handsome!!!”

— and by the time she registered the loop had gone out loud, retrieval was impossible. Heat detonated across her face, ear-tips to collarbone.

Ji-ho laughed — a real one, quick and low, and it softened his whole face at once, eyes lighting like something bright bursting open against a night sky.

“Why, thank you.” A small courtly bow, offered with a flourish. “Now — would the lady with the impeccable taste care to admit her visitor?”

“O-of course!” She scrambled aside, hands flying to straighten the hem of her hospital gown. “Come in, oppa, come in — I just— I didn’t think you’d actually come.”

He stepped inside, gaze making its quick habitual sweep. The single room was tidy to the point of desolation: fruit baskets going quietly stale on the nightstand; the only living thing a small sheaf of white lisianthus at the window, glowing faintly in the dusk.

“Have you eaten dinner?”

She shut the door and trailed after him, shaking her head.

“Not hungry yet.”

Her stomach chose that precise moment to file a loud, gurgling dissent.

The air froze for exactly one second.

“Your stomach has entered a formal protest.” A small frown. “How is a patient supposed to heal without eating properly? Hm?”

That particular flavor of concern — direct, uncushioned, and gentle anyway — slid into the cold hollow of her chest like something warm being poured. Her nose stung without warning; she ducked her head fast and studied her own toes.

“Then… then let me take you to dinner, oppa. Call it thanks — for saving my life.” She looked up, eyes suddenly full of light. “I know a barbecue place. It’s amazing. Come on?”

“Are you even allowed out of here?”

“I’m discharged in two days anyway — it’s FINE.” She was already behind him, both hands planted on his back, steering him doorward with her whole modest body weight. “Wait in the corridor, oppa! I’ll change! Two minutes!”
