Chapter Fifty-Seven

Song Library Access

Ji-ho walked back into the old building — into a changed world.

“Ji-ho-ssi! Hello — I’m Oh Hyun-jae. Your singing was amazing!”

“Thank you.”

“Ji-ho-ssi, Han Tae-hee. That song was incredible.”

“Ne, hello.”

Greeting after greeting, the whole corridor’s warmth pointed at him — the simple, universal arithmetic of respect earned by demonstrated skill.

Evaluation day carried no group classes. Instead it carried consequences: trainees whose scores had slipped were called in for talks and doubled drills; repeat underperformers, after review, were counseled out and released. His walk back passed more than one pair of red-rimmed eyes, more than one quiet wipe of tears in a stairwell.

They all knew the arithmetic of the single-log bridge — ten thousand crossing, a handful reaching the far bank — and they’d thrown themselves onto it anyway. The stage light was that bright: bright enough to blind people to the fact that the bridge’s far end, for the overwhelming majority, was a quiet exit nobody filmed. His own first life had ended exactly there. Tragedy wasn’t the exception in this industry. Tragedy was the baseline.

Which was why, system or no system, Ji-ho still trained like a man being chased.

SM’s men and women trained separately, but the last practice room down the corridor had long since become the unofficial territory of the senior circle — Irene, Seulgi — plus, today, one Wendy, whose mind was demonstrably not on practice, because she’d spotted him the instant he entered and came bouncing over like a spring lark.

“Ji-ho, you’re back! Where did you GO? Do you know how long I— how long eonni and I waited? And your song — it was SO good — when did you even write it?!”

He let the chirping wash over him, stopped, and regarded her with dawning interest.

“Waited for me? What’s this — that eager to receive your punishment?”

He stroked his chin, deliberating with the gravity of a sentencing judge — then brightened.

“Your penalty: one full serving of mint-chocolate ice cream.”

“WHAT?!” A wail of pure despair. “NO! You KNOW I hate mint-choc more than anything on earth!”

“Heh heh heh.” Watching Wendy question her entire existence was, objectively, delightful. “Don’t like it? Fine — alternative terms.”

“Yes! Yes! I knew you were the best—”

“For one month: you call me oppa.”

“YAH! We’re the same age! We’re CHINGU! Absolutely not!”

“Then we return to the mint-chocolate—”

Mint-choc: the absolute blacklist. Her face crumpled at the mere taste-memory. But calling him oppa… In Korean, the word carried weight — closeness, warmth, a particular flavor of intimacy. And she discovered, examining herself with some alarm, that the resistance wasn’t nearly as fierce as it should have been. Three full waverings later, out it came, soft as cotton:

“…Ji-ho oppa.”

The sound of it out loud was TOO strange — she stamped her foot and fled to Irene’s side to bury her head like an ostrich.

Ji-ho laughed, thoroughly content. Sweet-natured girls like Seung-wan were the best entertainment on the planet — a human pistachio.

Joo-hyun watched him over Seung-wan’s buried head, brows knitting. Forcing her to call him oppa — what a juvenile hobby. And then the drum of worry started up: if that’s what he made HER do… what exactly is he going to demand from ME?

His gaze swung to her. Her spine straightened on pure alarm.

“I’m warning you. Don’t push your luck.”

The grin spread across his face like sunrise. He genuinely hadn’t planned anything outrageous — but with an opening like that, outrageous was now mandatory.

“I want you to—”

“Ji-ho!” A voice from the doorway cut the sentence in half. Production Center team lead Jung Hye-won beckoned. “A moment.”

“Ne.”

He turned and went. Behind him, Irene stood blinking at the amputated sentence. Want me to WHAT? Finish the sentence! You can’t just LEAVE—

“Yah — Ji-ho! Finish what you were saying!”

A hand waved lazily over his shoulder.

“When I’m back.”

The catch lived downstream: on physical album revenue, JYP and YG split roughly 50/50 with their artists — while SM’s standard ran closer to 95/5 in the company’s favor. It was the core grievance behind the TVXQ rupture. Hence the industry’s oldest joke: everyone wants to TRAIN at SM — and everyone who debuts wants to leave.

He pocketed the schedule. She slid the second sheet across.

“Company song library account application. Fill in your details and I’ll activate it. From now on, anything you produce, you can upload as a demo. When our artists prep a comeback, they pull candidates from the library — if your song is selected, the company pays you a one-time buyout. You’re a rookie, so figure five hundred thousand to two million won per song.”

Standard new-writer rates — and crucially, the buyout was only the floor. It paid regardless of how the song performed, and it didn’t touch the downstream royalties: streaming, album sales, live performance, broadcast and sync licensing — all of it settled through the Korea Music Copyright Association and flowing back to the writer for as long as the song lived. For a career built on a vault of future hits, this single sheet of paper was the on-ramp to everything.
