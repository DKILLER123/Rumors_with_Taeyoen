Chapter Seven

Dying for the Dream

The lessons started from bedrock: music theory, pitch, rhythm, meter. She had him match tones against the piano until every interval sat exactly where it belonged, drilling beat-counting alongside until the pulse of a song became something he felt in his bones instead of counted in his head.

Field Notes

Kim Mi-yeon’s fundamentals:

Standing — chest open, core engaged, shoulders loose. Sitting — spine tall, always.

Breath — diaphragmatic and combined chest-abdominal work, until the air beneath every note is a pillar, not a prayer.

“The voice doesn’t sit in the throat. It stands on the breath.”

The regimen would run until the formal evaluation — and by a small miracle of scheduling, one hour of it landed squarely inside the God of Learning’s golden window. Every principle she taught in that hour etched itself into him permanently, like letters cut into stone.

In Kim Mi-yeon’s eyes, Ji-ho was the student teachers pray for: a once-in-a-decade instrument, and a learning speed that frankly unsettled her. She taught harder, leaned in closer. Two hours evaporated, and Ji-ho surfaced from the lesson still hungry. First of the Big Three for a reason. This is what professional actually means.

“You’re the most perceptive student I’ve ever taught.”

The praise was genuine — though not entirely selfless. Every coach at the company lived under performance review, and producing a standout student meant a very handsome bonus. Watching Ji-ho absorb technique like dry ground drinking rain, she could practically see herself collecting it.

He bowed, deep and sincere.

“Only because you teach the way you do, seonsaeng-nim. Thank you for your hard work today.”

“Yours too.” She nodded, warm. “Go home early. Rest.”

She began gathering her things — though her day was far from over: work emails, lesson reports, and tomorrow’s prep still waited.

Ding— “Challenge quest triggered: [Dying for the Dream]. Check the Quest Hall for details.”

Oh? A quest? A spark of delight — he pulled up the details at once.

Ji-ho read the quest twice, thumb tracing his jaw. Both rewards were dangerously attractive.

Everyone in this industry knew the ugly secret: trainee-level intensity ground bodies down. Overwork now became ruined knees and shredded vocal cords at forty — the bill always came due. A reward that erased the physical cost of training every single night was nearly priceless. And Advanced Rap Mastery plus the outfit on top? Non-negotiable. He was taking all of it.

From eight this morning to nine tonight — meals and breaks deducted — he’d logged nine hours, the standard rookie load. He killed the lights in the vocal room and headed for the dance studios. Through every window along the corridor: five, six trainees still drilling under the fluorescents.

The legends are true. Nobody on this earth grinds like this country grinds. Though he was hardly one to talk — back in his lounge-singer days he’d once run five venues in a night, and sung a 5 a.m. set at the end of it.

Heat rose in his chest. Fine. Let’s go to work.

Back in the rookie practice room, he replayed the day’s lessons in his head, loosened up, and dove in. Seven or eight others trained around him, each sealed in their own bubble of effort, all of them running from the same axe: the trainee evaluation.

He worked until 21:50, one ten-minute break in the middle. Then — as if on some prearranged signal — the others began packing up around him, one after another.

Ji-ho stopped, chest heaving, and permitted himself a single smug thought: When it comes to the grind, I learned from the American service industry. You’re all still amateurs.

Go on, go home, all of you. Twenty-one more minutes and today’s reward is mine.

He took a pull of water, reset, and threw himself back into the choreography.

At 22:00 sharp — click. The practice room lights died without a shred of warning.

“Holy—!”

The word jumped out in reflexive English, pure Los Angeles, as he stumbled and nearly ate the floor. Seriously? A blackout? In the capital? Some developed country this is!

He groped through the dark on memory alone, found his jacket in the corner, fished out his phone, and thumbed on the flashlight. The weak beam carved out a small circle of the world. Gathering his things, he hurried out — and discovered the corridor and everything beyond it still glowing softly. Only the practice-room lights had been cut.

He checked the tracker again.

Hold on. Everyone left. So who — who is still out-training me?

He stood in the corridor and turned a slow, careful circle. Not a soul in sight. The itch of the mystery only burrowed deeper. Decision made: he would walk the corridor and check the practice rooms, one by one, until he found this phantom.
