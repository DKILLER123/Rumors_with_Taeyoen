A Tug of War

Friday. Cloud building down the west of the city, twenty-two degrees going on twenty-five, the light doing its late-September impression of an Italian film.

The early-autumn evening had put a coolness into the air that hadn’t been there last week. The sky went orange over the rooftops and then a pale gray-blue, and somewhere below the windows, faint and stubborn, osmanthus was flowering along a wall, in among the dry smell of the season turning over.

Not stuffy. Not hot. Just cool enough to matter on bare ankles, and the streetlamps came on one after another down the block like a row of cue lights.

Seo Ju-hyun came home wrung out from a day of vans, trailing her bag and her manners, and nearly walked into Im Yoon-a in the hallway: an Im Yoon-a in outdoor clothes, at the mirror, at seven in the evening, going somewhere.

“Eonni? Are you going out?”

And then the younger of the two stopped, because the hallway mirror had a job to do and the answer was standing in it.

Im Yoon-a was considering her own profile with one shoulder turned to the glass. A long, plain-weave cardigan over a pale tee; a full pleated skirt in light apricot that moved when she did; and, when she stepped back to check the whole of it, five centimeters of heel, which came down on the floor with a sound like a decision.

She put a hand up and tidied two strands of hair behind her ear, and let her fingertips rest at the ends of them a second longer than tidying requires.

“Ne. I have an appointment.”

She bent, buckled, stood. Patted the younger girl’s shoulder on the way past, twice, warmly.

“Seo-hyun-ah, fold the things on my bed for me, would you? You’re the best. You’re the best in the entire building.”

“Ne, eonni.”

Dutiful, delighted, doomed: Seo Ju-hyun changed into her slippers, padded down the hallway, pushed open the door of the room in question, and stood in it like a woman at the edge of a ravine.

“Omo… daebak…”

Every color the girl owned was on the bed. Long-sleeve tees. Thin knits. Two shirt collars arguing. High-waisted jeans, cigarette pants, gray sweats, one pleated skirt, one plain skirt, a cardigan on top of a cardigan, layered, wrong-sided, tried and shed, the geological record of an entire evening’s indecision compressed into one small mattress.

“Seo-hyun-ah, what are you standing in the door for?”

A head came over a shoulder, took in the bed, and the eyes narrowed to two delighted lines.

“DAEBAK EVENT! DAEBAK EVENT!” The voice went up an octave and took the news to the whole floor. “Yeo-reo-bun! Im Yoon-a is dating! Get out here! Im Yoon-a is DATING!!”

“Mwo?”

“Really?”

“With who? With WHO?”

Inside a minute the room had more people than the fire code was written for.

A knit top was held up to the light, turned inside out, inspected at the seams, and dismissed. “What does this prove? This is me on any given Tuesday.”

“You—” the arms-folded voice of long experience cut across the room, “—are not Yoon-a. Yoon-a plans. Yoon-a caps her options at three outfits and feels bad about the third. There are fourteen garments on that bed.” A pause, for the jury. “The longer a woman takes to dress, the more the evening weighs. Fourteen garments, yeo-reo-bun. Fourteen.”

“Daebak-iya~” Two hands had gone to somebody’s cheeks. “She’s out the door in ten minutes on a normal day. TEN.”

“So,” said the longest pair of legs on the floor, from the doorway, with the air of a detective announcing the butler, “this man is not a simple man.”

“Is it Lee Seung-gi-ssi? He’s said on air, how many times now, that Yoon-a is his ideal type.”

“Ani, she refused him. Politely. Twice.” This from the doorway itself, delivered with a wave, the tone of a person settling a small account. “And she would not have tried on fourteen outfits for Lee Seung-gi-ssi. She’d have gone in jeans.”

“Then who?”

“Why are we guessing? She comes home tonight. We ask her tonight.”

“True.” The legs crossed themselves at the ankle, sagely. “And if she won’t say?”

And the room, in one voice, with the sweetness of nine years’ practice, said the same thing at once: “Then we put her under the lamp.”

Underfoot, marble the color of standing ink, polished until it held a blurred, swaying version of her. A corridor wide enough to land something in. No ornament on either wall; the lighting came from nowhere you could point at, soft, and slightly less than the rooms deserved.

No noise. No packs of staff. Not one other guest visible the whole way, and once, a waiter, who stopped, bowed to a clean ninety degrees, and kept his eyes on the floor with the obedience of a man who has been trained to forget faces professionally.

Im Yoon-a kept her chin level and her pace even, and if the corridor had contained a stethoscope it would have reported a heart doing overtime.

Song Ji-ho.

Taeyeon eonni’s boyfriend. Tiffany eonni’s. The chairman of LOEN. A man I have done a commercial with in the mountains (a stream, a piggyback, an ankle) and neither of us has mentioned any of it since, which is its own way of mentioning it every time.

Tonight, for me, is acting. For him: I know what tonight is for him. I did the sums in the car. I came anyway.

She had tried on fourteen outfits for a man she was meeting “for acting.” She had put on the perfume she was saving. All evening, at intervals, she had told herself the same small sentence, it’s basic courtesy, in the voice of a woman feeding a stray and calling it nothing.

“Miss Im. In here, please.”

The guide stood aside and opened a solid-wood door with both hands.

She inclined her head. “Thank you for your trouble.”

The door shut behind her, gently, like a book.

Cool sandalwood. Not heavy, not sweet; a held breath of it, doing the room’s thinking for it.

Im Yoon-a looked up, and her breathing stopped for one second, cleanly, like a lifted needle.

He sat on the sofa by the window, angled a quarter-turn toward the door, and the night had come all the way down outside; the courtyard lights stood up through the tall glass and drew his outline in low silver.

Song Ji-ho · the sofa by the window, nine o’clock, no cameras

The face above the collar was cold-pale and clean-lined, the lips full and warm enough to be an argument with the rest of it; and behind the gold rims, the eyes were the deep kind, the kind that make a room feel farther from the city than it is. It was, objectively, a face that was difficult to look at for long; not because anything was wrong with it. Because everything wasn’t.

And he was looking at her.

The cardigan, the pale tee, the apricot skirt. The small face, the fine, slightly pointed chin, the features put together with a confidence that had taken the whole industry twenty years to admit. Skin so white against all that pale cloth that the cloth looked like the instrument and the skin the note.

Clean. Cool. Very good-looking.

We both dressed for this. The same sentence went through two heads at the same moment, and neither of them showed it, and both of them knew anyway.

“You came.” He rose, and the corner of his mouth moved first. “Come and sit.”

She gathered herself, inclined her head the correct degree, and crossed the room to sit across the low table from him. Setting her bag down, she caught herself smoothing her skirt under her, and then caught herself having caught herself, which was worse.

“Sorry to have kept you waiting.” She looked up, the voice cool and even. “So. What am I calling you tonight? Seonsaengnim? Hoejang-nim? Hyeongbu—” a beat, one eyebrow’s worth of altitude, “—or ‘Song Ji-ho’?”

He reached for the decanter and began to pour for her, unhurried, the wine going down the side of the glass like silk off a table.

“Whichever you like.” He glanced up under the lamplight. “Call me whatever’s comfortable.”

Her fingertip tapped once, twice, on the table’s edge; her eyes made one slow circuit of his face, pricing it.

“Seonsaengnim~.” The word came out on a slide, head tipping, the face arranging itself into a portrait of deep academic deliberation. “Is probably the correct one. You are, after all, teaching me.” A pause with craft in it. “But it ages you so much. And later, if we’re actually running a scene opposite each other, I can’t very well act against my teacher, can I. The scene dies on the operating table.”

He said nothing. He let her work.

She met his eyes, and his gaze was still water: too still, the kind that makes a person want to drop a stone in it to see the rings, and she looked away first, to buy a second, and spent it on the glass.

“Hoejang-nim, then.” A beat; a small, unconvinced tilt. “Correct, and very proper, and—” the strand of hair by her cheek moved as she shook her head, a slow centimeter, “—no. It’s Friday night, and this isn’t LOEN. I don’t want to eat dinner across from a chairman. It’s exhausting just saying the word.”

His fingers came down once, twice, on the tabletop, a light, amused percussion.

She took a breath, and let it out, and gave him the middle word.

“Hyeongbu.”

The air in the room held still for exactly one second.

Im Yoon-a looked down at her glass, then up again, and something moved in her eyes that had currents under it: a joke with a stone in its shoe; a door opened one inch to see what stands behind it.

He didn’t read it. Nobody would have read it. It was one inch of a very well-built door.

“But I’m not eating a meal carrying that word, either. Not tonight.”

She looked up again, and the eyes had cleared, guileless as tap water.

“Song Ji-ho, then. That’s what I’ll call you.”

She sat back a degree; the shoulders came down off duty. “No title. No rank. Just — you. Tonight it’s two friends having dinner, some conversation, and somewhere in it, you teach me to act. All right?”

He laughed, once, out loud, actually out loud, and lifted his glass, unhurried, the light lying down along the gold rim of the wire frames.

“All right.”

The glasses met with a sound like a struck match.

The wine was a deep gem-red, and in the low light it had a nap to it, a sheen like velvet brushed one way.

She took a sip, and the first thing was silk: actual silk, chocolate gone liquid without any of chocolate’s noise, no bite, no burn. Then, arriving politely in single file: black cherry, blackcurrant, a thread of oak, vanilla somewhere in the weave. Not sweet. Long, and longer than long.

“Good wine.” She set the glass down, and the very tip of her tongue went, briefly, absently, to the corner of her mouth.

It was nothing. It was a movement with no intent in it whatsoever, which is what made it unbearable; and Song Ji-ho’s eyes stayed on the corner of that mouth for one full second longer than he had budgeted.

That should be regulated. There should be a government office.

He came back from wherever that was, raised an eyebrow, and put a thumb up.

“You can place that by taste? Remarkable, Im Yoon-a.”

“Xiao cai yi die.”

Clear Mandarin, syllable by careful syllable, learned, by the sound of it, from a screen, and worn at the edges with Seoul vowels.

“…And that’s how the phrase is used, is it.”

She looked at him and blinked. Once. Twice. The eyes were pond water, lit from underneath; clear, clean, innocent of everything, including the phrase itself, possibly including the concept of phrases.

And Song Ji-ho, a man surrounded at all times by every category of beautiful face the peninsula produces, a man with a working inventory, sat there for a second with the distinct sensation of having been taken out at the knees by something with no weight.

“It is,” he said. “That’s exactly how it’s used.”

He drank to cover the pause, and set the glass down with a click, and looked for higher ground.

“It’s usually jeans with you. I don’t see you in a skirt from one year’s end to the next. Did you dress up for tonight?”

Fourteen garments stood up in the back of her memory and demanded to be defended.

“Ani.” The smile was light, throwaway, seamless. “I just threw this on. It’s an acting lesson. It isn’t as if I’m meeting anybody who matters.”

Anybody who matters.

It went in like a splinter, a small one, clean, under the nail, and sat there.

He drank, and the wine went down better than the sentence did, and had, for the first time in his life, a taste he couldn’t name.

“And you?” A light came up under her eyes; the angle of the conversation turned over neatly in her hands. “I’ve rarely seen you go this formal. The last time was—” the strand of hair got tucked again, a pause dressed as memory, “—the day you were buying LOEN, I think. So. Are you meeting somebody who matters, tonight?”

She put her teeth lightly on the word matters, and held it a half-beat, and let it go.

He set the glass down and leaned back, one arm along the sofa’s back, all the leisure in the world arriving on him at once.

“Somebody who matters.” He looked at her, and let the amusement sit right out where she could see it. “You could put it that way, I suppose. Yes.”

Her eyes came up level, and there was a new thing in them: thin, bright, and sharpened on both edges.

“Then which number am I?”

His eyelids lowered a millimeter.

A probe. Which question is she actually asking: how many women I’ve seen this week, or how many I keep?

She didn’t leave him time to run both.

“Taeyeon eonni and Tiffany eonni have had schedules since eight this morning; I checked, mine was cancelled at noon, so I had the whole afternoon to check.” Even, pleasant, and each word arriving with its own small luggage. “And LOEN’s biggest story this month is a chairman who is directing the video for his own second album and sleeping in the edit suite. Every day. In that suit of chairs.” She lifted her glass and took a small sip, and her eyes never moved off him over the rim.

“So the number of people this suit gets worn for, in one week, must be short. Where on the list am I? What number?”

She set the glass down, and waited, with her hands folded like a bank teller.

Because the answer did the work, and she knew it. If she was incidental, dressed around rather than dressed for, then the evening had a floor under it, and she could stand on it and breathe. If he had dressed for her—

Her heart moved one beat ahead of the music.

Why did I ask that? Take it back. It doesn’t come back. Nothing about tonight comes back.

He looked at her, and his eyes were level water, and there was nothing in them to use.

Never rank your women in front of a woman — unless she is the first. Everything else you say after that is only the number, said out loud, by you.

He had dressed for her. That was the plain, inconvenient fact of the evening: the black suit was hers, and saying so was a hand of cards played face-up. And so he did not say so, and did not lie either, because there was a third road, and he took it at a stroll: the question turned neatly around, and handed back.

“Hm.” Mildly. Genuinely curious. “Why: in your eyes, am I somebody who matters to you?”

Im Yoon-a’s glass stopped for a half-second on its way to the table.

She didn’t fluster. She set the glass down with care, ran one fingertip once around the rim, one full circle, unhurried, a driver settling into a long race, and only then swallowed what was already in her mouth.

“Of course you are.” She looked up, eyes wide open, perfectly clear, absolutely level. “If I’m not somebody who matters to my eonnis’ man, then Taeyeon eonni and Tiffany eonni will be heartbroken. I couldn’t live with that.”

The two names went up between them like a shield with a family crest on it.

The amusement in his face came down one degree, a dimmer turned, precisely.

Those two names. There’s no clean way to catch a shield you’re the reason for.

He didn’t try. He leaned forward instead, and the distance across the low table folded itself in half, and the light off his glasses tilted.

“Since we’re on wardrobe.” The smile was set, easy, unreasonable. “How’s mine? Come on. Up close. Handsome, wouldn’t you say?”

It wasn’t a question; it was a doorway, with a light left on in it.

And her eyes went where eyes go. Brows like drawn lines. The nose with its clean, straight confidence. A face born expensive and raised expensively, polite over something not entirely polite; and the wire rims over it all, the frame around the picture: Sunday-school restraint on the outside of something that had never once been to Sunday school.

Her gaze stood on his face for one second. Two.

Then it stepped off, briskly, onto the wine.

Her heart arrived late, and loudly, like a train pulling into the station of her chest and blowing the whistle out of spite.

He didn’t miss it, didn’t miss any of it, and followed up at once, warm, patient, closing the door behind her: “Really quite something, isn’t it? Fit for your definition of handsome?”

She took a breath, took another, and got herself back on her own ground by main force.

Don’t walk where he points. He points at trapdoors. That’s all the pointing is.

Her eyes rolled up and away, and came back with the smile that has ended a thousand fan hearts on a thousand screens: the bright, deflecting one.

“Song Ji-ho-ssi. You already have Taeyeon eonni. You already have Tiffany eonni. Whether I find you handsome: does it still matter?”

And the shield went up again, smooth as a theater flat changing in the dark.

He laughed, out of pure appreciation.

All right. Now I see the shape of tonight. Every time the ground gets thin, out come the two names, planted like trees. As long as those two trees stand in front of her, she never has to say anything with her own mouth.

He stood up.

He came around the table unhurried, the heels of those mirror-bright shoes meeting the dark marble one after another, a slow, even percussion, and every stroke of it was a centimeter off her pulse rate.

He sat down beside her.

Close. Close enough that the sandalwood arrived, and under it a cold thread of wine on the breath.

Im Yoon-a shifted, reflexively, half a seat’s worth, and her eyes went to him sideways, wary as a cat in a carrier.

“Yoon-a-ya.” He turned, an arm along the sofa back, all the time in the world. “They tell me you’re the one in that group who’s close with everybody. Every member. No exceptions. Five years, nine years, whatever it is by now, and not one feud.”

“…Ne.” The voice had lost a layer of its polish. “Why?”

He moved in by one degree, mild as weather. “Then you’re close with Taeyeon. And with Tiffany. Close like sisters, yes?”

“Of— of course—”

She shifted again; the sofa’s arm arrived at her back, and that was the whole of the country left.

He was a fist’s width away now. His breath, when he spoke, was part of the same weather as hers.

“Tiffany knows Taeyeon is mine, and stayed. Taeyeon knows Tiffany is mine, and the two of them are excellent. Better than excellent; they went to Japan together last month and sent me photographs of the same dinner from two phones.” A pause, precisely long. “So tell me honestly, Yoon-a-ya. Do you think they would take you in?”

The air in the room stopped moving altogether.

“Mwo?”

She was up off the sofa before the word finished, too fast; her knee found the low table’s edge with a crack that would leave a mark she’d discover in the bath two days later, and she didn’t feel it now, because every alarm she owned was ringing at once.

She smoothed the hair back from her face. It had not moved. It did not need smoothing. Both hands did it anyway.

“I— I don’t know what you mean by that.”

And there it was, finally, out from behind the shield: Im Yoon-a, the composed one, the girl the whole country agreed was in charge of her own face, cornered in one sentence, red to the ears, holding her hair flat with two hands.

He smiled, slow, and reached for her glass. Hers: the one with the faint rose ghost of her mouth printed on the rim. He set his lips to that exact place on the crystal, and drank, his eyes on hers the whole way down.

The wine went down. Im Yoon-a’s face went up in flames, all at once, no reserve left to burn.

“Yoon-a-ya.” He set the glass down, gently, precisely. “If I set out to seduce you, do you think you’d fall?”

There was no composure left to lose, so she stood there without any, one hand on the sofa’s back, fingernails dug into the leather, her chest going like she’d taken the stairs.

The direct assault had taken all her formations apart. She reached for the last shield she had, and it came out in pieces.

“I— I’m the dongseng. Taeyeon eonni’s. Tiffany eonni’s. We’ve been — it’s nearly ten years—”

“And?”

He rose. He came on, one unhurried step at a time.

Im Yoon-a went back. One step. Two. Three. Her eyes were fixed on him the way the hare’s are on the stoat, the whole of her intelligence gathered in one point of attention, and her body already knowing the ending and running the other way anyway.

She wanted to bolt. Her legs had been nailed to the marble by something with a nail gun.

The sofa arrived at the backs of her calves.

She tipped, the gasp getting as far as her teeth, and an arm came around her waist like a custom fitting, and she was gathered, entirely, into warmth that went through two layers of cloth and made them a rumor.

“Even their dongseng — so what?”

He looked down at her from a height of one hundred and eighty-five centimeters, and the smile that came down to meet her was the wicked one, the one with no alibi in it.

“When have you ever seen a wolf leave a beauty standing in arm’s reach? Especially—” the arm tightened, gently, insultingly, “—one the entire nation has already voted on.”

The beautiful, shameless face, saying the unsayable in a voice you could have poured over ice. The gold wire rims catching one point of lamplight and wearing it like a monocle: respectability as a load-bearing costume, and nothing whatsoever respectable underneath the costume line.

Why is my heart doing this? Why does it feel like the top of a swing? Why are my knees filing complaints—

Her breath was coming fast; her chest was going; her hands were flat on his chest with the entire English and Korean vocabulary of no unavailable to them, and no strength in any of the fingers.

Push him off. Now. This second. Im Yoon-a — PUSH.

And she did: one great, graceless shove with everything the fear could lend her — and came free, staggering half a step, some of her hair down and most of her dignity with it.

“I— I have to go back.”

She turned. Her voice shook, and she walked for the door anyway, one step, two—

“Yoon-a.”

She stopped as if she’d walked into glass.

She stood with her back to him and did not turn around.

A threat? Say it. What walks out that door with me: the audition? The table? The four months? Writer Park’s script in my bag with the pages I’ve already creased?

Behind her, the voice changed costumes completely: light, easy, wounded for sport.

“It was a joke. Look at the state of you. One joke.”

Footsteps, unhurried. Hands arrived at her shoulders, and she was turned, gently, like a display item, and walked back to the sofa, and set down on it.

Then he crossed to his own seat, and sat, and the last two hours of geography were restored: one low table between them, and the whole width of it.

“Yoon-a-ya. It was a joke. Who runs from a joke?”

She sat with her eyes down and her hands in her lap and said nothing, and the inside of her was loud enough for the both of them.

A joke. A joke, this man says. A man who has had my measurements by the waist since a piggyback across a mountain stream says “a joke.” You marked me tonight like a page you intend to come back to. Don’t you dare say “joke.”

And the truth, which she had been keeping at arm’s length all evening, sat down beside her at last and introduced itself properly: this was the most dangerous man she had ever stood near.

She had experience. That was the thing people never believed about the face: the face got confessions the way statues get pigeons.

She had refused magnates, heirs, two chaebol second sons and one third, an actor with a shelf of awards, a ballad singer who cried, and a comedian who did not. And she had refused every one of them well. That was her craft, the thing she was actually proud of: nobody had ever left her angry. Yoon-a-ssi is such a good person. The timing was wrong. The company would never allow it. It isn’t you. Every door she had ever closed, she had closed so softly the man on the other side thanked her for the breeze.

This one would not be closed softly, and might not be closed at all.

Because against the list: handsomer than any man in her phone book, not by a little but by a systemic margin. Richer than any name she could think of and younger than he had any right to be and, she had overheard enough, through the dormitory’s entirely theoretical walls, of two eonnis’ late-night conversations, to know that in the one department no one ever discusses, the man was, in the eonnis’ own vocabulary, which had been shameless, a man among men.

If not for Taeyeon eonni. If not for Tiffany eonni.

Then he would already be mine, and I would already have introduced him to my father, and the paperwork could catch up at its own pace.

But there was no if not. That was the feature of the real world: no if not section.

So: hold the line, and become one more of his women? Her head shook, once, on its own.

Slap him and walk out? The head again, immediately, a small firm no: out the door with the slap would go the audition, the table, Writer Park, the four months, and a piece of the industry that she could not yet afford to have closed.

And… if I’m honest. If I’m truly honest, at the bottom of the bag, where I don’t look: I don’t want to cut the line either. So what is left, Im Yoon-a? He has decided he wants you, the whole of you. There is no version of tonight where you keep everything and lose nothing.

Choose, then. Choose with the list, like an adult.

She lifted her head, and looked at him, item by item, the way she’d been taught to read a contract: the face, the best she had ever seen, personally, in the flesh. The body, top of everything she had ever seen (and she had done a mountain commercial with him, so this was measured, not guessed. The ability, the talent: top of the industry, and everyone at the broadcasting stations knew it. The rest of the list, per the eonnis, per the shameless dormitory walls: peerless.

At this valuation… entering is no loss. Refusing: refusing is the only position that’s hard to hold, and he isn’t offering a third door.

She stood up.

She crossed the low table’s worth of distance, and stopped in front of him, and looked down at him for three whole seconds: a real three seconds, the audited kind.

And for the first time that evening, Song Ji-ho genuinely did not know what came next. (Which, as she would learn in the fullness of time, was the entire point of her.)

She bit her lip. She sat, sideways and neat, on his knee, and put her arms around his neck, and the apricot skirt arranged itself over the black wool suit like dawn getting organized over a night sky.

“Song Ji-ho.” The breath arrived at his ear warm and deliberate as a signature. “You meet my list. Every line of it. You overfill it. You are, exactly and precisely, the man I intended to date with marriage at the front of it. There is no one else on the peninsula who qualifies.”

“If you were not Taeyeon eonni’s man, I would not have let you walk past me. I want that on the record.”

“But.”

“I could give it to you.” Her voice stayed even; that was the astonishing thing, measured against the words it was carrying. “I’ve never had a boyfriend. If it were you — I would be willing.”

“And after tonight, you let me go. Please? Hmm?”

He looked down at the woman in his arms.

She meant it. That was the thing. There was no drama in her face and no bargaining in it: she had run the numbers the way she ran everything, and this was the answer, and she was holding it out to him flat-palmed, like a bank draft. Song Ji-ho could have Im Yoon-a tonight, the woman who defined the phrase “the face” for a generation, the nation’s template, the one with the fan sites in forty countries; could have her the way one takes a delivered package: entirely, tonight, with no resistance left in the wrapping.

And after?

And after, I own a beautiful vessel with nothing home. Do I lack for vessels? The peninsula runs a standing surplus of beautiful trainees; I could stand on the roof of the LOEN building with one arm raised, and by Thursday there would be a queue around the block with faces at her level and figures beyond it, and not one of them would be interesting for a month.

That’s the difference. The bad ones are boring by Christmas, and the pretty shells are boring by the second weekend. This one: the soul is a good one. Proud, decent, funny about it, and running the whole thing like an auditor. A soul this interesting, with its heart parked somewhere else… what is there to win? You can confiscate a body. You cannot confiscate a person.

He put his hands on her waist, and lifted her and set her on her feet, gently, like something borrowed.

And shook his head.

“Yoon-a-ya. The queue of women who want a place in that bed is long enough to see from the roof.”

“I don’t lack women.”

“You’ve priced yourself low — and me with me.”

He stood, and stepped around the low table, and walked for the door.

“Song Ji-ho!”

It cracked out of her, up an octave, all poisoned patience gone.

“It has to be this heart, then? The heart? Must Im Yoon-a fall out with Taeyeon eonni and Tiffany eonni — must Girls’ Generation come apart at the seams — for you?”

His stride caught. One step. He did not turn around.

“Yoon-a-ya.” His voice, when it came, was mild as milk, and twice as dangerous. “Did you know Sica is my girlfriend now, too?”

“Mwo?? Mworago??”
