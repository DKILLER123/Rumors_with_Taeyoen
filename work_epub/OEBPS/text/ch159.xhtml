Chapter One Hundred Fifty-Nine

Ji-ho, Just Bully Me Then

Sunday, January 13, 2013 Hannam — the new apartment roses, slippers, and samgyetang

With Lee Hyun-woo aboard, Dong-hwan’s load lightened visibly, and the whole operation ran smoother for it. Pre-production on The Girl in the Sun was in full churn — auditions being scheduled, invitations going out — while Ji-ho’s own days turned relatively unhurried: acting drills, script polish, the occasional obligation.

Sunday morning, he was up early. A scan of the news first — good; not a single hater in sight — then out the door in light disguise for supplies.

Roses, vivid enough to embarrass the florist’s other stock. A pair of house slippers in her size. And the full Joo-hyun snack registry: apple pie, crunch-pearl chocolate, cheese crackers, jelly cups, soft candy.

Utterly Unremarkable earned its keep again; he made it home unclocked. Then the television went on for company and the cleaning began — fresh bedsheets, a lit diffuser, every surface squared away.

“Today’s temperature: minus 3.4 degrees. Under the influence of a cold high-pressure system, moderate to heavy snow is expected by evening.”

He worked until ten, when the doorbell finally rang.

On the monitor: a small figure in a mask and baseball cap, a big grocery bag in hand, tipping her head back to blot sweat. Against the gray morning, the thick white puffer made her look like a little rice dumpling.

He was down the stairs in seconds.

She was laboring up the courtyard steps with the bag when the door swung open ahead of her. He took the bag out of her hands, then took the hand itself, and brought her inside.

“Slippers are here — change into these first.” He let go gently. “I’ll put the groceries in the kitchen.”

The house was deep-warm. She peeled off the puffer and gloves, stepped into the new slippers — her size, exactly — and padded into the living room.

Wide, precise, quietly expensive — the space suited him. The air held something clean and faintly sweet. And on the coffee table: a burst of roses in full argument with the winter outside, ringed by every snack she had ever mentioned liking. He had been preparing for this for days. The knowing of it landed somewhere soft.

While she stood there taking it in, he wrapped his arms around her from behind — and she yelped, spun, and delivered a flurry of fists that would not have inconvenienced a housecat.

Her cheeks were still stung red from the cold. He gathered both her hands into his, breathed warm air over them, and rubbed them slowly.

“You froze out there. This is why I said have the groceries delivered.”

“Ingredients have to be picked in PERSON.” She tugged her hands back a centimeter, entirely without conviction. “Let go. I’m cooking. Go entertain yourself.”

“No rush.” He drew her in instead, arms settling at her waist. “I missed you, nuna.”

The familiar scent of him closed over her. She looked up at the face at close range — bare-skinned and still unfair — and thought, with a small helpless swell of pride: this man is mine.

She rose onto her toes, eyes closing, breath warm against his mouth — and he stopped holding back.

A long moment later he let her go. Her eyes had gone bright and swimming, her face flushed rose.

He looked at her, liked what looking did to him, and pulled her back in for more.

“Are you planning to eat today or not?” she managed at last, pushing at his chest, laughing.

“Eating, eating. Let’s cook together.”

“YOU are not entering my kitchen. Go do your work.” She planted herself in the doorway.

In her parents’ house, her father had never once crossed a kitchen threshold — the architecture of it was load-bearing in her.

“Couples cook by availability,” he said, reasonable to a fault. “Whoever’s free cooks. Both free, cook together. Neither free, delivery.”

“No. Those hands are for holding a pen, not a spatula.” Immovable. “And anyway — this meal was always mine to make for you. It’s the only thing I CAN do for you right now.”

He looked at the set of her jaw, gave up, and ruffled her hair instead.

“Understood, silly. I’ll stay out of the way. Call if you need me.”

“Mm.” And the smile came out like weather clearing.

He leaned in the doorframe a while, watching her roll her sleeves and work — the knife sure, the sequence practiced. Not a beginner’s kitchen. Satisfied, he left her to it.

Girlfriend cooking; boyfriend not idle. He shut the study door and opened a document.

PRIVATE FILES · TRAINING PLANS IN DRAFT
01Bae Joo-hyun — Vocal & Acting Systematic Development
02Kim Taeyeon — Vocal Refinement (pre-solo)
03Son Seung-wan — Vocal Development

Source method: The Perfect Actor’s Encyclopedia + The Perfect Singer’s Encyclopedia — individualized regimens, real ceilings, honest timelines.

The two encyclopedias could read a person and write the training that fit them — sustained practice against those regimens produced real, measurable gains, up to each person’s natural ceiling. For Joo-hyun, the longer arc was already sketched: if the Shop ever cycled the Height-Reforging Milk again, 162 centimeters; meanwhile, craft — real vocal chops for a solo lane he’d write himself, real acting for scripts he’d build around her. A face like hers could carry a career alone. It shouldn’t have to.

Sulli he’d coach on set directly — no paperwork required. Seung-wan, dutiful little fan, got a plan of her own. Yoon-a’s gifts ran differently and would need hands-on teaching; Ji-yeon, someday, when the time was right.

He printed, bound, and filed the drafts, setting Joo-hyun’s and Seung-wan’s aside.

A loud gurgle from his own stomach broke the concentration. He laughed at himself and headed back out.

In the living room, Joo-hyun was crouched at the coffee table with a square of chocolate halfway to her mouth. She froze mid-bite.

“…What are you looking at?”

“Nuna. You look exactly like a hamster. It’s extremely cute.”

Her face outdid the roses. She shot upright and manufactured ferocity.

“This is YOUR fault!”

“How is this my fault?”

“If SOMEBODY hadn’t kept grabbing me and kissing me, would the samgyetang be running late? Would I be THIS hungry?”

“I refuse the charge.” He grinned. “You enjoyed every second of it.”

“Mwo?” Her eyes went slit-dangerous. “Say that again.”

In the interest of the evening’s remaining happiness, immediate surrender was indicated.

“I said: entirely my fault. Nuna is too beautiful, and my self-control is a ruin.”

As apologies went, it was indistinguishable from a love letter — which she noticed, and went pink about all over again. She grabbed two boxes of chocolate and pushed them into his arms.

“Here. Tide yourself over. The soup needs a while yet.”

He tossed the chocolate straight back onto the sofa without looking at it.

“You— what was that for?”

“The first meal nuna ever cooks for me — I’m coming to it hungry. I’m not blunting it with chocolate.”

She stared at him — this man and his catastrophic talent for saying the exact thing — and then she was on her toes with her arms around his neck, and the kiss was hers, given first.

Outside, unnoticed by either of them, the first heavy flakes had begun to fall — goose-feather snow, laying white over the whole hillside.

The afternoon folded them onto the sofa and slowed to nothing — closeness, warmth, the small private argument of who adored whom more, none of it hurried, none of it needing to go further to be everything. When she finally slipped free, cheeks blazing, the samgyetang was nearly ready and the whole apartment smelled like ginseng and home.

✦   ✦   ✦

She carried it all out herself — the samgyetang, rolled omelet, bibimbap — and they sat down across from each other.

He took the first spoonful of soup with ceremony and let it settle.

Hm. The broth’s a touch salty. And the chicken — the chicken has definitely been through some things. Overcooked. But this dish is hard to truly ruin, and the heart in it is not on trial.

He raised a thumb with total conviction.

“Nuna. This samgyetang is incredible. You can genuinely cook.”

“As long as you like it.” The smile bloomed. “Try the omelet.”

He did — and this one required no acting at all: fragrant, tender, exactly right.

“Mm. Delicious. Everything nuna makes is delicious.”

They ate slowly and talked quietly, and the meal ran long in the best way.

Afterward he gathered the dishes over her protests and stationed himself at the sink. She padded up behind him, slid her arms around his waist, and laid her cheek against his back.

An unnameable safety wrapped around her, dense and warm. A child learns its first happiness from its parents, she thought. And when the person you love does better than what you learned — the happiness doubles.

Dishes done, he laced his fingers through hers and led her back to the sofa. It was deep and soft and folded them toward each other; she tucked into the hollow of his shoulder, and his arm closed the circle around her.

He had the film queued — a love story. The opening music came up gentle, and he rested his chin lightly on her hair. Her snacks stood in formation on the table.

On screen, two leads ran laughing through rain. In the room, the world had narrowed to breath and warmth and the small sounds of two people existing next to each other. When she reached for a cheese cracker and he reached for a jelly cup, their fingers brushed; they looked at each other and laughed. Her cracker ended up in his mouth. His jelly ended up in hers.

Love stories have their heated scenes, and this one arrived on schedule — and somewhere in it, one of them turned, and then the other, and the movie went on without its audience for a while: the screen’s flicker reflected in his eyes, and beneath the flicker, only her, complete.

There may have been cars outside, and wind, and the hush of falling snow. Their world had shrunk to one sofa and one held person, and neither of them filed an objection.

We measure love with clocks and call it fast or slow — but when the heart moves, the second hand melts, and time is re-poured from scratch. Fast and slow are just the public’s rulers, laid against a private hour that belongs to two people alone.
