City lights coming up outside; he woke on the sofa and remembered the rewards he hadn’t finished opening. Mind down into the system; the blue screen only he could see:

THE SCOUNDREL’S FIRST KISS — each day’s first kiss lands like a live wire: flushed cheeks, racing pulse, a daze that lingers long after. Effective on partners. Daily reset.

Hm? WHO’S a scoundrel? The system was committing libel. …The skill did, however, look extremely useful. Delicious, frankly.

And then there was the necklace. He drew JADELIGHT OVER MOON out of inventory and turned it in his hand — vintage double-layer design, gold chain, one deep drop of green chrysoprase ringed in bright zircon. Made for exactly one throat.

The truth was, a worry had lived in him about Sulli for a long time. He’d once read an interview with an American psychiatrist — forty years of practice, and the honest count of patients truly CURED: none. The mild cases improved and lived; the harder ones held on by medication. The mind’s wounds rarely closed completely. The one reliable treatment was distance — from the environment that caused them — plus a new life with something to hold onto. Sulli had escaped the family that hurt her; she had him to hold onto now; but this industry’s pressure never stopped grinding. The necklace’s quiet field — a hand on her heart-rate whenever the spiral started — was insurance against the door he refused to ever let her walk through.

“Mmn~”

She stirred and opened sleep-fogged eyes — followed his gaze — and found the necklace.

Gold catching lamplight; the green stone deep as bottle-glass; zircon scattering tiny fires. No girl alive was constructed to refuse it.

“Wah — yeppuda.” Sleep evaporated on contact. “Oppa, this necklace… is it for me?”

“Mm.” He watched the pale line of her neck as he opened the clasp. “Let me put it on you.”

Full green stone, bright small fires, snow-field skin: the necklace had found its argument. He looked at the result with open satisfaction.

“Beautiful.”

She lifted the pendant, played it once through her fingers, and looked up under her lashes. “Me? Or the necklace?”

He caught her small hand and pressed it. “My Peaches, obviously. The necklace is borrowing your light — you’re what saves it from being ordinary.”

“You just know how to SAY things~”

Sugar-sated, she leaned up and kissed him —

— and the current hit. The day’s first kiss: a full-body shiver, cheeks flooding, head swimming sweetly. What was meant as a thank-you peck developed an unwillingness to end. THE SCOUNDREL’S FIRST KISS, performing dramatically above specification.

The evening went on from there as evenings between two twenty-year-olds in a brand-new home will — the necklace catching lamplight, the wind loud outside, the night uncommonly beautiful. It belongs to the two of them.

In planning manager Lee Hyun-woo’s office, a quieter campaign had been running for weeks.

Sharp nose, fast hands: he’d ring-fenced a promotion budget early, aimed squarely at the Western market — and skipped the traditional press-release pipeline entirely. Instead: YouTube. Music-recommendation channels with loyal followings, reaction creators, independent review channels — supplied with pristine audio and the official story-MVs, paid honestly, and invited to share their first reactions.

It began as scattered sparks. But the songs themselves were load-bearing: the theme’s pop instincts, the duet’s depth. The first Western listeners passed them on unprompted — and the internet did what the internet does to genuine word of mouth: multiplied it exponentially.

Mid-July, the detonation arrived from across the ocean.

Two OSTs from one Korean romance film, inside the Top 30 — on word of mouth and precision online promotion alone.

The news came home through the international wires and the music-finance press — and the country went up like dry grass.

The context made it seismic. K-pop’s best-ever Hot 100 result was PSY’s viral #2 with “Gangnam Style” the year before — a lightning strike, unrepeatable by design. And Girls’ Generation — the industry’s flagship — had spent SM’s fortune on a full American campaign for “The Boys”: television bookings, showcases, months of promotion — to brush the chart’s floor at #100, itself hailed then as the idol industry’s breakthrough. Now two soundtrack cuts from a domestic romance film, with no offline campaign at all, sat inside the top thirty. It read like a fairy tale with receipts.

And the peninsula’s fierce collective pride did the rest of the work. The writer was Chinese; the theme’s singer was American-born. But Tiffany was Korean-American, and Taeyeon was as Korean as the flag itself, the core of the national girl group — so the verdict came down instantly and unanimously: these songs are OURS.

National pride became a box-office engine of its own: people with no interest in romance films began buying tickets to “support the work that raised our flag.” The film’s plateauing curve bent UPWARD mid-run — weekday occupancy rising instead of falling, sold-out boards reappearing across the chains.

And for the first time, the name Ji-ho left the peninsula’s borders under its own power — no longer just “the Chinese genius producer” or “the dark-horse actor” of the domestic industry, but a musician certified by the world’s main market. Foreign outlets started asking who he was, and what film these songs came from.

The shockwave rolled back into China too — pride, adoption, hot searches, forum wars over whose he “really” was — and millions of new listeners went looking for the two songs that had “raised the flag,” whichever flag they meant.

Pulled by the global draft, both songs bloomed everywhere at once: “Love Me Like You Do” into the top three in over thirty countries and regions; “Shallow” into the top ten in a dozen more. From domestic smash, to Asian phenomenon, to global breakout — the triple jump, complete.

This summer he had set the peninsula alight. Now the fire had crossed the water — and the ground it cleared was exactly where the next album would be planted.
