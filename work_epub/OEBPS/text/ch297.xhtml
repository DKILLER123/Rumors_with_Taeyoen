Chapter Two Hundred Ninety-Seven

Upheaval

Friday · 17:52 → 22:41 a military base gate an hour north of the capital · a bar that never stopped being a rehearsal room · the chairman’s floor at LOEN · the T-ara dorm, one light short of full one anniversary armed with wine and lace, one summons that outranked it, one man left at a gate with a duffel and a dial tone, one band with a cure for heartbreak that is worse than heartbreak, one girl on a floor she’d never seen, and five women learning the same doctrine at the same moment: play dumb, take no sides

Outside the base, the autumn evening was doing its conscientious best, and Park So-yeon was dressed for a different agenda entirely.

She leaned against the car door in a beige trench coat with the belt knotted loose, so that when she walked, the front opened a negotiation’s width and offered, briefly, the black lace underneath. She had checked the effect four times in the elevator of her building. She checked a fifth time now, sideways, in the car window, and then repaired her lip gloss in the rearview mirror with the small absorbed smile of a woman whose evening is already won on paper.

Memory · the long way from fan to girlfriend

The man inside that gate had been her bias since she was a schoolgirl with a piggy bank. Ten years old, twenty months of saved allowance, and a sofa: picked out alone, paid for alone, delivered to a dormitory of boys she had never met, because that was simply the level of commitment the times demanded. Years later, when some variety show unearthed the story for a laugh, he had smiled like a man remembering weather, and said he still knew that sofa: ugly as sin, comfortable as a Sunday.

They met properly at the end of 2010, on an MBC variety set, Flower Bouquet, cameras everywhere, her heart doing paperwork in the background. A beautiful girl pursuing a man has only a gauze curtain between herself and victory. By December 31, 2010, the curtain was gone: it was official, between the two of them, a relationship.

What followed was the schedule from hell. T-ara at full altitude, and a couple who met in parked cars, in gaps of minutes, finishing sentences through half-open windows. In April he enlisted. Two years. One visit. And now, today, the gate was going to give him back; and tomorrow… tomorrow was the thousandth day.

Tonight she had a bottle of red breathing at home, and under this trench coat, the set she never wore, the one bought for exactly one category of occasion. A thousand days deserved both.

What she had not noticed, two rows back, was the black sedan with the limousine tint, and the two men inside it with their lenses already warm.

“What is the HOLD-UP—” one of them hissed, chewing a dead cigarette. “The ceremony ended forty minutes ago. They do paperwork, they don’t do sculpture.”

Her own patience was thinning along similar lines. She checked the time, checked the gate, checked the time. Past the ceremony hour, well past it, and no duffel, no buzz cut, no him.

Her phone rang. The screen offered Eun-jung — and the voice that came through had none of its usual porch-light warmth in it. Flat, fast, the voice of a person carrying something heavy upstairs.

INCOMING CALL · HAHM EUN-JUNG · 18:09

“Yeoboseyo.”

“Soyeon-ah. Where are you?”

“…Why, what’s happened?”

She was not going to say outside my boyfriend’s army base in lingerie.

“Wherever you are, come back to the dorm. Now. It’s enormous.”

The command tone landed wrong, the way command tones always land on a woman dressed for a man’s homecoming.

“Since when does the leader get to summon me like staff?”

“NOW you’re keeping score? The company’s been bought. Bought, Soyeon-ah. Nobody knows anything and everything’s on fire. If your career isn’t your problem, then stay wherever you are.”

Dead line. She hung up first.

“Mwo—? Bought? Bought by WHO? Not the president—”

The screen had already gone black. She stared at it, lip caught between her teeth, thoughts piling up like cars at a broken signal.

Not the president buying, then. Someone buying US. She ran the facts she had, which were no facts. I came into this company from the outside, never one of the president’s founding daughters, and every resource I ever got arrived half a step behind the others’. It took years to get mine. Then the scandal took the years back. And now, with the wound still open, the ground itself is being sold out from under the building.

If I’m slow THIS time, if I’m standing at a gate while everyone else is in the room where it’s being decided—

On one side of the scale: a man she had waited two years and one full day for, currently forty minutes late through a gate.

On the other: whatever was going to decide the next three years of her life, currently happening without her.

Her knuckles went pale around the phone. She gave it three seconds.

Then she bit down, swung the door open, dropped into the driver’s seat, and turned the car around with two clean motions. The chance could not be missed. The boyfriend would have to be explained to later. He’d understand… wouldn’t he? If he loved her, he’d understand.

In the sedan, journalism suffered a small stroke.

“Mwoya. She’s LEAVING?”

“The discharge-day exclusive, the thousand-day couple… she’s leaving BEFORE HE COMES OUT—”

“Aish. Follow the car!”

✦ ✦ ✦

The gate had no opinions. It let Oh Jong-hyuk out at a stroll, duffel over one shoulder, two years of posture walking off him in real time, a smile already spending itself on the search: the familiar car, the familiar face, the photo the fan in him had waited a thousand days for someone to take of the two of them.

Girls with flowers ran past him to other men. Parents aimed their sons at cameras like trophies. The stream of reunions thickened, and thinned, and kept not containing her.

The smile came down a degree at a time, like a dimmer.

He stood where the pavement gave up on ceremony and waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. The sun crossed the top of the sky’s exit and began its own discharge in the west; the gate crowd went from festive to stragglers; and his face settled into the flat, patient look of a man being educated.

He called her.

OUTGOING CALL · PARK SO-YEON · 18:31

“Yeoboseyo.”

Road noise behind her voice, hurried, running from something.

“Where are you?”

“Jong-hyuk oppa. Miane. Something exploded at the company, I have to go handle—”

“What thing? Name the thing that outranks your boyfriend’s discharge.”

The volume stayed low; the pressure underneath it didn’t.

“It’s not— oppa, listen for one second—”

“I’ve listened for two years.”

A breath. Then the lid came off.

“Park So-yeon. I got out TODAY. Today! You promised me the gate. You PROMISED—”

“Oppa, I didn’t plan this, it’s the COMPANY, I—”

“Enough.”

The line died mid-syllable.

He stood there with the phone at his ear a moment longer, listening to nothing at military volume, the tendons standing out along the hand strangling the duffel strap.

In the car going the other way, Park So-yeon lowered the dead phone and did not feel like the winner of anything.

During the scandal, how many nights did I surface out of a nightmare already crying? Where was he? Sixty kilometers and one army regulation away, rationed to phone calls that weren’t allowed to be long. The one season I was drowning, he couldn’t hold my hand. And the one evening he needed the gate, I can’t give it to him. That’s just the arithmetic of two people on two schedules. It isn’t a crime.

But the arithmetic had a remainder, and the remainder was hers: she had promised the gate. Tonight of all nights, the promise had been the plan.

She dropped the phone onto the passenger seat like something that had bitten her, and drove on toward the dorm. Apologize tomorrow. Tomorrow was the thousandth day; there was still a whole anniversary to spend on making it right. She pressed the accelerator a little, as if distance were the debt.

✦ ✦ ✦

A bar in Seoul, the kind with booths like bunkers and a whiskey list nobody read. Oh Jong-hyuk sat behind a firing line of soju bottles, and Click-B closed ranks around him.

“Jong-hyuk-ah. Welcome back to the human race.”

“Two years. TWO. We aged, did we age? Drink.”

“First one goes to freedom—”

He manufactured a smile, got the glass to his mouth, and lost the drink somewhere behind his teeth.

“What’s this face?” The vocalist, Kim Tae-hyung, read him the way only a man who has shared a van for fifteen years can. “You come out of the army after two years looking like you left something in there?”

“Don’t ask,” murmured the bassist, Woo Yeon-jun, under the table’s noise. “Word is Soyeon-ssi never showed at the gate.”

“Mwo?” Kim Tae-hyung’s brow folded. “How does that happen?”

“Company business,” Oh Jong-hyuk said finally, to his glass.

“Company business.” Woo Yeon-jun repeated it with the contempt reserved for ex-girlfriends’ employers. “Three years you’ve given this thing. And company business outranks the gate?”

“Tomorrow it’s a thousand days.” Another glass went the way of the first.

“A THOUSAND DAYS and she doesn’t come?” The vocalist shook his head slowly, a man mourning civilization. “That woman is getting a little too impressed with herself.”

“Right,” said the drummer, Yoo Ho-seok, landing the plane. “Are you her boyfriend, or her fan? Click-B, hyung. CLICK-B. When she was ten she bought us furniture. She wasn’t too busy THEN.”

“Enough, enough.” Oh Jong-hyuk waved it down. “Drop it.”

But brothers do not drop things. Click-B was a rock band, and the whole religion of rock bands is that a brother’s bad night is a congregational emergency. The table leaned in, the soju multiplied, and the diagnosis got louder and less careful.

“DRINKS!” Kim Tae-hyung’s palm hit the table hard enough to relocate the bottles. “Nobody finds the door tonight. Not standing, anyway!”

“Right—” Woo Yeon-jun was already signaling the far wall like a man raising a drawbridge. “Let’s get some people over here. A welcome-home needs a CROWD.”

The crowd arrived on cue: several very tall women with immaculate makeup and studio smiles, threading between tables like intent.

“Jong-hyuk oppa~ welcome back from the army~”

One of them, long-haired, sat directly beside him without any visible negotiation, filled a glass, and held it up to his lips with both hands, smiling like a promotion.

He looked at the strange face close to his, and somewhere behind his eyes, for one frame, Park So-yeon checked her lip gloss in a rearview mirror.

One frame.

He took the glass and emptied it. The model’s smile sweetened by a whole degree; her arm arrived on his shoulder with the naturalness of a coat. He did not remove it.

Round followed round. The music went up, the laughter went up, a photographer’s flash went off (someone was always taking pictures in booths like this), and the man in the middle of it smiled and drank and held what was put near him, while the small guilty candle in his chest guttered, drowned, and went out entirely.

✦ ✦ ✦

At LOEN, on the floor the elevators only admit by intent, Chae Soo-bin held the chairman’s office door open and stepped out of the way.

“Jiyeon-ah. After you.”

Park Ji-yeon stood at the threshold and took one breath, the kind you take before an audition you already know you’ve won.

She had been to LOEN dozens of times. Never this floor. The corridor ran quiet as a museum after hours, all black glass and city light, Seoul laid out below in its working jewelry. And she had dressed for the altitude.

The ensemble is one long sentence about firsts: first time on his floor, first night the company changed hands, first date stamped by a trading halt. Deployed by a woman who checked herself in the corridor glass and found the reflection pictorial-grade, which was the plan, because the audience is one man and he reads everything.
Bought and assembled Friday afternoon, between a floor-roll of joy and a wardrobe avalanche, on the instruction that the evening’s dress code was “that set, tonight.” Sheer obedience was never this well dressed.

Park Ji-yeon · LOEN tower, the chairman’s corridor, 20:12

She pushed the door with two fingers.

He was standing at the glass wall with his back to her, on the phone, one hand in his pocket, the city doing its spangled background work behind him.

OUTGOING CALL · UNLISTED · 20:09

“No. The notice stands as issued.”

The other end, a murmur the glass does not pass on.

“Then say it plainly: before the open tomorrow, the procedure is reconfirmed with the exchange, line by line.”

Again the murmur, longer this time.

“And the investor line is staffed from eight. They read from the notice. Nobody improvises.”

A pause, and somewhere a page turning.

“Yes. That’s all.”

The voice was pitched lower than she had ever heard it, each word set down with weights on it, an authority with no performance in it whatsoever: the voice of the man who had bought a company before dinner.

Park Ji-yeon stopped where she stood, hand still on the door.

Is that… mine? The one who laughs at my terrible jokes, who teases me until I’m horizontal on his office couch, that one is gentle, that one is MINE. And this one, at the window, issuing instructions like weather—

The word arrived before she could card it: handsome. Her heart dropped a beat somewhere in the machinery and kept going without it.

He put the phone away and turned. The executive weather left his face in half a second, the way a front clears off a coastline.

“You’re here.” Softer now, with the smile arriving under the words.

She didn’t answer. She looked. The deep grey shirt with the sleeves rolled to the forearm, the wrists clean and exact; the collar open two buttons, giving up only the idea of a collarbone; the city’s light laying itself along the side of his face like it was on the payroll. The whole arrangement, she decided, was an act of outright provocation.

Some men are sewn into dragon robes and never look like the prince. Some stand in a grey shirt at a window they own, and you know the summit was poured for their feet.

Pride came up her throat like warm water, and the corners of her mouth rose without asking.

This is my man. That window is his window. That company is his company. And he told me to wear the set tonight.

“What are you looking at?” He came toward her, unhurried.

She let him arrive. And at the exact inch of arrival she went up on her toes, both arms around his neck, and kissed him first: no approach, no runway, just liftoff.

He took one half-second to catch up, and then his arm was around her waist and the kiss had two signatures on it. The cherry taste of the new tint opened against his mouth, sweet and cool at once; through the thin dress his hand could find the warmth of her side without any guessing.

She stayed in it until her lungs filed a complaint. They surfaced together, foreheads touching, breathing out of order.

“Missed me that much.” Low, amused, against her hair.

“Mm.” She nodded, eyes lit like a plugged-in sign. “To death.”

He bent and took one more small kiss, like a stamp on a document.

“I missed you too.”

She laughed and put her face into his chest, and the next part came out muffled and honest.

“Oppa. You were so handsome just now.”

“And on other days?”

“Other days too. But today was different.” She tipped her head back to look at him properly. “Today you were like… like Hoejang-nim.”

He laughed outright and pinched her cheek, gently, the way you test a peach.

“And which one do you like?”

She gave the question the full weight of her consideration, eyes narrowed, national-business face.

“Both.” Verdict, no appeal. “The gentle one is mine. And the mighty one is also mine.”

The look he gave her then had entire seasons in it, and the office held nothing for a while but the two of them breathing in alternation.

Outside the door, Chae Soo-bin eased it shut on the last half-inch, and went to be busy somewhere very far away.

✦ ✦ ✦

At the dorm, Park So-yeon parked, cut the engine, and then sat in the dark of the car the way people sit when the sitting is the decision.

The phone in her hand showed his name. Call again? He was at the boiling point; anything she said now would arrive pre-burned. Tomorrow. Tomorrow she would do the anniversary properly, the wine, the dinner, the surprise, and the evening would fold tonight’s failure into a story they’d tell for years. Remember when I stood you up at the gate? They’d laugh eventually. Couples bank on eventually.

She got out, crossed to the lobby, and had just stepped into the elevator when the phone buzzed: a message from Eun-jung. Where are you. The whole company is upside down and everyone is waiting on you.

She sighed, thumbed the screen dark, and let the elevator lift her.

What she did not know, at that moment, from inside that rising box of light: in a bunker booth across the city, the man with tomorrow’s name on it was holding court with a model against each shoulder, being photographed by someone’s flash, and smiling, brighter (said the booth) than anyone.

The dorm door opened on gravity. The members were arranged across the sofas like a board meeting of the bereaved, faces set to solemn, and the television wasn’t on, which in this dormitory was a five-alarm indicator.

Park So-yeon counted heads with her coat still on. One short.

“Where’s Jiyeon?”

“You haven’t looked at your phone once tonight, have you.” Park Hyomin raised her head from the sofa arm, and her voice had the flattened calm of a person who has been rebooted. “The buyer. The one who’s taking CCM. It’s LOEN.”

“It’s Jiyeon’s boyfriend.”

The floor of Park So-yeon’s understanding opened, briefly, onto empty air. Song Ji-ho. The boy who wrote songs. The one from the lessons, the scandals, the cover stories. He had bought the company, HER company, between the announcement and the close of trading, on the same Friday her man walked out of a gate she wasn’t standing at.

“The president telephoned me directly.” Hahm Eun-jung was already standing, jacket in hand. “He wants us at the company. Now, tonight. Let’s go.”

“Wait.” Qri’s hand closed on Eun-jung’s sleeve, quiet as a bookmark. “Eun-jung-ah. Did he say what for?”

“No. Nothing. Just… come in.”

Qri’s whole body performed one long, invisible sigh. A leader, at a hinge of exactly this size, should have a read on the room; and hers was standing there holding her jacket like a coat-check ticket.

“Eun-jung-ah. Think it through with me for one minute.” She kept her voice at corridor volume. “That company, for fifteen years it’s been the president’s whole biography. This past year he’s been fighting to keep his hands on the wheel: acquisitions, mergers, whatever the papers were calling them. And tonight the company itself was bought out from under him, by a boy young enough to audition for him. You think a man like that swallows that in one breath?”

“…So?” Eun-jung’s eyes were somewhere between the ceiling and the floor.

“So. When we walk in there, whatever he says, grief, fury, poetry, a plan, we are idiots. Complete idiots. We don’t understand business. We’ve never understood business.”

“…Idiots.”

“Certified. We’re artists; the operations of the company are weather that happens to other people.” A beat, and she laid the second card down flat. “And think of the arithmetic. With Jiyeon in his pocket, Song Ji-ho isn’t going to let T-ara come to harm, whatever the president tells himself tonight. But the president fed us for years too, and decently, whatever else he is. So we take nobody’s side. We do the work of artists, we keep the mouths of artists, and we let the men with the money fight over the flag.”

The understanding arrived in Hahm Eun-jung’s face the way light arrives in a hallway: one switch, all at once. Don’t choose. Nod at everything. Survive the transfer.

And still she stood there a moment longer, jacket over her arm, not moving. She had come up inside this company, a founding daughter in the way that mattered (her trainee years, her debut, every January contract, all of it inside these walls), and the resources had never once been shorted. The doctrine was sound. The doctrine was also asking her to stand in front of the only father the company had and play the fool.

She looked at Qri. Qri looked back, unhurried, already wearing tomorrow’s face.

“…Then we go as artists.” Eun-jung put her arms through her jacket, one side at a time, like a woman dressing for weather she had no forecast for. “Boram-ah, Ah-reum-ah. Coats. We’re expected.”

They went down in a body, five women in evening coats, into the parking level and the night beyond it, toward a company with a sold sign on it, a president with a grievance in him, and a morning that already had the printers warming up.
