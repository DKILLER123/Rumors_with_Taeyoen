Chapter One Hundred Sixty-Four

Casting Confirmed

Late January 2013 SM practice rooms → LOEN — the audition hall breath control, auditions, and one white cat

The SM practice room burned bright against the late hour.

“Ah— oh— ee—”

“Breathe — hold — control—”

Joo-hyun held Seung-wan’s hands as the two voices rose and fell through the empty room — one demonstration smooth and endless, one attempt visibly running out of runway.

“Ha— ha— kh— khk!”

Seung-wan doubled over coughing, face red from oxygen debt, wiping tears from the corner of one eye.

“Eonni, does this method actually WORK? My lungs are filing a complaint.”

Joo-hyun — fresh off a flawless round, breathing only slightly quick — rapped her knuckles gently on Seung-wan’s forehead.

“Of course it works. Isn’t this the practice plan a certain Ji-ho wrote out SPECIFICALLY for you — the one you never stop talking about? Doubting it now?”

“Appa~” She retreated, clutching her forehead. “Ani, I’m not doubting HIM — it’s just this method is exhausting. Worse than the vocal coach’s.”

“Seung-wan-ah.” The teasing went out of Joo-hyun’s face. “He used to say: effort gets you to passable. Only going all-out gets you to excellent. You’re at exactly the age for all-out. Stop counting the tiredness.”

“Mama Bae is stricter than the company…” It came out at mutter-volume.

“Hm?” The eyes narrowed with dangerous pleasantness. “What was that?”

“Nothing! Nothing at all!” The ingratiating smile deployed instantly; an arm looped through Joo-hyun’s. “I SAID, eonni’s gotten even prettier lately. Your skin is literally glowing.”

“At least the mouth works.” Joo-hyun’s mood curved visibly upward. “Come on. Practice break — I’ll treat you to green tea.”

“WAH! Thank you eonni!” A beat. “…Wait. Isn’t the company’s green tea free?”

“I’m brewing it for you. That doesn’t count as treating?”

Seung-wan wilted, nodded meekly — it counts, it definitely counts — then rallied for one more attempt at freedom.

“But eonni, if I drink tea now I’ll never sleep—”

“Then we practice another hour and go home at eleven.”

“Ah~ NO~”

Dragged corridor-ward in a state of theatrical despair, Seung-wan managed one last mutter near the tea room.

“Honestly, eonni’s turning INTO Ji-ho lately. Same bullying energy.”

“What did you say?”

She was already running, laughing over her shoulder.

“Look at yourself! You quote him constantly! You’re even starting to TALK like him — the whole style!”

Joo-hyun’s cheeks went warm as she gave chase. Could it be helped? Two people sharing a pillow trade habits without noticing. Carry someone in your heart, and he leaks into your sentences.

“Don’t you RUN!”

Two silhouettes chased laughter down the corridor of the sleeping building.

✦   ✦   ✦
January 25 · Morning LOEN — the grand conference room The Girl in the Sun — casting day

The twenty-fifth broke clear and warmer, and LOEN’s grand conference room had been converted overnight into an audition hall.

The judging table, in order: producer Shin Min-hyuk; director Kwak Jae-yong; screenwriter Ji-ho; CJ Entertainment’s project lead Lee Yong-hwa; and — seated at the end with the special status of confirmed female lead — Sulli. The executives were there for process; the actual casting authority sat with the writer and the director.

Kwak Jae-yong’s name carried its own weight: the fantasist of the romantic comedy, the man who folded dreams and time-slips into fine-grained feeling — My Sassy Girl, The Classic, Daisy. A pure-love fantasy like The Girl in the Sun sat squarely in his wheelhouse; he’d read the script once and said yes.

And in this industry, the screenwriter’s chair carried authority that sometimes outranked the director’s — casting rights, script control, held by whoever originated the work, regardless of age. The top drama writers chose their own directors, casts, and budgets outright. The system protected the words. Today, the words belonged to a twenty-year-old.

At nine sharp, it began.

“First auditionee — Chae Soo-bin, reading for Choi Yoon-ju.”

The door opened on a girl in a simple white sweater and full skirt. She bowed to the table — and when her eyes crossed his, the polite smile flickered with something complicated.

“Hello, Writer Song, Director Kwak, honored judges. I’m Chae Soo-bin, auditioning for Choi Yoon-ju.”

He blinked. The girl he’d known as neighbor Bae Soo-bin — now under a stage name. And visibly unable to hold his gaze.

“Soo-bin — you?”

The awkward smile told the whole story: her agency had learned she’d once been his housemate and slotted her in, betting the writer would look after his own. She’d read the intent instantly — and hated it. Her parents had raised her better than that. She wanted the part on merit or not at all; unable to refuse the company outright, she’d planned to walk through the motions and out.

“Ji-ho — you KNOW this… Soo-bin-ssi?” Shin Min-hyuk was scanning the file.

“Ne, bonbujang-nim. You may remember — when I first joined, I asked the company’s help with a lawsuit. She was the bystander who got hurt in that incident. My housemate at the old building.”

“Ahh — HER. I remember.”

“Right.” He turned back to her, even and unreadable. “Soo-bin-ah. Please begin.”

She’d chosen the scene where Choi Yoon-ju, having caught her brother’s hidden feelings, needles him about it half-jokingly on the trip to Go-i’s hometown. And she was sleepwalking it. Distracted. Deliberately unremarkable. He watched for ten seconds, put the pieces together — the embarrassment at the door, the throwaway effort — and understood exactly what she was doing.

“Hold on.”

She stopped, startled, and found him looking straight at her.

“Soo-bin-ah. Do you remember telling me your dream was acting?”

“…Ne. It is.”

“Then leave everything else outside the room.” His voice stayed gentle and carried anyway. “Owe it to the version of you that got up before dawn for years. Now — again, please.”

The words went through her like current. He’d seen it — seen the surrender in her, and cut it off before it could finish. Chae Soo-bin, she told herself. You defied your parents. You came to Seoul alone. You played PROPS in stage plays and kept getting up in the dark. Was all of that for a deliberately failed audition? If she flopped here, with their history public knowledge — wouldn’t THAT breed uglier rumors than trying? She took one long breath and did it properly.

The performance was green — but stage-trained green, with real bones under it. And Choi Yoon-ju didn’t demand virtuosity. When she finished, Kwak Jae-yong stroked his chin, thinking hard. The craft cleared the bar easily. The problem was the face: too beautiful for a supporting role in the heroine’s orbit. Not prettier than the lead — that wasn’t the issue — but two faces at that level in one frame and the camera stops knowing where to look. Fatal, in a pure-love film. Still… the writer clearly knew her, and Kwak liked this script and its author enough to solve the problem instead of discarding it. The hero’s company had a female-staffer role — Choi Seung-ah — zero shared frames with the lead. He leaned over and murmured; the writer nodded along.

“Soo-bin-ssi — try this scene instead.” An assistant carried the new pages over.

A role switch mid-audition — routine for the industry, bewildering for a rookie holding unfamiliar pages.

“Soo-bin-ssi, take some time outside with the script first,” Kwak said kindly, old hand smoothing new nerves.

“Next — Lee Kwang-soo, reading for team leader Park Jung-soo.”

The next auditionee ducked slightly through the doorframe on pure reflex, and the room relaxed by involuntary degrees. Some people are funny standing still.

“Hello, everyone — Lee Kwang-soo.” The bow was deep and earnest; the grin that followed found the writer’s seat. “Writer Song. We meet again.”

“Kwang-soo-ssi. Please begin.”

His scene: the team leader mid-crunch, catching his subordinate Seo Yeon-woo’s first collision with Go-i in the office. Tired eyes, warm center — and the acting was genuinely good, a different species entirely from his variety persona.

“Strong work. One concern,” Kwak said directly. “Park Jung-soo is written mid-thirties. You’re twenty-seven. Too young for it?”

“Director-nim, age is a makeup problem.” The comeback was ready. “Also—” the grin swung toward the writer’s chair, “—standing next to Writer Song, I look VERY mature.”

The table laughed.

“I played above my age in Dong Yi and the response was good. And Park Jung-soo isn’t just Yeon-woo’s boss — he’s a guide figure. My height opposite Writer Song reads naturally as a protective senior in the frame.”

“Kwang-soo hyung is saying I look like I need protecting?”

“ANI, ani!” Both hands up. “Compositionally! A TALLER sunbae beside you serves the relationship! Purely geometry!”

Kwak was visibly sold; he turned to the writer, who gave it straight.

“His comic rhythm balances the light humor your films run on, Director. It fits.”

“Next — Lee Min-ki, reading for Choi Yoon-hyuk.”

A different temperature walked in — still, literary, self-contained. His chosen scene had no lines at all: Choi Yoon-hyuk keeping silent company beside the person he loves without saying so, everything carried in the eyes and the shoulders. When it ended, the room stayed quiet an extra beat.

“Excellent.” Kwak clapped first. “The gaze work — restrained, deep. That’s the whole character, and you played it without a word.”

“Thank you, Director.”

“I’ve seen your Haeundae,” the writer said, flipping the file. “Very different register from today. How did you recalibrate?”

“Every role has its own nature,” Lee Min-ki answered evenly. “Yoon-hyuk’s love is silent — an undertow. I didn’t recalibrate much. This is roughly who I am. I can play it as myself.”

Writer and director exchanged one glance: second male lead, found.

The day ran on rails. A few unremarkable readings; one actress whose Choi Yoon-ju arrived several sizes too large — “I’m sorry; your instrument suits sitcoms better,” Kwak said, not unkindly, and she left pale and fast. The afternoon settled the supporting board: Go-i’s parents went to two veterans, Kim Hae-sook and Sung Dong-il, who needed one short scene together to produce thirty years of marriage and a bottomless love for a daughter. The mysterious Samshin-grandmother figure went, by unanimous instinct, to Kim Young-ok — the nation’s grandmother since her thirties. And Seo Yeon-woo’s younger brother went to a newcomer, Park Gun-woo, whose audition nerves happened to be the character.

One principal remains uncast — and the director is about to say the word “cat.”

“Your read?” Kwak asked when the last chair emptied.

“The principals all fit. Kwang-soo hyung brightens the palette; Lee Min-ki-ssi gives the second lead real gravity.”

Shin Min-hyuk traded a look with Lee Yong-hwa.

“Then we lock it today. Contracts within the week.”

“Wait.” Kwak raised a hand. “One more principal. The cat.”

A full second of table-wide blankness.

“The kitten, Director?”

“The kitten runs through the entire film — it’s the load-bearing image. We need an animal actor with genuine presence.”

Casting humans was procedure. Casting a cat was a research project. The silence stretched — until the end of the table spoke up.

“I know a place.” Sulli sat forward. “It might have exactly the cat we need.”

✦   ✦   ✦
Days later A shelter on Seoul’s outskirts the last principal signs

The animal shelter sat quiet under leftover snow, a dozen cats arranged in various states of dignity behind the glass.

“This one came in about a month ago,” the staffer said warmly. “Unusually clever — honestly, sometimes it seems to understand speech.”

A pure white kitten, fluff not yet grown out, with eyes of a rare amber. It sat collected as a small monk, ran a careful survey of the assembled humans — and settled on Sulli.

“It likes you,” the writer observed.

She approached by centimeters, hand out, hesitating — and the kitten closed the distance itself, tipping its head up to rub its cheek along her fingertips.

“That’s the one.” Kwak’s verdict was instant.

The adoption paperwork went through the same afternoon — adoption, not rental; she’d decided that somewhere between the first head-rub and the second. On the drive back, the kitten purred in its carrier while she hung over it, besotted. She had always wanted a cat.

“Name it,” came the voice from the front seat, eyes finding hers in the mirror. “In the film it’s ‘Peach.’ Off camera it can be anyone.”

She stroked the white fur, thinking it over properly.

“‘Lucky.’ It was lucky to meet us — and I want it to bring the film luck too.”

The way meeting oppa was lucky. For me.

“Lucky.” The smile was audible. “Good name.”

The car ran toward the city through an orange-burning dusk. From the passenger seat, Kwak turned around to watch the girl murmuring to the carrier — “Lucky-yah, we’re making a movie together” — and the kitten answering with one considered mew.

“I have the final shot already,” Kwak said. “Cherry blossom season. Seo Yeon-woo on a bench. The kitten appears again — and leads him back to Go-i.”

“The new cycle begins.”

“A cat gets a sixth of a human’s years,” Kwak said, settling back, “but nine chances to spend them beside the one it loves.”

The car merged into the current of the city, driving toward a story about memory, love, and meeting again — while across town, in an SM practice room, two trainees packed up for the night. “Eonni, when does his film start shooting?” — “Next month, probably.” — “Can we visit the set?” — “We’ll see… maybe.” Seoul burned bright in every window, and everyone in it had a story either happening or about to.
