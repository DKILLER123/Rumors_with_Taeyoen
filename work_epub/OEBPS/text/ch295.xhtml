Song Ji-ho Is Indeed a Bastard

Thursday 23:11 → Friday 00:53 the inner cabin, where a notebook opens · one waitress’s borrowed minute · the top deck, under somebody else’s fireworks one answer deferred on favorable terms, one lesson taught off a call button, one confession prepared the only way she knows how, replaying everything, and one kiss that arrived with a verdict attached

Confess?

The color came up in Im Yoon-a’s face at reading speed. She pressed her lips, dropped her lashes, and folded whatever had just surfaced neatly under the eyelids, the way she folded everything.

When the eyes came back up they were clear water all the way down.

“I’ve never confessed to anyone. So I don’t know how the scene is played.”

A beat, and then, evenly:

“Teach me well tonight, seonsaengnim, and I’ll attempt it.”

And with that she set the terms of the surrender she hadn’t agreed to: door neither opened nor closed, initiative still in her pocket, and one piece of information left on the table where he couldn’t miss it. I have never done this. Not once. With anyone.

Every man alive carries the old hunting firmware. Put an undone thing in front of him and the wanting to be the one who does it with you arrives on its own. That way, whenever it finally happens, the first time, the very first time, is his. Possession, pre-installed at the factory. Let him do the math.

Something moved through the fine grain between his brows: pleasure, at low concentration, briefly visible. He was already reorganizing the night around a syllabus.

“Come with me.”

He stood and pushed open the door of the inner lounge, and the boat went on carrying them up the river, both banks trading lights through the glass.

Inside, the cabin held only the two of them and the small patient sound of water taking its turns against the hull. Yoon-a took the seat by the window, opened a notebook, and held her pen the way other people hold rosaries. He sat down across from her and leaned in a degree.

“There is no shortcut for this craft. I’ll say that once, and then never again.”

“I know.” The pen touched down on the page, ready. “That’s why I’m actually here to learn. Not to audit.”

“Then we start at the foundation—”

He stopped mid-sentence. His eyes had gone to the service bell on the wall.

“One moment.”

He pressed it. She waited, faintly puzzled, until a knock arrived and a waitress came in with a tray, twenty-seven or eight, uniform pressed to the standard of a flag, and a professional smile curved at exactly the angle the manual must have drawn.

“Excuse the interruption. Your coffee.” Two cups went down without one sound of porcelain on tray. “Anything else for you tonight?”

“Nothing. Thank you.”

“Enjoy your evening.”

A bow measured to the millimeter, and she was gone.

The door had not finished closing before the smile left her face entirely, switched off at the wall. The shoulders came down half an inch. A quiet breath went out of her; one hand rose and kneaded the back of her neck; and her steps, going away down the corridor, had put on weight like a coat.

He turned back to the table.

“The waitress. What did you see?”

“The waitress?” A blink, then a rewind. “She was… lovely. Smiled the whole time. Very professional.”

“And?”

She went digging and came up with nothing but the smile. She shook her head, rueful.

He didn’t correct her. He picked up his coffee and drank instead, and let the boat talk for a moment.

“This vessel is chartered, almost exclusively, by money with names. Chairmen. Top-tier talent. The kind of guest she cannot afford to have see her tired.” He set the cup down. “When she came in: the arc of the smile, the state of the eyes, the tone pitched warm with the ends lifted. None of it surprised you, because none of it was fresh. It’s the smile she has worn at this door a thousand times.”

“And when she went out?”

“You didn’t see it. The door closed, and the smile was gone before the latch. Shoulders down. One breath. A hand at her neck. Her steps got heavier as she moved away from us.” He let that sit. “That is the whole first lesson.”

The last lesson · the river classroom · six studies, one waitress

The working actress’s curriculum the Encyclopedia’s five dimensions, translated for one student between an album and a tour

1Live first, observe on top of it. Every teacher says “enter the character’s world early, keep a notebook.” Nobody tells you what to write down. The waitress gave us a minute, and the minute held her whole job: the trained arc, the lifted tone ends, the cup that never spoke against the tray. And then the door, the one instant the performance wasn’t running. That instant is the woman. Surface observation is what everyone collects; you are after the drop after the smile.

2Muscle memory, by the hundred. A speech read fifty times and the same speech read five hundred are different artworks. The target: run the passage at dinner, in the shower, in the car between schedules, without a stumble, without a dropped syllable. Past that threshold you are finally free to stop thinking about the line and start playing the moment, because the actor who is half-drafting her next sentence has a face the audience reads like a subtitle.

3Move in before you perform. The question is never “how do I play her.” It is “if I were her, what would I do.” Write the woman whole (childhood, damage, what she loves too much) and then give the body its own vocabulary: the earlobe when she lies, the cuff-button when she’s cornered, the lip when she’s caught being happy. An audience cannot remember a mood. It memorizes a gesture.

4The repeat exercise. Repetition until the planning drops out of the exchange. The instrument stops announcing and starts answering. (Demonstrated below, at cost to everyone’s dignity.)

5Daily maintenance. The suspended wrist: write a page in the air and keep it level; the breath worked low, for lines that ride on it; the animal study, for a body that can hold shapes it has never had a name for. Every day. The pianist does not skip scales because the concert went well.

6Review, and review again. Watch your own takes back, past the squirming. Where was it too much; where was it not yet anything; where did the release run past its mark. Then compare against the ones who do it best, and go again. Anyone can let go. Coming back on the exact beat is the entire craft.

She wrote all six down. Then she underlined the first one twice, because a waitress she had barely looked at had just handed her a stranger’s whole biography in under a minute, and the top of her head was never going to be quiet again.

“This is genuinely ridiculous,” she managed, laughing into her wrist.

“This is genuinely ridiculous.” Perfectly grave. He did not stop, which was the entire point of the exercise and also somehow its punchline.

She got her breath back and looked at him with new arithmetic behind her eyes.

“You mean… don’t preload. Only answer.”

“That’s the whole machine. Most actors have the next beat decided before the current one has finished landing, the tear here, the flare there, all of it scheduled. That isn’t acting; that’s a tram route. Real exchange is: something arrives, you catch it, you answer it. The exercise sands the planning off your reflexes until what’s left is response.”

The pen moved: repeat until answering replaces planning. She looked up, lit from somewhere behind the face.

“This one seems… hard. And expensive.”

“Hard is correct. If it were easy the country would be wall-to-wall grand-prize winners.”

They worked through five and six the same way, his pen rapping the table at the wrist exercise until she held a page of air steady enough to be read; a lecture on the injured cat (“you go down onto the floor and you stay where it hurts and you don’t flinch on cue; a frightened person has cat logic, and you are learning to hold their stillness”) that had her solemn for a full minute, and somewhere in the middle of it the city outside the glass began thinning its lights, and the night leaned in.

She closed the notebook at last and let a long breath go, the satisfied kind, heavy as a finished set.

“Song Ji-ho. Thank you. This is… months of work, in one night.”

“So the teaching was good.”

“Ne,” she said, smiling despite herself. “The teaching was good.”

“Then—” The mischief surfaced, unhurried. “Your confession, Im Yoon-a. The examination is now in session.”

“…I need a moment to prepare.”

“By all means.”

She closed her eyes.

Memory · everything, on request, in order

The first meeting was a hospital corridor of all places, a senior member laid up, a visit organized like a mission, and herself performing Concern with both hands, voice modulated, timing flawless, until she turned from the bedside and found a stranger in the doorway watching the whole second act with polite interest. She had wanted the floor to open. She had also, embarrassingly, noticed: he’s rather something.

Then one day the word came down about that rather-something face: the monthly evaluation, first place, taken with a song he had written himself. The practice rooms did their gossip rounds. A face was one thing; a face with a pen was another category of problem.

Then the birthdays, small parcels traveling in both directions, the cautious opening moves of people pretending to be ordinary friends. Then the rumors with Taeyeon eonni, and the first real appetite: what kind of trainee makes Taeyeon eonni lower her guard? Then the song the whole peninsula hummed for a season; word was, it had been written with her in mind. A woman with that temperament, sung to like that. What chance did she ever have.

Then the endorsement: the airport and its bickering; the Alps, snow down her collar and his laugh carrying across it; the trail, and his hand arriving exactly when the footing stopped being an opinion; the sea rescue; the long walk out on his back, her cheek against the damp heat of his shirt, his pulse keeping honest time under her ear; and later, in a hotel room, her ankle in his two hands and his complete, unbearable attention while he worked the strain out of it. Her heartbeat that afternoon had been an incident she never reported.

And then the man had sat across a table, mild as milk, and said that Taeyeon was his girlfriend now, and added, with the same calm, that she herself was surely the sort of bad woman who slipped in through cracks. Her memory had filed that sentence under unforgivable, in a folder that also, traitorously, kept the ankle.

She had been a girl with eyes, once, fifteen, sixteen, a new crush every season, this one’s shoulders, that one’s voice. Then debut had closed over all of it like water: the schedules, the company rules, years of it. The drought had lasted so long she had stopped expecting weather.

He had been the first. And then this absolute. She clenched a fist on her knee, breath quickening, because this man had confessed to dating her eonni, to her face, and called her the bad woman in the same breath. As if the married man in the equation were not him. As if the pure-hearted deer had wandered into his garden and not the exact other way around.

The verdict had been written. Death sentence. Filed, stamped.

Unfortunately, the appeals process had three grounds.

Ground one: the face. I have never been a shallow woman, but I contain a human being, and the human being has eyes. There are faces that would survive being caught red-handed. His is one. I would forgive the crime while photographing the evidence.

Ground two: the miracle. One year, one acquisition, LOEN whole. Nobody can explain it. Tiffany eonni could, maybe. Taeyeon eonni certainly. Sica eonni probably counts herself in. That’s the pattern, isn’t it. Understanding him is how they all fell in. It’s a well with no bottom and every bucket comes up shining.

Ground three, the one the defense hates: a man this finished, this supplied, went out of his way to court me. I knew it was wrong. I knew. And some cellar of me was purring the entire time. A script from one of the three great screenwriters. A chairman worth a trillion won, teaching me acting in the middle of his own album crunch, at night, on a boat. What was my heart supposed to do with that? It isn’t carved from riverstone.

When she opened her eyes, there was a fine shine of water sitting low in them.

“Follow me.”

He went where her hand took him: through the hush of the corridor, out the cabin door, up to the open top deck, where the night was running its usual jewelry heist, both banks lit, the water carrying the stolen goods. Seoul never slept, but it had, for the moment, consented to lower its voice: traffic gone dumb in the distance, the river’s small applause gentled, even the wind walking on tiptoe.

She stopped at the rail and let go of him and turned to face him, and the wind took the small hairs at her brow; she caught them and tucked them back, unhurried, eyes never leaving his. She breathed in until her shoulders could not rise any further.

“Song Ji-ho.”

The full name, set down one syllable at a time, with the care of a woman who had been rehearsing it all evening without admitting that was what the rehearsal was.

“I don’t know how to do this.” Her eyes dropped; a small self-mocking curve visited her mouth. “I have never confessed. Truly never. Someone pursued me, I went around him. Someone liked me, I played ignorant. It wasn’t pride. It was… fear.”

She looked up again.

“Because once it’s said, it can’t be recalled. And what you are in my chest, I can’t even say that clearly to myself. How was I supposed to say it to you?”

The wind worked at her voice and lost; every word arrived whole.

“But tonight, through all six of those lessons, observing, entering, answering, one question kept running underneath. If this were a scene. If I were playing a woman in love with you… how would I play her?”

The smile that crossed her face was small and lit from inside.

“I’d let myself stand near enough to catch your scent. I’d watch you while you talked, until you were the one who had to look away. I’d keep a ledger of you: the way you press your lips before a decision, the way you go quiet when you’re actually thinking, the way you tap the rim of the cup once, always once, before you drink.” A breath. “I kept the ledger. Every entry.”

She paused, and the river waited with her.

“But Song Ji-ho — I’m not playing.”

“Everything you taught tonight about observation, I don’t know how to spend it on a part yet. It’s all been spent on you.”

She came forward one small step. The wind moved a strand of her hair onto his shoulder; she left it; so did he.

“I can’t find the beginning. Maybe the forest road, on your back. Maybe the hotel room, with my foot in your hands. Maybe—” the chin came down, the voice went muffled, “—maybe the moment you told me about Taeyeon eonni. Yes. That one. That’s the one.”

“Do you know what that news did, coming in? First nothing, a blank. Then, ah. So that’s how it is. And then later, alone, with no warning, it simply hurt. Not a clean pain. A stoppage. Something lodged where swallowing happens, that wouldn’t go down and wouldn’t come up.”

“It took me until that week to name it. Envy.” Her eyes came up, rimmed and steady. “I envy Taeyeon eonni. Not the momentum. Not the polls. I envy her the man standing in front of me.”

She sniffed once, and the grievance arrived.

“You are hateful, Song Ji-ho. Do you know how hateful? Handsome, that would be fine, but then talented. Talented, I could survive, but then sincere. Sincere, tolerable, but then good to me. Why are you good to me? Did it occur to you that I would notice? That a woman who hasn’t moved her heart one centimeter in years, when someone is suddenly good to her, will not know where to put her hands?”

Her fist arrived at his chest, landed, and retreated, a push with the approximate force of a petal.

“You did it on purpose.”

Something at the base of him was visibly melting, degree by degree, under the lamplight.

She drew her hand back, looked at the deck, and was quiet for three seconds. When she spoke again, the voice had softened through and through.

“So. Tonight was never about the acting.”

“I wanted to see you. That’s the whole syllabus.”

“I wanted a reason that would keep us in one room a little longer.”

She lifted her eyes, and the mist in them finally consented to gather, pooled, bright, and absolutely refusing to fall.

“Song Ji-ho. I like you.”

Four words, quick and light, the way one sets down something breakable before the nerve runs out. Then her lips pressed tight, and she stood at the rail watching him without one blink, a woman awaiting a sentence.

The wind passed between them, taking her hair across his lapel. The neon on both banks went on rearranging itself; the water went on wearing the city upside down; the world, in every particular, was the same world it had been one minute ago.

But in that minute she had unfolded the thing she had kept folded longest, and held it out open.

She could no longer say which of it was scene and which was self. Possibly she had lost the ability some time ago. Possibly there had been no scene to lose.

The Rail · Wind Level · A Question of Permission

“Yoon-a.”

“May I kiss you?”

“Are you certain? They say a person’s soul is born in two halves and scattered, and that a true kiss is the halves recognizing each other.”

“A false kiss is only temperature. A true one numbs you to the fingertips, stops the heartbeat mid-count, and mutes the world. What if you kiss me and I feel none of that?”

“Yoon-a-ya. Every lie in the world fails the moment a kiss lands.”

He brought his mouth to hers, and the shoreline chose that exact second to disagree with the night: a chrysanthemum of gold went up over the bank and opened, and the sky rained down its thousand sparks across the water, across the rail, across the two people who had stopped being separate.

Inside her head a single tone drowned everything. Fireworks were going off above her, and all she could certify was the soft warmth at her mouth. Then the numbness set out from her lips like news, through the blood, out to the fingertips, down every nerve to its farthest desk. Her hands found his shoulders and discovered they had no staffing. She was emptied; she was filled; the two conditions kept trading places.

Her heart dropped one beat entirely, then went after it at a gallop.

She opened her eyes: his were closed, lashes trembling in the changing light of the fireworks, and the sight of that tremble undid the last of her arguments.

So it’s true: the numbness. The stopped count. The mute world. My body signed for this long before I found the courage to. It knew him first. It was only ever waiting for me to catch up.

The kiss began the way careful things begin, testing, confirming. When her answer arrived he gathered her in by the waist and closed the last distance, and the kiss deepened the way a sentence deepens when it stops being dictated and starts being meant. He held her lower lip like something he was in no hurry to swallow. Her replies went from novice to feral by degrees, hands sliding from his shoulders to the back of his neck, fingers laced: him advancing, her yielding; her advancing, him yielding: a duet nobody had rehearsed, danced better for it.

Overhead the sky kept spending: gold, then red, then violet, the night taking color like a page. The wind took her hair and wove it into his. The boat went on without them, laying its thin seam of wake across the river, leaving both banks’ lights a little further behind each minute. None of it registered. The world had two people in it and had given the rest of the seating away.

A kiss is the most binding of contracts, truer than an oath, heavier than a ring. Once it has landed, you can never again convincingly pretend you don’t love. From that hour, wanting has a voice, liking has a shape, and missing has an address.

Some unhurried time later, mouths parted. She stood in the circle of his arms with her forehead against his jaw, breathing in small quick installments. Her face was cooking; the heat had reached her ears and was making progress down her neck.

He looked down at her and the laugh arrived before he could file it.

She felt it arrive. She tipped her head back and aimed her eyes at him with intent, and the intent dissolved in transit, and what landed was a look with no ammunition in it whatsoever.

“What’s funny?”

He didn’t answer. His hand rose and laid the wind-worked hair back behind her ear, and his thumb took one slow pass along the outer edge of the ear on its way out of the job.

She pulled her neck in like a tortoise. The ear went redder.

“The question from before.” Low, unhurried. “Do you have your answer now?”

She blinked. The false kiss and its three symptoms; his counter about lies; all of it coming back at once, and the numbness, she found, had still not fully filed its departure.

Any doubt that had survived to this point did not survive the trip back up the river. She liked him. He liked her. The kiss had signed for both of them.

She bit her lip, hunted for the sentence, and found the vault empty. What emerged, at last, was:

“…It was just a scene.”

He laughed out loud and folded her back in. She struggled, numerically, for about a second and a half, then let the arms have their jurisdiction, and spoke into his shirtfront.

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm.”

“You haven’t answered me.”

“Answered what?”

“Don’t you dare play obtuse—” She tipped her head back, eyes up at him with the whole threat catalog in them, none of it operational. “I said I like you. And you have not, even once, said—”

“Wasn’t it a scene?”

“It was a scene. But I acted it with real feeling.”

Something in him went entirely to soft at that, the woman who was composure itself at every press line in the country, standing in his arms at midnight, waiting on an answer like a girl at a gate.

He bent and set one kiss on her forehead, unhurried, a signature.

“Im Yoon-a.”

“Mm?”

“I think… I have liked you for a long time too.”

She went still.

And then she smiled, eyes into two crescents, brighter than anything the sky had spent all night, and put her face back into his chest and her arms all the way around his waist, and held on with the grip of a woman docking a boat she intends to keep.

Song Ji-ho is, by every court’s finding, a bastard. The particular subcategory of bastard a woman cannot bring herself to refuse.
