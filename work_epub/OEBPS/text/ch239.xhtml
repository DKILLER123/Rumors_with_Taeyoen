Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Nine

A Home with Peaches

August 6–7, 2013 Incheon arrivals · LOEN Entertainment · a fifteenth-floor apartment in Seongsu-dong a girl comes home from a tour, asks for a song — and gets a curtain rod, a kimchi crock, and a family

August 6, 2013.

Ji-ho woke, reached across the pillow, and found only the faint warm ghost of her scent.

Girls’ Generation’s renewal window was closing in, and SM was extracting every remaining calorie of value — schedules that would have run a general ragged. Until signatures landed, the machine would not slow down. He washed up and went to the company.

✦ ✦ ✦

At the same hour, at the international airport, f(x) slipped quietly home from their North American run.

SM had one habit no rival shared: the team above all. Other agencies, handed a suddenly white-hot member, would pivot the whole company to that one career. SM did the opposite — a member catches fire, and the fire is used to light the whole group. Choi Sulli had detonated with The Girl in the Sun; the moment the film promo wrapped, she was folded back into the team and shipped to America. On the Austin stage they’d run through their catalog for a crowd that knew the words — and come home with Billboard and MTV coverage and a real crack opened in the Western market. Today they landed; tomorrow, song selection for the second full album began.

Sulli came through arrivals in an apricot floral slip dress and flat woven sandals, the high waistline and ankle-length fall of it making her look like something painted. She ducked into the van, dropped into Victoria’s lap with a happy wriggle, and addressed the driver’s seat.

“Oppa — the route back to the dorm passes LOEN, right?”

“If we take Chunho-daero… yes, we’d pass it.”

“Then please stop there for a minute when we do~”

“Understood.”

Victoria looked down at the head in her lap, amused.

“Sulli. Wheels barely down and you’re off to see… Chairman Song.”

“We have a new album coming!” — delivered with shameless cheer — “I’m going to see if I can beg a song off Oppa.”

“REALLY?”

Amber, Luna, and Krystal turned as one, eyes lit with naked hope. Everyone on the peninsula now knew the math: a Ji-ho song was a chart event. Two of them had reached Billboard. Nobody dared dream that far for themselves — but a domestic smash? Entirely plausible.

“Will he really write for us?”

“Weeeell, no promises~” Sulli performed modesty with the technique of a master. “You know Oppa’s a chairman now, he’s busy. But I’ll work hard at it. Eighty percent odds, maybe? Who told Oppa to dote on me~”

The van filled with the citrus tang of collective envy.

“I’m so jealous, Sulli. Having a big brother like that.”

“Sulli, FIGHTING! The whole team’s riding on you!”

Victoria ruffled her hair.

“Do your best, hm?”

“Arasseo, eonni~”

Victoria watched the girl beam and felt a small private awe. Two foreigners had landed on this peninsula to try their luck — and one of them had become an owner inside a single year, while she was still grinding schedules. People and people, she thought, cannot be compared. It only ruins your morning.

✦ ✦ ✦

Half an hour later the van pulled over near LOEN, and Sulli hopped out with a wave.

The sidewalk pickets — Solbeam and IU fans on their daily vigil — spotted her in seconds. YA, ISN’T THAT SULLI? — WHERE? — SHE’S WALKING OVER— DAEBAK, IT’S REALLY HER!

“Sulli-yah! Sulli!”

“Ne~ annyeonghaseyo.”

She bowed her way through the small crowd, smiling wide, while security — long since briefed on exactly who this visitor was to the chairman — closed around her and walked her toward the doors.

“Can we get a photo?”

“An autograph??”

“Sulli, are you here to see Ji-ho Oppa?”

At that one she stopped, and the smile went full crescent.

“I’m here to see Ji-eun AND Oppa. Everyone watch out for the heat today~”

And she was gone through the doors.

✦ ✦ ✦

Upstairs, Ji-ho was flattening a stack of signature files with mechanical efficiency — check, sign, next — until the last folder closed.

“Ahhh~ finally.”

He stretched, deeply grateful, not for the first time, that he’d kept Cho Young-chul. The internal line buzzed.

“Yeoboseyo.”

“Hoejang-nim — Choi Sulli-ssi is here.”

Peaches was back. His mood lifted a full floor.

“Send her up.”

He put down the phone and — with total sincerity — fixed his collar and his hair. Excellent. Maximum handsome.

At the office door, Sulli thanked the secretary, waited for the corridor to clear, took out a compact, confirmed the state of the national treasure, and knocked.

“Come in.”

She pushed the door open onto a tableau: the chairman at his desk, studying a document with the profound gravity of a man who had definitely not just fixed his hair. A laugh escaped her before she could stop it. Oppa KNEW she was coming. This entire composition was for her benefit. Though — credit where due — the white shirt against that frame, under that face… the staging worked.

He looked up with beautifully performed mild surprise.

“Oh. Peaches is back.”

“Wah— is that really MY Oppa? Daebak. Is my Oppa really a CHAIRMAN? So handsome~” — playing her assigned role with equal commitment.

“Hahaha.” He rose, dropping the act. “Peaches. How do you like my office?”

She crossed the room fast and arrived against his chest.

“Can’t see it, can’t see it. My eyes are full of Oppa.”

The soft warm weight of her, the fruit-sweet smell, the shameless purr of it — entirely unfair as a combination. He picked her up and spun her, and her laughter rang off the window-wall, bright as bells. Two rotations; touchdown; and he looked down into the fresh, clean face grinning up at him, and kissed her, unhurried. Peach lip balm. Notably sweet. When they parted, her eyes had gone glossy and enormous.

“Oppa. I missed you so much I nearly went insane.”

Against the furnace of her he felt the small familiar guilt — a man with no shortage of company claiming equivalent longing would have been lying. She read the silence and, characteristically, didn’t mind it in the least. She laced their fingers.

“Oppa. Come to mine tonight.”

“Of course.”

He sat and gathered her onto his lap; her pale calves swung and hooked at the ankles.

“Busy stretch coming up?”

“These two months are album prep. Light at first — recording and the music video is when it gets heavy.” She blinked, then deployed the eyes at maximum aperture, arms going around his neck. “Oppa. Write us a song? Pleeeease~”

He was, of course, always going to say yes. But agreeing instantly wasted a perfectly good negotiation.

“A song, hm.” He manufactured difficulty. “Things are busy lately. The schedule—”

“Oppa~ please please please~” She rocked him bodily side to side, voice going syrup-soft. “You’re SO talented, if you write it, it’ll definitely blow up. Please? Please~? Hm? HM? Oppa, help me~!”

Between the full-body aegyo and the hemline consequences of all that rocking, his negotiating position deteriorated rapidly and enjoyably.

“All right, all right. I’m willing to write it — the real question is whether SM is willing to BUY it. I don’t come cheap, you know.”

Her brow puckered — that was, in fact, a real problem — and the crestfallen face broke his resolve completely.

“Fine. Come on, get up. I’ll take you home.”

“REALLY? Oppa’s skipping work??”

“When I was an artist I couldn’t set my own hours. If I’m chairman and STILL can’t set my own hours, what was the point of the acquisition?”

“Wah. Oppa. ICONIC.”

“Let’s go.”

“Yay!” She was already halfway to the door, walking on springs. “Perfect timing — I bought SO many things for the new place. Oppa can help me set it all up.”

He smiled — and remembered, with a click, the quest list. Eighteen wishes. Three at a time earned a reward. Decorate the home. Try making kimchi. Learn to cook. All three, achievable this very week.

Her new place was in Seongsu-dong — a high-end building he’d had sourced quietly, a hundred and forty square meters of light. They took the chairman’s elevator to the garage and slipped out the back gate.

The afternoon still held its summer heat. She hung off his arm with half her weight, floral hem swinging, a butterfly with a parking pass.

“Oppa, I bought SO much — curtains, a rug, cushions, and this GORGEOUS floor lamp!” She tipped her face up, eyes crescenting. “I couldn’t move any of it myself. It’s all piled in the living room.”

“So I’ve been recruited as a laborer.”

She popped up on her toes and kissed his cheek.

“The HANDSOMEST laborer Oppa~”

“Fine. The laborer reports for duty.”

Fifteenth floor, light everywhere. The living room was an archipelago of boxes. She dragged him to the window — THE HAN’S RIGHT THERE, IT’S SO PRETTY AT NIGHT — then the bedroom — THE BED’S BUILT BUT I SAVED THE BEDDING FOR US TO DO TOGETHER — then the kitchen — I BOUGHT EVERY POT THEY HAD, I CAN COOK FOR MYSELF NOW — then the balcony — I’M PUTTING FLOWERS EVERYWHERE, OPPA, WHAT FLOWERS DO YOU LIKE? — a small excited sparrow narrating her whole future at once. And something in his chest went soft and strange, because he understood exactly what he was watching: a girl who had never once had a home, building one. He knew better than anyone what that word cost, and what it bought.

“Boxes first.” He rolled up his sleeves, and his voice had gentled without his permission. “What do you want to start with?”

“The floor lamp! It goes in that corner — I’m going to read on the sofa at night with it on.”

He cut boxes open; she crouched beside him, chin on both fists, supervising.

“Oppa. You look handsome opening boxes.”

“When do I not look handsome?”

“When you drool in your sleep?”

“I DROOL in my—?”

“Kidding~ hahahaha—”

She laughed herself sideways; a dress strap slid off one shoulder unnoticed. He reached over and set it right, fingertip grazing the round of her shoulder — and the laugh stopped, and pink climbed her cheeks, and her eyes stayed on him, very bright.

The air acquired a certain charge. He coughed once, diplomatically.

“Lamp’s up. Let’s see it.”

Vintage brass stem, warm yellow shade. Lit, it poured a soft gold pocket into the corner and the whole room changed temperature. She padded over, dropped into the still-wrapped sofa, and patted the spot beside her. He sat; she folded into him instantly, head on his shoulder, knees drawn up, the whole of her fitting into remarkably little space.

“So good,” she said quietly. “Oppa, and a new home.”

“It gets better from here.”

A pause. Then, very softly:

“Oppa. You know this is the first room that’s ever been mine?”

He looked down at her.

“When I was little it was me, Mom, and my brothers in one small room. Then Mom sent me to SM and it was dorms. Always dorms. I’ve never lived anywhere that was just… mine.”

Something caught at his chest, low and old. He thought of a basement room with no window, of years of other people’s holidays observed from the outside, of instant noodles on New Year’s Eve eaten standing up. Different city, same shape of alone.

“Me too.”

Her head came up, surprised.

“My father left when I was eight. My mother’s last call came when I was in middle school. After that it was other people’s spare rooms, then a basement with no window — you couldn’t tell day from night down there. Under-the-table jobs. On holidays, when everyone else had a table full of family, I ate ramyeon alone.”

Her eyes reddened in real time. She had never heard any of this — the second person after Taeyeon, though she didn’t know that, to be handed the actual past. So THAT was why he’d been kind to her from the very beginning, back when there was no reason. He’d recognized the species. The resonance of it went through her like a struck tuning fork; her nose stung; she reached up and took his face in both hands.

“Oppa. You’re not alone anymore. Not ever again.”

Tears standing, smile blazing — the whole contradiction of her in one expression.

“Neither are you.”

The tears fell. The smile stayed. Something changed hands without a word.

Not pity — recognition. Not sympathy — the fierce economy of two people who knew the same cold and had found the same fire.

“Back to work?” He thumbed the wet from her cheek.

“Mm. Back to work!”

✦ ✦ ✦

The curtains were milk-white linen. He stood on a chair and hung them while she fed him hooks from below, looking up at intervals to grin like a fool. WHAT? — YOU LOOK SO DOMESTIC, OPPA. — DOMESTIC?? — HANDSOME AND DOMESTIC. I WON THE LOTTERY. He nearly fell off the chair.

The bedding was pale pink, printed with tiny strawberries, aggressively adolescent, and she loved it with her whole heart. They shook the sheet out like a sail, each working their own side; stuffing the duvet turned into a border conflict — YOU’RE PUSHING IT CROOKED — YOU’RE HOLDING THE CORNER HOSTAGE — and she emerged from inside the cover with staticky hair and a scarlet face and they both broke down completely.

When the bed was made they collapsed onto it in parallel, staring at the ceiling.

“Oppa.” She rolled onto her side, chin propped. “Don’t we look like newlyweds?”

The dusk was in her eyes when he turned — soft and deep as standing water.

“We do. Day one of the marriage: decorating the house.”

“Then today’s our wedding day.” She wriggled closer, satisfied — and then the eyes went wide and fraudulent with innocence. “So shouldn’t there be a wedding night?”

“Peaches. You’re playing with fire.”

“Is Oppa going to put it out?”

The city lit itself outside the new curtains. The rest belonged to the two of them.

✦ ✦ ✦

Deep in the night she lay against him, drawing idle circles on his chest, and asked what he’d wanted most as a child. A ROOM OF MY OWN, he said. SMALL IS FINE. A BED AND A DESK. THE BASEMENT DIDN’T HAVE A WINDOW — I USED TO DREAM ABOUT SUNLIGHT ON A FLOOR. That simple? she said, coming up on an elbow. That simple, he said. And she settled back down and told him hers: A HOME. NOT A HOUSE — A HOME. PARENTS. HOT FOOD. SOMEONE WAITING WHEN YOU COME BACK. He moved his hand slowly over her hair and said the thing he actually believed: home isn’t a place. It’s between people. Wherever people who love each other live — that’s the address. She was quiet for a second, and then she was crying into his chest and laughing at the same time, and the words came out muffled: THEN OPPA’S MY ADDRESS.

✦ ✦ ✦

Morning came in through the milk-white linen and lay in warm stripes across the strawberry duvet.

She slept like a cat, one leg annexing his, breath slow and even. He woke first and watched her — the sleeping face quieter than the waking one, lips slightly pursed around some dream — and moved her bangs aside with two careful fingers. Same broken childhood. Same missing rooms. And now: each other. She burrowed closer, muttered something in dream-language, and slept on. He didn’t move for a long time.

She surfaced eventually, straight into his eyes.

“Oppa’s been awake long?”

“A while. Sleep well?”

“With Oppa here? Best ever.” She nuzzled in — and her stomach growled with theatrical timing, and she went pink.

“Hungry. I’ll make breakfast.”

“I’m coming! I want to watch Oppa cook.”

The refrigerator turned out well-stocked — eggs, vegetables, milk. He called the menu: omelets, a salad. She draped herself over the island counter, chin on hands, and provided commentary. HOW DOES OPPA KNOW HOW TO DO EVERYTHING? — LIVE ALONE ABROAD LONG ENOUGH, IT’S COOK OR STARVE. A thoughtful pause. Then: OPPA. WHEN YOU COOKED BEFORE… WAS IT ALWAYS FOR ONE? His knife stopped for half a beat. MM. FOR ONE. And she came around the island and wrapped him from behind, cheek between his shoulder blades, and said into his shirt: FROM NOW ON YOU COOK FOR ME, AND I COOK FOR YOU. His hand closed over hers at his waist. OKAY.

“Peaches. Want to learn?”

“NOW?”

“I’ll teach you. So when I’m not here you can feed yourself something better than delivery. And—” a beat, softer — “cooking together is what makes it taste like home.”

Her eyes went glassy and enormous, and then she smiled with everything she had. OKAY. TEACH ME.

Breakfast became a cooking class. He stood behind her, hand over hand on the spatula. The first egg lost its shell into the bowl — AAAH — retrieved with chopsticks, dignity partially intact. The second went in clean and she cheered like a stadium crowd, and got a kiss on the cheek for it. The first omelet tore mid-roll and she deflated; he told her about the time he’d nearly smoked out an entire basement floor and been mistaken for a house fire, and she laughed until she had to sit down, and the second omelet — lopsided, structurally suspect — held. I DID IT! OPPA, LOOK! — VERY GOOD. PEACHES IS A GENIUS. And the genius glowed brighter than the stove.

They ate on the balcony in the morning wind, and she declared her own crooked omelet the best thing she had ever tasted, and he did not argue.

“Peaches. Want to make kimchi?”

“KIMCHI?”

“Homemade beats store-bought. And—” he paused, and told her the true part plainly, “—my third year here, there was a halmeoni next door who put up kimchi every winter. I lived alone; she’d bring me some, every time. And every time I ate it, the room felt less empty. I promised myself: when I had a home of my own someday, I’d make my own. Kimchi you put up in your own kitchen tastes like family.”

She listened with her eyes going red again, and crossed the kitchen and held onto him. FROM NOW ON I’LL MAKE IT WITH YOU. EVERY YEAR. — OKAY. — HUGE BATCHES. A WHOLE YEAR’S WORTH. — OKAY. — AND WE GIVE SOME TO FRIENDS AND TELL THEM: OPPA AND I MADE THIS. He kissed the top of her head. OKAY.

She pulled back, bright-eyed and mercantile.

“I want to go BUY the ingredients together. That’s what makes it count! …But. Ugh. Fine, delivery—”

And that was when the thought arrived, and he went very still. UTTERLY UNREMARKABLE — could it stretch?
