Chapter One

Back to 2012

2025 Seoul — Breeze Lounge last set of the night

Stepping into Breeze felt like stepping off a plane in Bali. Rattan lamps, slow ceiling fans, house-special cocktails that tasted like a vacation you couldn’t afford — the place drew a steady crowd of young office workers chasing exactly one glass past sober.

Tonight the booths were quiet. A song drifted through the little bar, carried on a warm, unhurried male voice — rich and textured, like spring water heated just short of boiling, sliding down slow and settling somewhere behind the ribs.

Bleeding Love · Leona Lewis · 2007

Closed off from love,

I didn’t need the pain.

Once or twice was enough,

And it was all in vain.

Time starts to pass…

“Bleeding Love.” The song that had made Leona Lewis a global name — and one of the resident singer’s personal favorites.

His name was Song Ji-ho.

Original
Song Ji-ho
Singer-songwriter · Producer · Protagonist
SM Entertainment trainee
Born: January 21, 1994

A Los Angeles-raised Korean-American who crosses the Pacific with a remarkable voice, a producer’s memory, and one last chance to rebuild his life.

His father had walked out of their Los Angeles apartment when Ji-ho was eight and never come back. His mother lasted until middle school before she, too, disappeared from his life, leaving a Korean-American kid to raise himself in Koreatown on free lunches and borrowed couches. What he had left was music. He had a voice people stopped to listen to, so he used it — singing in bars and clubs before he was even old enough to legally sit in them, saving every crumpled tip for two years. At eighteen, he boarded a one-way flight across the Pacific to the land his grandmother used to call uri nara — our country — to chase the only dream he had: becoming an idol trainee on the Peninsula.

It didn’t work.

Eighteen was already old for a trainee. He was a gyopo nobody with no connections, no proper dance foundation, and a voice that was gorgeous but completely self-taught — all instinct, no technique. Thirteen auditions. Thirteen rejections. Ji-ho refused to quit, so he stayed in Korea, working days and dreaming nights.

A penniless foreign kid making it big in someone else’s country — it was a fairy tale, and he’d understood that far too late. When the last of the hope burned off, he took the practical road: a residency gig as a lounge singer.

Twenty-seven hundred dollars a month. Six nights a week, four forty-five-minute sets a night. Room, meals, insurance, the works.

No girlfriend. No plans to marry. When the mood struck, he’d drift into some other bar, charm someone lovely, and take her home. It was a glittering, hollow sort of life, and he’d gotten very good at not looking at it directly.

Tonight’s sets had gone well — a hundred and twenty dollars in tips, and a rare lightness in his chest. After closing, he changed clothes and cleaned up.

Broad shoulders, narrow waist; a plain white shirt sat on his gym-built frame exactly the way shirts were supposed to. A high, straight nose gave his profile a carved look, and when his thin lips pressed together, there was something cool and untouchable about him. Loose black hair fell over a smooth forehead; the line from brow to jaw ran clean, without a single flaw.

His parents had taken no responsibility for his life — but they’d left him a heaven-sent voice and a face that opened doors. It had kept him fed. It had, honestly, kept him more than fed. Ji-ho chose to think of it as fate’s apology gift.

At the next bar, he spotted her almost immediately: a pale-pink slip dress, delicate features, thin straps resting lightly on soft, rounded shoulders. Below the lace hem, her legs were slender and fair, almost luminous under the bar lights.

“Buy you a drink? Or — you could buy me one. I’m flexible.”

She’d caught sight of his face and her eyes had lit first; she beat him to the opening line. They kept the unspoken rules: no real names that mattered, no histories, only easy talk and easier laughter. Two drinks in, his arm found the curve of her waist as if it had always belonged there.

“Let’s go.”

Back at his place, a shared shower, a hundred calories burned in the dark. The woman beside him slipped quickly into a deep, tired sleep.

Ji-ho pulled on a shirt, eased out of bed without waking her, brewed a cup of lavender tea, and sat down at his computer.

On the screen, an interface glowed softly, waiting for him: Dream Life: The Simulation.

He’d stumbled onto the game five years ago, a random download on an insomniac night.

The protagonist’s backstory had stopped him cold — it was his. Beat for beat. The vanished parents, the bar singing, the flight to Korea. Except for one difference: the game’s protagonist had passed his trainee audition, and step by step, had climbed all the way up.

Ji-ho’s real life wasn’t bad, exactly. But somewhere deep down, the stage had never let go of him — the lights, the roar, ten thousand people singing his name back at him.

So he played. He hadn’t expected to still be playing five years later.

It was, the loading screen said, a dream-life simulation built around one purpose: mending a player’s regrets. The art was exquisite, the sound design world-class, the systems deep, and — miracle of miracles — it never once asked him for money.

The story unfolded in a parallel world, its details all subtly different from reality but its shape nearly identical. Perhaps because Ji-ho had chosen the superstar route, the game’s entertainment industry mirrored the real one almost perfectly — every classic song and drama from the real world existed there too, wearing a different face. But the mirror only reflected up to 2012, the year the game began.

For Ji-ho, playing from 2020, that made him a prophet.

And what was a game for, if not to feel invincible? He’d played the shameless transmigrator, “borrowing” the real world’s biggest hits years before their parallel versions existed, sweeping award after award. From trainee to debut, from Ballad Prince to Genius Songwriter to King of Pop; from Rookie of the Year on the acting side all the way up to Best Actor at the top of television and film. In the game, he had everything.

Tonight — tired as he was — he’d dragged himself out of bed for a reason. Tonight, his character stood nominated for the game world’s highest musical honor: the Heavenly Voice Awards. Their Grammys.

Performance

— the arena lights sweep gold across ten thousand faces —

“And the Song of the Year goes to… ‘Leave the Door Open’ — Ji-ho!”

The hall erupts. The cheering crashes over him like a breaking wave.

Nudged by a friend at his table, the Ji-ho on screen rose and walked slowly toward the stage.

The moment his hands closed around the trophy, the camera cut in tight. Ji-ho could see it clearly: his character’s eyes were rimmed red, right hand white-knuckled around the award — and that face seemed to look straight through the monitor, directly at him. Then the words came, one line after another, soft in his ears:

“Every line you sing with your whole heart — it echoes back, eventually.”

“I made it. How does that feel, watching me?”

“Do you regret giving up? Maybe… if you’d held on just a little longer, you’d have found a completely different life.”

Ji-ho sat in silence. Everything in the game was virtual — he knew that. But the fullness in his chest, that ache of a dream made real, was not.

Did he regret it? He honestly couldn’t say.

But he had imagined it — so many times. If I’d had a decent family. If someone had backed me, and I’d started chasing this earlier. If I’d just held on until now…

Too many ifs breed too many what-ifs.

And what if? What if he’d held on into the streaming era, caught one good break at the right moment — wouldn’t that have opened a completely different, blindingly brilliant life? The thought had lived under his skin for years, gnawing like rot in the bone.

“Remember this feeling. Everyone on their feet, screaming your name.” The figure on screen kept speaking, gaze steady. “Now — here’s your second chance.”

Light detonated across the room, so fierce it was nearly blinding. Ji-ho’s eyes slammed shut; his hand flew up too late.

Ding—! A crisp chime rang out of nowhere.
