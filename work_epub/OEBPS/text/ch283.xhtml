Chapter Two Hundred Eighty-Three

Not More than Taeyeon, and Not Less

Monday · 08:51 Seoul · Yongsan-gu · the hill · the villa, front hall, and afterwards the black glass of the second car three days of shooting in the body, forty-one minutes on the clock, and a house that has become briefly too quiet

Breakfast finished the way an awkward thing finishes: all at once, and with plates.

The house has one table long enough for twelve and this morning four people had arranged themselves along one side of it, which is what a room full of people does when nobody has decided where to sit.

The coffee had been poured by a cook who had been told, correctly, that three of the four at the table would drink it and that the fourth would pretend to. There was a plate of cut fruit in the middle that nobody reached for except Taeyeon, who reached for it four times, and each time her hand went to the same three pieces, and each time the bowl was turned around by somebody else so that the good slices came to her without a word being said about it.

Tiffany ate with her phone face-up beside her elbow, the screen going dark and light and dark again, and put it away twice and got it back twice.

Kim Taeyeon had eaten the egg, the rice, half the soup, and nothing else, and had done all of it in the specific silence of a woman composing a sentence she intended to use later and hoping she would not lose it.

Once, at the corner of the table, their eyes met for less than a second, and both of them went back to their own plates at the same moment, and the noise of that little movement was the loudest thing in the room.

Somebody had left the front door unlocked, which in a house on that hill is a thing the staff comment on in the staff room, and nobody at the table noticed, because the sound of the four of them eating was enough.

There had been eggs, and rice, and the kind of soup that is only ever served in a house with a cook, and for eleven minutes nobody had said anything that mattered. Tiffany had said two things about the weather. Taeyeon had said nothing about anything. The Chairman had asked, once, whether there was going to be enough food on the table for a man who had slept for forty minutes, and had been answered by a glass of water being pushed toward him without a word, which he had taken as a complete reply.

Song Ji-ho went upstairs and came down changed.

He put on the charcoal two-piece, the one cut close enough across the shoulders that it does the work of a sentence before he says one, with the jacket long enough in the body to make the length of him look careless rather than measured. He did up the cufflinks at the counter by the stairs, one, then the other, the small silver things that had been a gift from a man who had wanted something, and he did not hurry, because a man who has slept for forty minutes learns quickly that hurrying is what other people do to him.

Song Ji-ho · going out, Monday no camera on him at all

Suit · charcoal wool, two-button, cut in Seoul and finished in Naples, the sleeve a half centimetre short because he prefers his watch visible and prefers it for a reason he has never once explained

Shirt · white, spread collar, no tie, the top button open on the working assumption that he will be in a car within four minutes

Cufflinks · silver, plain, a gift from a man who has asked him for three things since and been refused all three

Shoes · black, a sole thin enough to hear himself coming, which is the only reason the two women in the hall looked up when he did

He is dressed for a day in which he will be photographed by people who think they are not photographing him, in a building where the lifts have cameras and the loading bay has a man with a clipboard who has already decided that the Chairman is on time.
There is nothing in the outfit for the benefit of a camera, which is the point of it. A man who dresses for a lens wears something he has had approved. He is wearing what he put on while two women were eating in his kitchen, and the top button is open, and nobody in this house has ever once got him to change that.

“Let’s go,” he said, into the room. “I’ll take you both.”

“Wait, wait, wait — my phone is charging!”

Tiffany was already moving, slippers going pa-tak, pa-tak on the floor as she ran for the bedroom, the ponytail behind her swinging twice, catching her on the jaw, and being ignored.

That left the hall with two people in it.

Without Tiffany in the middle of it, Kim Taeyeon finally got her chance.

She pressed her mouth flat, crossed the three steps to him in a straight line, put out one small hand, pale as the inside of a cockle shell, found the soft flesh above his hip through the shirt, and twisted.

“Hssss—!” The air went out of him through his teeth. His face folded. “Noona. Noona, noona, noona—”

“You bastard.” She kept her voice down to the volume of a room with ears in it, and did not relax one syllable of it. “You had this planned since last night, didn’t you. You invited us to drink. You got us drunk on purpose.”

She was genuinely using her hand, and the place she was using it on is a place a man is not built for.

“Ow, ow, ow — there was nothing to it,” he said, working at the small hand with both of his, trying to get it off him while saying, in the same breath, “You were both drunk. What was I supposed to do, carry you back to the dormitory like that?”

“Besides,” he said, “I held you both while you slept. Not one bad thought in my head.”

“And this morning?” She did not let go. She added to it. “Tiffany’s skin. Nice to touch, isn’t it?”

He was being twisted so hard that his teeth were showing, and he stopped fighting at that point, and looked down at her instead.

She had her face up at him, glaring, the brows drawn in, doing everything she could think of to look like a woman who was going to make him pay for it. The trouble was the shape of her: eyes large, the outer corners falling very slightly, so that three parts of every expression she makes are innocent and the other seven are soft, and there is nothing whatever she can do about the arithmetic. Twenty-five years old, and the skin on her cheek is the white of an egg that has just been peeled, with a faint pink under it. The sun came in across the full height of the glass and put a light on the small hairs at her temple, and made them visible, every one of them.

Fierce, in the way that milk is fierce.

The pain stopped, and that was not a decision he made.

He looked down the line of her: the point of her brow, the bridge of her nose, and then the mouth, which had pressed itself shut in anger and had achieved only the opposite. The colour of her lips was pale pink, the pink of a cherry petal at the end of winter when it has been on a branch too long and has gone soft. They were standing far too close for a hall in a house where a third woman was looking for a phone in a bedroom, and he could smell her — the sweetness of a peach taken off the tree that morning, and a little citrus at the edge of it.

Below that, the small piece of her above the collar of her top. The line of her collarbone, showing and not showing, so pale in that light that it looks transparent.

His eyes went hot, and there was no management plan for it.

“Yah.” She felt the change in his face before she saw it, and her hand loosened one degree. “I am having a serious conversation with you. Where are you looking—”

She did not finish it, because an arm came round her waist.

The next second she was inside a warmth with a heartbeat on the other side of it.

His arm closed at her waist and held it there. The waist itself is a joke a stylist made two years ago and nobody has been able to stop repeating since — narrow enough that a man’s hands are a span apart, and through one thin layer of cloth he can feel the temperature of the rest of her. Her hands went flat against his chest by reflex.

“You!” The protest came out in the wrong register, gone soft at the first syllable. “What are you doing — let go, she’ll be out any second—”

He put his head down and came close to her face.

Nose against nose. The smell of her everywhere, and the two breaths mixing.

Her lashes moved. There was a flash of panic in those large, badly-designed eyes, and she stayed exactly where she was through all of it.

“Noona.” His voice came out low and was carrying a smile he had no intention of hiding. “You were right. It was on purpose.”

“Mwo?”

“From the moment I brought you both back here last night, to getting you drunk.” He kept her eyes, and he took his time, one word after another, so that she could not pretend to have misheard. “On purpose.”

Kim Taeyeon stopped.

“And I’ll do it again,” Song Ji-ho said. His thumb moved at her waist, one slow pass through the cloth, and the little heat of it went through her and took the strength out of her knees. “You are mine. And so is she. Neither of you gets out of it, not for a lifetime.”

“You—” She opened her mouth to say something to him and found that there was nothing in her head at all.

There was only a heartbeat, going, loudly enough that she could hear it in her own ears.

He put his mouth to her cheek and kissed it, and then, very lightly, bit it.

“Wait for me at home tonight.”

Low enough that the room did not have to know.

She bit her own lip, turned her face to the other side, and would not look at him. The red went to the roots of her ears and down into her neck, and there it stopped at the collar, and the collar kept it.

She nodded, once, very lightly.

There were footsteps on the stairs at exactly the moment that that meant the most.

Tiffany came down with her phone in her hand and a smile on her face, found the hall with her eyes, found the two of them in it, and the ponytail swung as she came, brisk, pleased, entirely unrepentant. “Found it,” she said. “Found it.”

Kim Taeyeon stood sideways. She did not turn to face her, and the colour that had started at her cheeks had gone the whole way to her ears and was now going down her neck, where the collar was doing its best.

Tiffany looked at her. She looked at the man. She looked back.

“Taeyeon-ah,” she said, taking her time across the floor, drawing the ends of the word out, both eyes curving into crescents. “Why is your face so red?”

Somebody’s heart missed a beat. Somebody’s face stayed where it was.

“Is it?” Taeyeon said. “Probably — I did some stretching just now. Yes. Stretching.”

“Stretching?” Tiffany put her head on one side, and the smile on her did not so much grow as settle, the way something heavy settles. “Oh. Is that what it is.”

She let the last word run on for a while after the sentence had ended, and she moved her eyes from Taeyeon to him and back again, and did not draw a single conclusion out loud.

Song Ji-ho stood by the console with his hands at his sides and a face that could not have been more open if it had been filed.

Being looked at like that made the hairs on her arms get up, and she went to the door quickly and with her head down. “Let’s go. We haven’t got time.”

Tiffany laughed once, did not push it, and followed at her own speed.

Only as she passed him did her fingers go once, lightly, along the back of his hand, with no weight in them at all.

It itched. It went on itching, from the back of his hand, all the way in.

At the gate of the villa the black Mercedes stood with its nose out and the engine off: the exact posture of a car whose driver has been waiting eight minutes and would rather be shot than admit it.

Chae Soo-bin had been in the driver’s seat for four minutes.

She saw the three of them in the mirror before she saw the door open: two women in front of a man, which had been the shape of the morning so far, and which she had decided twice already not to read anything into. She got out, went round, and opened the rear door.

Kim Taeyeon bent and took the seat. Tiffany took the seat. And then the Chairman also took the seat, and folded himself in beside the second of those two, and the car took his weight with a small rock of its suspension.

Chae Soo-bin’s hand stopped on the door for the length of one breath.

Very well, she thought, and closed it, and went back round to her own.

There is a great deal of room in the back of that car. There is not enough of it for three adults who are all aware of the exact position of the others.

Kim Taeyeon had the window on the left. Tiffany had the right, and the man in the middle of her side of her. He settled as though this were the natural arrangement of the world and had been so since the car was designed.

“Yah.” Tiffany pushed at his shoulder. “Sit in front.”

“Since when does a chairman ride in the passenger seat,” he said, not moving one centimetre. “Scoot in.”

She was moved, in the end, by his elbow, and he sat down in the space that that made, and the car rocked again, lightly, once.

The gate opened. The villa went behind them. They came into the morning traffic out of Yongsan and were taken the length of it without being asked, and for a while nobody in the car said a word at all.

Kim Taeyeon looked out of the window. The first three blocks she watched properly; after that the traffic went past without being observed, and the fingers of one hand, thin and green-long, had found the glass of the door and were tapping on it without any instruction from her at all. Once. Twice. Three times.

The car was quiet enough to hear the wipers at the junction, although it was not raining.

Song Ji-ho’s hand behaved itself for the first five minutes.

After that his hand was, entirely by accident, lying on Tiffany’s thigh.

She had her head down over her phone. Her body went rigid in one piece, from the shoulder to the jaw.

Skinny jeans, the dark pair, worn thin at the thigh by a woman who has them in four colours and wears this one the most; the denim pulled tight enough to draw the whole length of the leg, long and round and made of somebody who dances for two hours a day in a room with a mirror in it. His hand lay there. It was warm. It itched.

The tips of her ears went pink, then red. She bit the inside of her lip, kept her head down, and did not move his hand or her leg.

His fingers moved once, the length of a knuckle, and through the thin cloth of her jeans there is no distance at all between one and the other.

The screen of the phone in Tiffany’s lap went through eleven lines of a message that she did not read, and stayed on the eleventh while she looked at it.

The eleventh line stayed on the screen and stopped being a sentence at the point where a hand arrived on her thigh.

It was far too quiet in the back of that car for it to be only the sound of breathing, and every one of the three people in it knew which breathing was being listened to.

In the mirror, Chae Soo-bin looked once. She looked away again so quickly that the movement was closer to a flinch, and both hands went flat on the wheel, and the tips of her nails had gone white at the edges. She took a breath in through her nose, and let it out, and drove.

Concentrate on the road, she told herself, in a voice she had borrowed from somebody else.

The Girls’ Generation dormitory is not the sort of building that photographs well, and at nine in the morning it is the sort of building that nobody outside it should be standing in front of, which is why the car came round to the side and stopped under the stairs.

Kim Taeyeon had her hand on the door handle when Tiffany spoke.

“Daedae,” she said, in a voice arranged for the purpose, pleasant, unhurried. “Go up first. There is something I need to say to him.”

The hand stopped on the handle. Taeyeon turned on the seat and looked at her.

Tiffany smiled back, and there was not a thing in the face to be found.

“…All right.”

The door opened and she got out and bent at the waist to look back into the car, because there is a habit in her of making sure, and today it was going to cost her.

Song Ji-ho was sitting where he sat, and he was looking at her.

Two people, and the distance of a car door between them, and nothing said at all.

Kim Taeyeon opened her mouth, with something in it that arrived too late to be chosen, and closed it again, and bit her lip instead.

There was a reproach in the look. There was also the thing that comes with not wanting to leave it. Everything she could have said was in it and none of it was on her face, and the whole of that was over in a second and a half, and then she turned and ran for the outside stairs, and the unit door took her, and that was that.

The door closed.

“Well,” said Song Ji-ho, turning his head with his hands still in his lap, one corner of his mouth already committed to whatever was coming. “Say it. What is the matter?”

Tiffany said nothing.

She came forward on the seat, slid one hand into the hair at the nape of his neck, put the other flat on his jaw, and kissed him.

Mouth against mouth, with no introduction and no apology in it, the whole of California sun in the way she does a thing she has decided on in the space between two streets.

He was, for the length of one beat, surprised. Then an arm came round her waist and pulled, and she went, and the air in the car, which had been a sensible twenty-two degrees, was not that any more.

After an amount of time that neither of them was measuring in the same way, she let him go. Her forehead came to rest against his.

Her nose against his nose. Breathing, two of it, in the small space.

“This morning,” she said, in a voice that had gone soft at the edges and was still slightly out of breath. “You kissed Taeyeon.”

His eyebrow went up. “You saw the whole of it?”

Tiffany did not take that. She went on with her own.

“One minute fourteen seconds.”

He said nothing.

“I got thirty.” She put a little distance between them, and looked at him straight, the way she looks at a producer when a mix is wrong. There was no laughter left in those eyes and they were bright in a way that is difficult to describe as anything other than a small fire burning somewhere behind them.

She paused. Every word separately.

“Remember it: you still owe me forty-four seconds.”

“I will not take more than Taeyeon takes. But I am not going to want less, either.”

Song Ji-ho looked at her face, at a distance from which the small hairs on her cheek were a line of gold in the window light, with the morning coming through the side glass and laying a rim of it along the side of her jaw, and every one of them visible.

He put his hand out to bring her back.

She was already away from him.

She put her collar straight, took the hair that his hand had disturbed and put it behind her ear with two fingers, and glanced at the front seat as she did it.

In the mirror she met Chae Soo-bin’s eyes.

One second. Two.

Tiffany smiled at her, with her eyes curved, and the whole of the smile was bright, the specific California brightness of a person who intends nothing and is understood anyway.

That man Song Ji-ho, she thought, on the way to the door. Hiring a secretary who looks like that.

There had been, between eleven o’clock last night and eight this morning, a great deal to be embarrassed about, and it was a fact about herself that she had managed to lose sight of an enormous threat for the whole of it.

Little sister, isn’t she a would-be actress. Not working on her craft. Working on how to look after a man.

Tonight, she decided, she would put it into Taeyeon’s ear while they were both lying down, which is where these things are done properly: the chairman needs a different secretary, because this one is too pretty to be an assistant to anybody.

“I’m going up,” she said, to the man in the car, gently, with a smile that had nothing whatever in it.

She was out and up the concrete in four strides, hair swinging at the back of her neck, and the outside stairs took her, and the corridor light came on twice.

The dormitory in the morning is a building of closed doors, and Kim Taeyeon went along the corridor in her socks, with her shoes in one hand, and did not look at the camera in the corner of the ceiling, and did look at it, because it is the kind of thing you cannot stop checking.

In her own room she shut the door, and stood in it for four minutes doing nothing at all.

Then: the mirror on the wardrobe door, and her own face coming to meet her, and the mark on the cheek not there, because it had never been there in the way she had been afraid it was; a little pink, and that is all, and it had come from the pressure of a hand.

She put both hands flat on the cold top of the drawer and looked at the pink and thought: You bit me in a hallway at eight o’clock on a Monday morning.

There were facts in the room that she had to arrange before she could go to a rehearsal: a call at half past, a dress she had said no to, a schedule with her name on it twice, and a man who had told her to wait for him and had then said it in a voice that had made the waiting feel like a thing she had agreed to in writing.

There was a knock at the door, twice, in the rhythm of somebody who has decided to knock rather than open it.

“Noona.” Seohyun’s voice, on the other side, careful and flat. “The car is at nine-fifteen. And there is a picture of your face going round a fan board already, so you may want to see it before you eat something.”

“I am not eating anything,” said Kim Taeyeon to the mirror.

“You said that at seven.”

“Then I will say it twice,” she said, and went to find her socks, and did not find them, and stood in the middle of the floor with one foot up on the bed while she remembered that she had put them down in the corridor.

The door closed.

The car was quiet in the particular way it is quiet when there are two people in it and one of them has something she has already decided to say.

Song Ji-ho sat back against the headrest and pressed his mouth together.

There were two tastes on him, and neither of them had gone.

Taeyeon’s, sweet, the water of a peach in early summer.

Tiffany’s, and the other thing entirely: a rose at the exact point where it has opened all the way and is starting, slightly, to be too much.

He stayed there for three seconds with his eyes on the back of a headrest, thinking about it, and then: “Drive. The office.”

Chae Soo-bin started the car.

All the way across the river neither of them spoke, and the only thing in the cabin was the engine’s low note under the road noise.

She held the wheel with both hands at the top of it, and her insides were like a spice jar that somebody has knocked off a shelf: sour, then bitter, then the hot one, all of it in the wrong order, none of it identifiable.

She looked at him in the mirror, and could not help it.

He was looking out at the city with his brow and nose doing what they do in profile, the line of his jaw clean, the whole of him handsome in the ordinary way, and there was a smile at the corner of his mouth of a man who is remembering something and enjoys it.

Chae Soo-bin felt her chest go tight.

Twenty years old. Famous. Gifted. Powerful. A producer at the top of the trade, the most-watched solo male singer in the country, and at twenty the man who controls a company the size of LOEN.

A person like that is written evidence that heaven plays favourites.

No. That is not what it is. Favouritism has limits, and this one is past them: it is the fact that he can keep this many women in this much order, without one of them shouting, that puts him in a category of his own. He is heaven’s own illegitimate son, and the rest of the country is the paperwork.

The car went out on to the bridge across the Han.

The water under the morning was broken into scales of gold, and the scales shook with the swell, and the traffic ran both ways, and every one of the people in all those cars was going somewhere that they had decided for themselves.

Chae Soo-bin opened her mouth and shut it, and then she said it.

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm?” He came back from the window by way of the mirror, and her eyes were in it.

“Right now,” she said, and pressed her mouth together first, “I would like to ask you a question. As a friend.”

“Ask.”

“Why—” She took the length of a lane to find the words, and chose them the way she chooses everything, one at a time, and tried to keep the voice of her out of it. “Why has it become like this?”

“Like what?”

“This.” She looked straight in front of her. “The way you toy with women’s feelings.”

The sentence was out before she had finished deciding to say it, and only then did she hear how heavy the verb is. There was no way to put it back into her mouth.

She held the wheel with both hands and waited for what came.

What came was not anger.

He laughed, briefly, twice, at a good question rather than at her.

“Toy with?” He turned the phrase over to look at it. There was no mockery in his voice at all; it was doing arithmetic. “You think I am deceiving them. That I am playing with them.”

Chae Soo-bin said nothing, and watched the road.

He let his head go back against the seat and looked forward through the windscreen, and when he spoke it was not entirely to her.

“When Taeyeon got out of this car two minutes ago,” he said, slowly, “did she look like a woman who had been played with?”

Chae Soo-bin saw it: the turn of the head at the door, the look back into the car that she had not been able to keep off his face, the reproach in it, and the part she would not name, and the attachment with no sentence attached to it.

Everything, and the pain of a person who has nowhere to put it.

Nothing whatever of a victim’s.

“And when Tiffany kissed me just now,” he said, “was there on her face the look of somebody being used?”

And then the other one: the small triumph of the smile when she had sat back on the seat, the eyes bright and the mouth doing that thing it does when there is no humiliation in it and no resentment either, only the whole of the greed a person can bring herself to say out loud, and the pride of a woman who has just announced where the boundary of her field is.

Chae Soo-bin could not say anything.

He smiled, a little, and his voice stayed level.

“They know.”

He let the car take a corner, and looked out at a city going the other way.

“Every one of them knows that the others exist. Every one of them knows what I am doing. And every one of them made the choice herself.”

“Right now,” he said, “I am not deceiving anybody.”
