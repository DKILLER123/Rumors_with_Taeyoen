The descent took them through a ceiling of cloud so flat it looked laid, and on the far side of it the peninsula came up grey and low and familiar, the Han a dull ribbon, the whole of it under the kind of September sky that has already given up on summer. Jiyeon had her forehead near the window and her reflection going in and out of the wing.

“Nervous?”

“A little.” She said it the way you admit to a headache: factually, and with the hope of not being made a case out of it. She turned her hand over inside his and held it there, because her fingers had gone cold and were doing a thing they sometimes did in the ninety seconds before a stage door opened, which was trying to count something.

“You don’t have to be,” he said. “Stay on my right side, don’t look at the lenses, and don’t answer anything — not even the polite ones.”

“Then they’ll have both hands full.” He patted her knuckles once, unhurried, the way you reassure a horse at a lorry. “We go through the staff corridor and out the east door. There are three cars and I know which one has the tint.”

The nose came down; the wheels found the same tarmac she had left eleven weeks ago; and the seat-belt sign went off with a small chime that she heard entirely separately from her own heartbeat.

Cabin crew opened the forward door and stood back with their hands folded for the first-class row to go first, and there was no applause, no shout, no announcement, and the quiet of it was worse than any of that would have been, because it meant the noise was all on the other side of a curtain, waiting, the way a crowd waits.

His stride checked, half a beat, once — the stride of a man deciding something at a speed no camera in the hall could catch. What he decided was not to run and not to turn and not to look at the crowd, and it took him the half-beat it took: his arm came across her shoulders, his hand took the far side of her neck, and he walked her out of it at exactly the same pace, head turned slightly toward her, and that was the whole of it. Nothing said. Nobody answered.

Three minutes, from the door to the black glass of the second car, and the door shut, and the corridor behind them went on shouting at a wall.

“Eyes forward, ten seconds, then the van,” said a security man through a radio, and that was the only instruction anybody gave them all day, and it was enough.

What remained was the noise, and the photographs, and thirty sets of hands lowering cameras to look at what they had just got, and what they had just got — the pale jaw, his arm across her, the way she had been carried along like a thing worth stealing — was better than anything they had come for. By eleven that night, the shots were on every portal in the country, graded hard and dark, both of them looking guilty of something, which was the correct temperature for the caption.

INDUSTRY NOTE · WHAT A SILENCE COSTS TO HOLD

There is a reflex in the Korean entertainment business that predates every agency in it: when a member of a large family’s circle is attacked, the family answers within a day, because an unanswered hit is read as permission for the next one. A label that says nothing for thirty-six hours about its own chairman — the man who owns its biggest act and its second-biggest revenue line — is not a label that has lost control of the story. It is a label that has decided the story is going somewhere and has booked a seat.

The people who push a fire for free are good at telling you when they stop being paid for it. They stopped. By Wednesday evening a number of columns that had run hot on Tuesday had been taken down at the author’s request rather than the editor’s, which is the tell, and the accounts that had supplied the phrases for them were quietly editing the words out of their histories.

A boycott is cheap to start and expensive to sustain. What LOEN had bought, at enormous cost and with enormous patience, was not the crowd’s opinion. It was the crowd’s memory of how loudly it had shouted, spent in advance on a story that was going to end differently in six weeks.

Somebody in the planning office in Yeouido had a bad night’s sleep over it and asked, in a message sent at four in the morning to a man he had no reason to be friendly with, whether the chairman’s camp “intended to release anything on the casting before the album is behind schedule.” The reply came back at six, one line long, and was not about the casting.

“Ask your marketing team why a distributor in Busan has ordered eleven thousand more copies than forecast, and then ask me again.”

It was nearly eleven at night at the kerb below the building on Teheran-ro. The driver had been sent home for the weekend he had not had, so it was Chae Soo-bin in the front seat with the engine ticking over and a phone in her hand, and she had been reading for twenty minutes at the lights with her thumb over the brightness, so that the glow would not lift into the rear-view mirror and show the back seat what she was doing.

“Hoejang-nim. Is it really — are you sure it’s all right?”

“No.”

She looked at him in the mirror, and found that he had said it without any drama at all, and had said it to the window.

“There’s a saying,” he said. “Gojin gamrae. The bitter first and the sweet after.”

“…Gojin gamrae.”

“Mm.” He looked at the back of her hand on the gear lever, and at the traffic, and did the arithmetic of the sentence he was about to say and said it anyway, because he had never in his life found a useful way of being kind that didn’t involve one extra step. “Think of it as a spring. You press it down — you take the flat of your hand and you press it until it is on the floor and your knuckles are aching — and it does nothing. It doesn’t argue, it doesn’t leak, it doesn’t make a sound. And the harder it is held, the worse the day is going to be for whoever let go of it.”

“And then it comes back up.”

“That is the whole of the saying, yes.”

He turned back to the window, and in the wet glass the city was a line of lights going past, and the face in it was the face of a man thinking about the expressions of a particular four thousand people on the day the spring comes up, and finding the thought pleasant, which is not a thing a person should find pleasant and is a thing a man can be forgiven for, having paid for it twice.

Soo-bin nodded at the road, understanding about half of it and understanding that the half she had was the useful half.

“Oh, by the way.”

Her name in his mouth at eleven at night, in a car at a light on the ridge road, went through her at a speed she could not account for. By the time he had said “here” and slid something forward over the console into the passenger seat beside her she had already arranged her face into the neutral one and lost, because it was a box.

A jeweller’s box, small, the corner of the paper slightly creased where it had been carried in a coat pocket all day and then one more day, and she took it with both hands the way you take a thing that might not be for you, and opened it, and stopped.

The leaves were tipped and serrated, and the silver was thin enough in one place that the interior light came through it, and the two of them sat in the box looking at her the way they had looked at her on the counter at the co-op in California — exactly as they had looked at her, in fact, and she had looked at them for two full minutes before she had put them back.

“What — what is this.”

“I thought they suited you.” He said it looking out of the window, in the voice of a man discussing a delivery. “So I bought them.”

Her nose stung, and she got it in, and something warm went up her spine from a place she had not known was cold, and she said the first thing she could find, which came out at a volume two notches too high, because it was easier than the alternative.

“If I’ve worked hard, give me a raise! What is this — you can’t — that’s such an ordinary present, honestly, this is — a pair of — how much can that even have been, you know — the richer a man is the meaner he is, everybody knows it, and it’s true, look at you—”

“Ordinary?” She looked round; he had turned his head off the window and had an eyebrow up and the whole tired face of a man doing sums. “Give it back, then.”

“Ya!” The box went to her chest and both her hands came over it like a woman covering a plate at a table of strangers. “Since when does a gift get recalled! That isn’t how anything works!”

“Since the moment it was called ordinary by a person holding it in both hands.”

She turned back to the road with her jaw set and the box in her lap and the two leaves of silver catching the streetlight through the windshield, and she did not say thank you again, because she had said it about four hundred times this week and this one she was going to keep. She had come to California with a suitcase containing two blouses, a rehearsal skirt, and a jar of her mother’s chilli paste, because eleven weeks on a probationary salary does not buy a holiday wardrobe; and she had counted, at the counter, the exact distance between what she had on her card and what was in the basket, and had done the sum and put the basket’s contents back in the right order, and had said nothing to anybody, and had thought about it — which she would not have told anyone, not even here, not even at eleven o’clock with the two people in the world who would not have laughed at her — for the whole flight home.

And he had noticed. Standing at a counter in a foreign country, in the middle of a hundred things he had been doing at the same time, he had noticed a girl looking at a pair of earrings and had bought them and had carried them in his pocket for two days and had put them in her lap in a car in Seongsu-dong in the rain and had said four words about it and looked out of the window again.

And he had thought they suited her. Which is a sentence about two people having the same eyes, and she had been hired to have none of her own.

There is no rule against a small kindness.

That’s what makes it so difficult to be on the receiving end of one from the wrong man — because you know exactly how far you ought to step back, and there is nothing at all to step back from, and the box is already in your hands, and your mouth has stopped being able to hold a straight line, and you can feel it happening in a car, in the rain, in the mirror, at eleven o’clock, all the way up the hill.

Some people will tell you they know the shape of a thing they should not want. They will tell you early, in writing, to themselves, in the way a person files a report nobody asks for — she knew the arithmetic of the room better than anyone in it: the chairman, the actress, the eleven weeks, the probation, the audition hours protected in a contract she had read twice. And still, every now and then, out of nothing at all, a man does a small decent thing, and the heart goes up on its hind legs like a dog that has been told it may.

Up the hill the second car’s headlights came round the bend and the rain stood in the beams for a moment and went out, and nothing in the world had changed: the comments were still up, the brands were still asking questions, and the album had six weeks to go.

In a safe on the ninth floor, a phone with a cracked corner and a square of tape on it sat in a sealed case with a date on the paper beside it, and a long way north of the Han, in a room with the curtains open for the first time since August, a girl who had been believed once, in a corridor, was asleep without the light on.
