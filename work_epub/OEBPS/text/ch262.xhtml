Chapter Two Hundred Sixty-Two

Starship Entertainment: Future Stars, Activate

LOEN Entertainment · executive conference room two acquisitions, two labels, and a screenwriter carrying one of the decade’s defining dramas

“Notify every senior executive. Conference room in fifteen minutes.”

“Ne, Hoejang-nim.”

Fifteen minutes later, LOEN’s leadership team had assembled around the long table.

Ji-ho entered with an even stride. Everyone rose.

“Annyeong haseyo, Hoejang-nim.”

“Sit.”

He took the head chair and surveyed the room.

“I apologize for the short notice. There is an acquisition proposal requiring immediate review.”

CEO Cho Young-chul leaned forward.

“Which target?”

“Lee Kyung-hoo of CJ came to LOEN this morning. I accepted a provisional price of ninety-five billion won for Core Contents Media, subject to full due diligence and final approvals.”

The room broke into startled murmurs.

“CCM?”

“From CJ?”

Cho Young-chul frowned, visibly calculating.

“Sajang-nim, say what you’re thinking.”

“Hoejang-nim, LOEN is already evaluating the purchase of seventy percent of Starship Entertainment.”

He opened a folder.

“At the same time, management and production operations are being reorganized into two independent labels. Closing CCM now could tighten liquidity.”

Starship.

SISTAR’s company—and, in the future Ji-ho remembered, the home of WJSN and IVE. Names flashed through his mind: Jang Wonyoung, An Yujin, Bona, Cheng Xiao, Meng Meiqi.

Then another memory surfaced.

Chae Soo-bin.

Wasn’t she tied to the wider Starship–King Kong structure later?

LOEN’s future acquisition path returned to him: a controlling Starship stake in late 2013, followed years later by King Kong Entertainment and a major actor roster.

Whether every detail of that future remained fixed no longer mattered. If the opportunity was available now, Starship was too valuable to ignore.

“What capital does the Starship transaction require?”

“Our estimate is fifteen to twenty billion won.”

“Cash currently available?”

“A little over one hundred ten billion.”

LOEN’s Melon business generated steady cash flow, but cash on the balance sheet still had to support payroll, platform operations, albums, marketing, and the new screen and trainee divisions.

“Does that include the final settlement from The Girl in the Sun?”

Cho Young-chul looked up.

“The film distribution proceeds?”

“More than thirty-three billion won to LOEN. CJ delivered the statement today.”

“Then no. Treasury has not booked the receipt yet.”

“The transactions are possible,” Ji-ho said, “but we do not empty the company to prove ambition.”

“Stage the closings. Protect album budgets, payroll, Melon operations, and minimum working capital. If diligence reveals hidden liabilities, we reprice or walk.”

Cho Young-chul nodded.

“With those conditions, the cash position can support both.”

Ji-ho looked around the table.

“Objections to opening formal CCM diligence?”

No one objected.

With CCM and Starship, LOEN would hold T-ara and SISTAR—two of the digital era’s strongest girl-group names—alongside Korea’s leading female soloist and its fastest-rising male solo act.

The music chain would deepen overnight.

The meeting adjourned with assignments for legal, finance, HR, rights management, and artist-contract review.

✦ ✦ ✦

Cho Young-chul followed Ji-ho into the corridor.

“Hoejang-nim, the screen division and trainee department are operational.”

“Already?”

“Director Ryu has been relentless. SM’s temporary advisory staff also helped us formalize the trainee curriculum.”

“Should we begin open trainee recruitment?”

Ji-ho slowed.

“Proceed according to the approved plan. Quality over volume.”

“And accelerate both acquisition workstreams.”

“Understood.”

Back in his office, Ji-ho considered the improbable turn.

He had not intended to buy CCM. The company had delivered itself to his door.

T-ara and SISTAR.

Two groups with enormous digital strength.

Thinking of Starship brought Chae Soo-bin back to him—the quiet neighbor with clear eyes and a stubborn determination to survive acting work no one seemed to notice.

They had spoken less since he moved. The difference in their circumstances had grown uncomfortable.

If she truly was under Starship’s umbrella in this timeline…

Ji-ho found the number he had not called in months.

✦ ✦ ✦
Seoul · late-August afternoon a struggling actress hears an old friend’s song

Chae Soo-bin had not been doing well.

She had appeared in a successful film within the novel’s altered timeline, yet public attention had passed around her as if she were part of the scenery. Whether the stage-name transition from Bae Soo-bin had confused recognition or the industry simply had no immediate plan for her, meaningful offers had not followed.

She attended acting classes, worked part-time jobs, and waited.

At the end of one shift, she walked through the humid evening with fatigue pressed into every step. A coffee sounded less like pleasure than survival.

Cool air met her when she opened the café door.

“Eyes, Nose, Lips” played through the speakers.

Soo-bin smiled faintly.

Old neighbor. You changed so much.

He was a star now. A chairman.

She remained an actress whose own industry often failed to recognize her.

Do you still remember me?

She joined the queue.

Her ringtone—“If You”—began almost immediately.

Soo-bin looked at the screen and jolted.

“Ohmo. Ohmo…”

She checked the caller twice, apologized to the people she had startled, and hurried outside.

INCOMING CALL · SONG JI-HO

“Yeoboseyo?”

“Soo-bin-ah. How have you been?”

The voice is unchanged—warm, familiar, and capable of making months of distance disappear in one sentence.

“I’ve been… fine. You?”

“Very well.”

A short silence. Soo-bin searches for a reason he might have called.

“Did you need something?”

“Which agency are you signed with?”

“Starship.”

So the connection exists in this timeline too.

“Let’s meet.”

“What?”

✦ ✦ ✦

Ji-ho encountered Dong-hwan as he left for the appointment.

“Hoejang-nim.”

“Dong-hwan.” Ji-ho touched his shoulder. “Sajang-nim says the trainee system is in good shape.”

Dong-hwan smiled with unaffected pride.

“Thank you.”

Then his expression became apologetic.

“I still haven’t found the right executive assistant or personal secretary.”

Some candidates had approached the role as a route to Ji-ho rather than a profession. Others possessed skills but not the discretion required around an artist-chairman whose personal and corporate lives repeatedly collided. Dong-hwan’s new department left little time for careful screening.

“Don’t rush. Competence can be trained. Discretion cannot.”

“Are you heading out?”

“Meeting an old friend.”

“I’ll drive.”

“All right.”

Private café room the neighbor he left behind

Soo-bin sat opposite Ji-ho with both hands around her cup.

She wore an ivory dress, hair gathered loosely behind her head. Nervous color warmed her cheeks. Whenever she looked up, admiration surfaced before she lowered her eyes again.

Dong-hwan passed the partition, recognized the expression, and continued into the neighboring room without comment.

He had worked beside Ji-ho long enough to recognize affection hidden under careful manners.

“I never expected you to call,” Soo-bin said.

“I didn’t expect to learn you were at Starship.” Ji-ho studied her. “Are you truly doing well?”

She nodded, then shook her head.

“Not really?”

“It’s strange. The film did well, but no one noticed me. I work part-time, attend acting classes, and wait for auditions.”

Embarrassment touched her expression.

“You became famous so quickly. Songs, movies, LOEN… everywhere you go, people look at you.”

Soo-bin’s own path had always been uneven. Projects could succeed around her while recognition went elsewhere. Later, variety appearances would finally help audiences notice the person behind the roles.

“What work are you doing now?”

“A barbecue restaurant. Sometimes flyers. Anything that pays.”

She smiled with quiet determination.

“It’s tiring, but I can support myself.”

Ji-ho listened.

When he first arrived in Korea, they had lived in neighboring apartments. During the dating-scandal chaos, extremists gathered outside his home. Soo-bin was struck in the confusion and fell on the stairs; Ji-ho took her to the hospital.

“Do you remember the night outside the apartment?”

Her eyes brightened.

“How could I forget? You took care of everything. Thank you.”

Her smile faded as she measured the distance between that boy and the chairman before her.

“Back then, we talked and ate together. Now…”

She did not finish.

Admiration had begun as a neighbor’s affection and grown quietly while she watched him climb beyond reach.

In the next room, Dong-hwan formed a practical thought. Soo-bin was discreet, hardworking, and already trusted Ji-ho. She might suit a temporary assistant or creative-liaison role while pursuing acting—if she wanted it. The choice would have to remain hers; stability was not a reason to erase her original ambition.

Ji-ho did not tell Soo-bin about the Starship acquisition. A small, mischievous part of him wanted to see her surprise when the transaction became public.

“Don’t assume the quiet period is the end,” he told her. “Your work will meet the right opportunity eventually.”

Soo-bin nodded firmly.

“I’ll keep trying.”

They spoke a while longer, mostly about the extraordinary year that had separated them. Soo-bin listened closely, gaze following every movement.

When the meeting ended, Ji-ho put on his mask and sunglasses before entering the public café.

Soo-bin followed, reluctant to let the reunion end.

At the bottom of a short step, a distracted woman rounded the corner carrying an iced coffee and an open laptop.

Neither saw the other in time.

The collision sent coffee across Ji-ho’s shirt. The woman lost her footing and landed hard on one knee. Her laptop struck the edge of the step with a dull crack and went black.

Ji-ho immediately reached down.

“Mianhamnida. Are you hurt?”

The woman barely heard him. She seized the laptop and pressed the power button.

Once.

Again.

Nothing.

Color drained from her face.

“Do you know what was on this?”

Panic pushed her voice higher.

“Two days of writing. Twelve hours today without stopping. The first two episodes—everything.”

Dong-hwan arrived quickly, positioning himself between the stranger and Ji-ho without touching her.

“Please step back. Everyone is upset, but grabbing him won’t recover the file.”

Several customers had begun watching.

Ji-ho kept his voice level.

“The collision was both of us. I’ll pay for the computer and professional data recovery. If the storage drive survived, the scripts may still be recoverable.”

The woman’s anger ran out as quickly as it had risen. Exhaustion replaced it.

She wiped her face.

“Mianhamnida. I walked into you. I didn’t sleep last night, and I’ve been writing all day. I panicked.”

“The coffee ruined your clothes too. I’ll pay for cleaning.”

“Forget the shirt. Save the writing first.”

Her willingness to admit fault improved his impression immediately.

“Take it to a specialist before anyone repeatedly powers it on. If recovery fails, contact me.”

Ji-ho turned to leave with Soo-bin and Dong-hwan.

“Wait.”

The woman hurried after them with the damaged laptop against her chest. She produced a business card.

“Are you an actor?”

Dong-hwan almost laughed.

The woman continued before he could answer.

“I’m Park Ji-eun, a television writer. Today was my mistake. If you’re trying to enter drama, I can arrange a small role as an apology.”

Real
Park Ji-eun

Known for Queen of Housewives · My Husband Got a Family

A commercially formidable writer whose sharp dialogue and high-concept romances will later produce My Love from the Star, The Producers, Crash Landing on You, and Queen of Tears.

Ji-ho accepted the card.

“You’re a writer?”

“Yes. I write on this laptop, which is why I lost my mind when it died.”

She gestured toward the café.

“The shop stays open until five in the morning. I came here so I wouldn’t fall asleep at home. I finished the opening episodes just before the accident.”

Soo-bin had been studying her.

“Are you the Park Ji-eun who wrote Queen of Housewives and My Husband Got a Family?”

The writer looked at Soo-bin and brightened.

“That’s me. And you’re beautiful. Are you an actress?”

Soo-bin’s cheeks reddened. The fact that Park Ji-eun did not recognize her still hurt, but she nodded.

Ji-ho repeated the name silently.

Park Ji-eun.

Recognition arrived.

The writer behind My Love from the Star, The Legend of the Blue Sea, Crash Landing on You, and Queen of Tears.

Ji-ho removed his mask and sunglasses.

“Writer Park, annyeong haseyo. Song Ji-ho.”

Her eyes widened.

“Song Ji-ho?”

She looked from his face to the mask in disbelief.

“How did I fail to recognize you? Even covered up, you look like someone a camera should be following.”

Ji-ho smiled and replaced the disguise.

“You said the new script has a title.”

Park Ji-eun was still recovering from the revelation.

“My Love from the Star.”

Ji-ho felt the future shift beneath his feet.

There it was.

The drama that would sweep across Asia.

In Korea’s live-shoot environment, a writer did not need every episode locked before seeking a production partner. Opening scripts, a compelling treatment, and a bankable creative team could begin financing, network, director, and casting discussions while later episodes continued to develop.

Park Ji-eun stood before him holding a damaged laptop that might contain the first two episodes.

“Writer Park.” Ji-ho’s tone became serious. “LOEN is building a screen division and actively seeking projects.”

“If the files are recovered, I would like to read the script and discuss investment, production, and distribution.”

The writer stared at him.

Beside them, Soo-bin held her breath.

One accidental collision had just placed an unknown actress, a new studio, and one of the decade’s defining dramas in the same café doorway.
