Chapter One Hundred Ninety-Nine

How Shall I Punish You, Ji-eun

June 2, 2013 · evening → night KEKU bar · his LOEN office rock and roll for a jazz man — and a wager gleefully re-armed

KEKU. The VIP room.

“Director Kim. A glass, from me.”

“Ji-ho-ssi. No need for ceremony.”

They drank it down together.

“Director Kim — may I ask you something?”

An eyebrow rose; a hand turned palm-up. Proceed.

“You love jazz. Don’t you.”

The pause was nearly invisible — nearly. “You’ve clearly done a great deal of preparation. But what does my taste in music have to do with our negotiation?”

“Everything.” He set his glass down. “Because the soul of jazz is improvisation — free creation inside a framework of rules. And the soul of rock and roll — is breaking the framework.”

He held the older man’s eyes.

“Thirty-some years ago, a band called Queen wrote a song called ‘Bohemian Rhapsody.’ It broke every rule on the books — six minutes long, no chorus, opera fused to folk fused to hard rock. The label executives heard it and said: this can never be a hit single.”

“And Freddie Mercury said: I don’t write hits. I write history.”

His voice picked up heat. “That song became one of the greatest rock recordings ever made — played over a billion times since. And what did it prove? That real creation has never once followed the established rules.”

Kim Sung-hwan’s finger tapped the table — the thinking tell.

“Ji-ho-ssi. You’re using rock-and-roll romanticism to discuss a three-hundred-billion-won transaction?”

“No.” A head-shake. “I’m telling you why only I can make LOEN worth what it’s actually worth.”

He leaned in, serious now.

“SK has held LOEN for eight years and managed it as a ‘digital media asset.’ And the books are lovely — Melon prints money, the stock climbs. But behind those numbers — what did LOEN lose?”

A beat.

“It lost its ambition. It lost the nerve to break the frame. Today’s LOEN is a technically flawless jazz player running the same standards every night — every note correct, and no one in the room stands up to applaud anymore.”

And there — at last — the composed face moved: the mouth tightening, something stung flickering deep in the eyes.

“Do you know what my plan for LOEN was, when I took the division three years ago?” The voice came slow.

“I know.” He sat back down across from him. “A cultural engine for the peninsula — not a music vendor. A production house, film and drama investment, an incubator for independent musicians. A complete creative ecosystem.”

“And the group gave me no resources, and the Fair Trade Commission stood behind me with a stopwatch, and every won of investment crawled through four layers of approval.” For the first time, something pressurized escaped into Kim Sung-hwan’s voice. “So in the end, I optimized the reports. Raised the margin. Made the quarterly deck look pretty.”

“And that’s the whole problem.” He nodded. “LOEN was strapped into the chaebol frame and turned into a MANAGED project. It was never allowed to be a CREATED one.”

He drew out a small signing folio and slid it across the table. On the page, three lines in clean handwriting:

Handwritten · three quotations

“Rock and roll isn’t a genre. It’s a state of spirit.” — Lou Reed

“Being careful is advice for other people.” — Patti Smith

“If it’s too safe, it’s wrong.” — David Bowie

“Director Kim — you admire Miles Davis, yes? He had a famous line: do not fear mistakes. There ARE no wrong notes.”

“What LOEN lacks most right now is exactly that spirit. It’s so terrified of a wrong note that it only plays SAFE ones.”

He pointed at his own chest, and the grin that came with it was almost insolent.

“And I — I AM the wrong note. A twenty-year-old entertainer trying to buy the company he records for. From where you sit, that must look like a magnificent mistake.”

“But the history of rock and roll is WRITTEN in those mistakes. When Elvis Presley moved his hips, the television producers called it indecent. When The Beatles grew their hair, the management companies said they didn’t look like idols. When Nirvana buried the world in dirty guitar noise, the entire record industry said it wouldn’t last.”

He leaned forward, voice dropping low, each word placed like a stone.

“And then Elvis became the King. The Beatles rewrote popular culture. Nirvana buried an era.”

“Director Kim — you can sell LOEN to some investment fund. They’ll optimize the reports, cut the unprofitable divisions, shine Melon up like a showroom car, and flip it in three years for a thirty percent spread.”

“Or…”

He let the word hang in the air.

“You can hand it to someone willing to carry the risk. Willing to break the frame. Willing to believe that inside the wrong note — the next era is hiding.”

The room went long-quiet.

Kim Sung-hwan took off his glasses and polished the lenses with a cloth — slowly, thoroughly, a full minute of it. Then he set them back on his nose.

“Young man. It’s moving, what you say. But this is a three-hundred-billion-won transaction. Not a rock concert.”

“I know. Which is why I brought this.”

He set his phone on the table and pressed play. A drumbeat rolled out — spare, primal, unmistakable: Queen, “We Will Rock You.” He left the volume low, letting the stomp-stomp-clap tremble quietly against the tabletop between them.

“Director Kim. Have you heard the line — rock and roll won’t change the world, but it changes the people who change the world?”

“LOEN doesn’t need to change the world. It needs to change the Korean music industry. And to do that, what it needs isn’t better management. It’s—”

He touched two fingers to his own sternum.

“—the fire in here.”

Kim Sung-hwan said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his eyes had gone somewhere twenty years away.

“Do you know — I had a band once. University. I played keys in the Seoul National jazz society.”

A pause; a small, unpracticed smile.

“Fridays we played a little bar in Hongdae. A dozen people in the audience, maybe. But back then — every time I put my hands on the keys, I felt like I was MAKING something.”

“Then SK. The first suit and tie. The first balance sheet. The first lesson in what ‘return on investment’ means. The keyboard went into its case. The band dissolved. Friday nights became — different lights.”

His voice had gone quiet enough to be talking to himself.

“All these years, I’ve run LOEN well. Shareholders satisfied. Reports clean. Stock steady.”

“But every time I pass those kids on the street — singing, practicing choreography, scribbling songs — I think of that out-of-tune piano in the Hongdae bar.”

He raised his head and looked at the young man across the table.

“You said it right. I DO want LOEN in hands that will treasure it. But there are many ways to treasure a thing. Some treasure its profits. Some treasure its market share. Some treasure the brand.”

“What is it YOU intend to treasure?”

He didn’t answer at once. He thought — actually thought — and then said:

“The songs that haven’t been written yet. The choreography that hasn’t been danced yet. The melody sitting in some high schooler’s notebook right now that might change everything.”

“I want to treasure that out-of-tune piano in the Hongdae bar — not because it plays true, but because it still makes sound.”

“I want to treasure POSSIBILITY, Director Kim. LOEN’s greatest value was never this quarter’s revenue. It’s how many possibilities it can still turn real.”

He stood — not toward the window, but directly before the older man — and put out his hand.

“Give LOEN to me. And I’ll show you: the plans you never got to execute, the ambition the balance sheets buried, the dreams folded up inside that jazz keyboard—”

“None of it disappeared. It’s all just waiting for someone with the nerve to play it again — loud.”

Kim Sung-hwan looked at the offered hand for a long, long moment.

Then he stood. He did not take it.

“You’re naïve.”

“Idealism doesn’t make money.”

He turned, walked several steps toward the door — and stopped, without looking back.

“But this world needs idealists.”

And he was gone.

Ji-ho drained his glass in one motion.

The world was a noisy, grasping place — and still full of pure people. Or people who had been pure once, and remembered where they’d buried it. What had moved Kim Sung-hwan tonight wasn’t rock and roll. It was hearing music again after twenty years of hearing numbers.

He stood, paid, and left.

✦   ✦   ✦

The LOEN tower, early evening — cold white corridor light on polished stone.

He was reaching for his office door when the next one down opened, and Ji-eun stepped out: oversized white tee, pale-blue denim shorts, long straight legs, high ponytail, a few sweat-damp strands stuck to her cheek.

She registered him, paused — and her nose wrinkled.

“You’ve been drinking?”

She came closer and tilted her head back to inspect him — an angle that wasn’t tall enough to threaten anyone and exposed the clean line of her throat above the collar.

“A little.” He rubbed his temple. Genuinely, faintly buzzed.

She stared at him for a few seconds with those round eyes — then walked straight past him, pushed open HIS office door, and let herself in.

He raised an eyebrow and followed.

Thump. The door shut.

She’d installed herself on the sofa, cushion hugged to her chest, legs folded up.

“You’ve felt the atmosphere in the company lately, right?”

He hung his jacket and opened his top two shirt buttons. “What atmosphere?”

“Playing dumb.” Her lip curled. “The whole building knows LOEN’s being sold. SK’s shopping for buyers, the Commission’s deadline keeps getting closer.” She watched his face. “They say several funds are circling. Including that terrifying Starr one. The executives are in meetings all day. Everyone’s rattled.”

He poured himself a glass of water and leaned against the desk edge. “And?”

“AND—” she hugged the cushion tighter, chin settling onto it, “—SOMEBODY once declared, with a completely straight face, that he’d buy LOEN within three years.”

Her head tipped; the ponytail swung.

“It hasn’t even been a year, and the company’s changing owners. And from the look of things — the buyer is definitely not you.”

The tone was a compound: three parts mockery, two parts triumph — and five parts something she hadn’t diagnosed in herself. Concern?

He drank his water and said nothing. She read the silence as confession.

And thought of Sulli. And Taeyeon. Philandering radish. Serves him right.

What came out of her mouth was: “What, drinking because you’re sad? Drowning sorrows?”

He set the glass down, crossed to the sofa, and bent over her — close enough that she could smell faint alcohol woven through his cologne.

“Ji-eun-ah.” A small smile. “You’re that certain the buyer isn’t me?”

Her heart skipped one full beat. Too close. Lash-counting distance. And when did his smile get THIS unfair? Shibal. Dog of a man. What’s the FACE for?

She wrenched her eyes away and manufactured maximum scorn. “What else? A one-year-old artist buys LOEN with WHAT? Your royalties? The Gucci fee?”

Momentum arrived; weeks of accumulated feelings found the exit.

“Ji-ho, I KNOW you’re talented. The songs are great, the face is unfairly good — but in front of CAPITAL that’s worth nothing! LOEN is five hundred billion won. FIVE HUNDRED BILLION. Do you understand what that number means? You couldn’t earn it in a hundred years!”

He rubbed his temples and sat down on the sofa’s other end. She stood immediately — reversing the elevation, hands on hips, insufficient height compensated by maximal posture.

“When you made that bet with me — did you feel COOL? ‘I’ll hold LOEN. Three years. If I fail, do with me as you please.’ WOW. Real chaebol-drama male lead lines.”

She performed his tone back at him, then snorted.

“And now? The company’s being sold to somebody else, the CEO dream is dust — so you go drink?”

She leaned down, nearly nose to nose, and fired the last shot one word at a time.

“Philandering scumbag. Weren’t YOU going to acquire us? Hm?”

He looked at her — eyes bright, cheeks flushed with the joy of combat, mouth curled in triumph.

Enjoying yourself, Ji-eun? Fine. Let’s play.

He let out a sudden, broken laugh — retreated two steps — dropped onto the sofa — and buried his face in both hands, shoulders trembling faintly.

She froze solid.

This was not in any forecast. Denial — expected. Anger — possible. Deflection — likely. But — BREAKING? A grown man — crying?? Have some shame!!

“Hey—” Her voice lost all its edges at once. “Are you… okay?”

No answer. The posture held.

The office went horribly quiet. She bit her lip, crossed over, sat beside him. Her hand rose to pat his shoulder — hovered — retreated.

At last he lowered his hands. The rims of his eyes were red.

From laughing. Half a minute of silent, strangled laughing. Thank god for acting craft.

“You’re right.” The voice came out sanded. “I was too naïve.”

Something twisted in her chest.

“I really did think I could do it.” A bitter head-shake. “Wrote a few hits, landed a few endorsements, worked with a few top artists — and floated. Thought there was nothing in the world I couldn’t reach.”

He looked at her, letting exactly one flicker of fragility through. “Even made that ridiculous bet with you. Looking back — childish.”

Her mouth opened. Her throat had tightened inconveniently.

“Three years to buy LOEN—” a self-mocking laugh. “I must have been insane. You must have been laughing at me this whole time. An arrogant lunatic with no sense of scale.”

“I WASN’T!” It escaped before review.

His eyes came up, disbelieving. She wilted slightly.

“…Fine. At the very beginning, a little.” A pause; her voice dropped. “But then I kept listening to the songs you wrote. You really are talented. So the laughing — stopped.”

And immediately regretted the entire sentence. Why does this sound like CONSOLING him?!

She scrambled for recovery: “What I MEAN is — you’re arrogant, and a philanderer, and you talk enormous nonsense, but — but at least there’s real talent under it. And nerve. Most people never even dare to THINK it.”

It came out magnificently crooked. He nearly lost the entire performance on the spot and had to turn his head.

A moment. Then he turned back, eyes carefully vacant, letting a little light refill them.

“Then — our wager. Does it still stand?”

Her heart rate spiked again.

“OF COURSE it stands!” Pure reflex, instantly re-armored in spite. “What, trying to welsh NOW? YOU’RE the one who said ‘LOEN in three years, or do with me as you please’!”

She folded her arms and looked down at him from the fullest altitude available.

“There’s plenty of time left on the clock. The company being sold to someone else doesn’t void the bet! Don’t even THINK about slipping out!”

“So—” he tilted his head up at her, one corner of his mouth barely lifting, “—even knowing I can’t possibly win anymore… you’ll still hold the full three years?”

“OBVIOUSLY!” Hands on hips. “I want to WATCH you fail. And then— and then punish you PROPERLY! Make you pay for that wandering heart of yours!”

She delivered it through gritted teeth. He rose slowly — and now the elevation reversed again, and the distance had gotten very small.

“Ji-eun-ah,” he said softly. “If — I’m saying IF — I actually pull it off?”

Her pulse went to a gallop. She forced the sneer.

“Impossible.”

“And if?”

“There IS no if!”

“But if there were—” he took one step closer, “—if I really did buy LOEN, inside three years?”

She stepped back. Wall. Nowhere left.

She tipped her head up at him — the deep eyes, the trace of a smile at his mouth, the collarbone visible at his open collar—

“Then—” her voice betrayed her with a small shake, “—then I honor the bet.”

“Honor it how?” He repeated it slowly, like tasting the words. “Specifically?”

Her face reached egg-frying temperature.

“Like— like the wager SAID!” It came out nearly shouted. “I’ll be your lover! SATISFIED?!”

And the instant it left her mouth she heard how it sounded. Like — anticipation?!

He smiled. A real one this time, eyes bending into crescents.

“Good. I’ve noted it down.”

“And when the time comes — I’ll punish YOU properly, too.”

He put weight on the word. A shiver ran through her, and her face went hotter. Punish HOW? Certain private films of certain genres began screening themselves, unbidden — bound wrists? blindfold? …whip? candle wax?? — she physically shuddered. NO. How did he drag me into the gutter with ONE WORD? …Speaking of those films, she’d acquired several new ones. Should she invite Ji-yeon over for a screening? Ji-yeon HAD introduced her to some remarkably spicy—

He watched Ji-eun’s expression migrate somewhere distant and deeply suspect, and took two prudent steps backward, opening the distance.

“It’s late. You should get back to practice.” He crossed to the desk and woke his computer. “You have recording tomorrow, don’t you?”

She surfaced, found him already behaving as if nothing whatsoever had transpired, and stood there blinking.

The distinct sensation of having been PLAYED — with no locatable evidence.

“You—” she began.

“Mm?” He looked up, expression of perfect innocence. “Something else?”

“…Nothing.”

She bit down, spun, and marched out. At the door she stopped and glanced back once: he sat lit by the monitor glow, profile clean-edged, attention already elsewhere.

Philandering radish. Megalomaniac. FRAUD.

She fired the full clip silently and left without another look.

The instant the latch clicked, his head came up, and the smile he’d been suppressing surfaced entire — the satisfied kind.

He picked up his phone and messaged Lee Myung-ha: Spoke with Director Kim privately. His position has softened. Expect accelerated progress.

Not just the rock-and-roll sermon. The Orb, turning its quiet gears underneath.

He set the phone down and looked out at the city — Yeouido’s towers like lit crystal, the 63 Building gilded at its crown, the Han doubling all of it in black water. Beautiful, in the cold way spreadsheets are beautiful.

And against all that cold light, the memory of one small furious woman: hands on hips, chin up, eyes round, ponytail swinging with every volley — an angry rabbit in a white tee.

Unreasonably cute.

And when she finally learned WHO had bought LOEN—

What would that face do? Freeze, first. Then the eyes going wide, the lips parting. Then the full-spectrum flush — cheeks to ears, ears to neck. Then the foot-stamping, and the “you LIED to me, you FRAUD” — and then he could take her chin in two fingers and inquire, pleasantly: “Ji-eun. You wouldn’t want to miss your comeback window this fall… would you?” — and watch the wronged, mutinous, force-smiling negotiation begin.

He was looking forward to it. Enormously.

Then the anticipation was pressed flat by the actual weight of the thing.

An acquisition at this scale was never a romance montage. Diligence: two to three weeks — five years of statements across a dozen subsidiaries, three CPAs cross-checking every consolidated line for buried debt, undisclosed related-party deals, dressed-up margins. Legal: hundreds of thousands of copyright chains to verify; every artist contract screened for change-of-control clauses — mishandle one and inherit a year of litigation. Commercial: Melon’s share bleeding to Genie and Bugs; growth curves, conversion rates, royalty cost structures, platform architecture — every metric to be modeled. And then the REAL negotiation: SK’s veterans packaging every strength as scarcity and every weakness as ‘industry standard’; price volleys — 290 against 260 against 285 — each new number arriving wrapped in fresh comparables. Wildcard bidders. Payment structures. Integration plans. Management architecture. And above all of it: the humans. Kim Sung-hwan was moved — but above him sat the chairman, the vice-chairman, the strategy committee, and none of THEM had a jazz keyboard in the basement. For them the story needed translating: not a sale — a strategic investment; keep twelve percent and ride the upside. Not an exit — an ecosystem: LOEN×SK Telecom, streaming products on next-generation networks. Not the end of a relationship — an upgrade: a standing cultural-sector partner named Ji-ho.

He rubbed his temples, shut the laptop, and stood at the floor-to-ceiling glass looking down at the city that never fully went dark.

A colossal machine, this acquisition. Fine. One gear at a time.

Step by step — to the altitude that would astonish everyone.
