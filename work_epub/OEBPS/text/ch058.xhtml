Chapter Fifty-Eight

The Best Dancer in the Building

He returned to the practice room to find Wendy bouncing up from the floor before the door had finished opening.

“Ji-ho… oppa.” The word still came out sideways. “What did Jung seonsaeng-nim want?”

“Oh? You want to know?”

“Mm! Mm!” Vigorous nodding.

He patted her shoulder with warm, infuriating benevolence.

“Then hurry up and make A-rank.”

And he walked on past, leaving her breathing hard at his back, small fist raised at the ceiling. HATEFUL. I want to HIT him SO much.

Irene was mid-stretch at the barre — side leg raised, heel hooked over the support bar, hand pressed to her pointed toes, torso folding down over the line of her leg with the deep, practiced compression of a career dancer. He didn’t interrupt; he dropped into his own stretching routine nearby and waited.

She unfolded at last, toweled her face, caught her breath, and looked over.

“Fine. Say it. You want me to what?”

He paused mid-stretch, and considered his phrasing.

“Nuna — in your honest assessment, if I’m going to make it as an artist, where am I weakest?”

“Weakest?”

She ran the audit, top to bottom, entirely professional about it. The face: clear-browed, clean-boned, structurally excellent — no notes. The build: lean and taut, rolled sleeves over forearms with visible working muscle — she frowned slightly, hesitating.

“…Your body is, if anything, too good.”

“Being in shape is a FLAW now?” He flexed, aggrieved.

“At our company? Yes.” Seulgi weighed in from her cross-legged seat on the floor. “SM doesn’t allow the men visible muscle definition. Cream-boy standard. Good body, soft silhouette.”

A physique like his would have made JYP weep with joy; SM preferred its idols airbrushed by genetics. At 182 centimeters, the company’s target weight band for him ran 64–68 kilograms. He shook his head, unrepentant.

“I refuse to accept ‘too fit’ as a weakness.”

Three heads nodded in unconditional agreement. On this one point, the committee was unanimous: men should have muscles.

Joo-hyun returned to the audit, chin in hand. Not the face. Not the body. Sang better than she did. Arranged, wrote lyrics, worked like a machine, upward, kind… the list kept returning only assets. Which was normal, she reminded herself — nobody leads with their flaws; early acquaintance is a highlight reel—

“Ji-ho oppa’s WORST thing is obviously his dancing!” Seung-wan announced, clapping. “It’s about the same as mine!”

The DANCING. Of course. She’d watched him drill for weeks — the dancing genuinely lagged. So why hadn’t it been the first thing out of her mouth? …Am I filtering him already? A small ripple crossed her composure. This man might actually be dangerous.

“Slander. I’m CLEARLY better than you.” Even a weak dancer had a floor to defend. “Your dancing is elementary school. Mine is at LEAST middle school.”

“Pfft—” Seul-gi lost it first.

Joo-hyun laughed too, ripple smoothed over, and returned to the point.

“Your dancing is weak — fine. What does that have to do with the bet?”

“We already practice late together most nights. So my request is: in those last hours — coach me. You’re the best dancer among the trainees, full stop; I’ve watched you correct Seung-wan and she improves visibly every week. And if I take first this month and then get flayed by the instructors NEXT month over footwork—” a grimace, “—the humiliation would be fatal.”

“Coach — ME? Coach YOU?” She pointed at her own nose, genuinely startled.

The praise landed — this month’s first-place finisher, rating her dancing highest in the building — and warmed her more than she let show. But recent history had taught her caution around Ji-ho. Not caution about him. Caution about herself. Twice now her heart had bolted; she neither could nor would date — and if the exposure continued at this rate, she wasn’t certain the choice would remain fully hers.

And yet — he’d never once made a move. If anything, he and Seung-wan bickered like a comedy duo. Set romance aside and the ledger was plain: hardworking, disciplined, gifted, and a friendship worth its weight once he debuted. Wanting to fix his weakest subject before the next evaluation was entirely reasonable. And a bet was a bet — she’d lost fair and square. (Fair-ish.)

“…All right. After eleven-thirty, I’ll make time and coach your dance practice.”

Then, pivoting:

“Seung-wan trains with us too.”

“EHHH?!” Seung-wan’s face collapsed. “Eonni, I don’t WANT to practice that late—”

“What do you mean, don’t want!” The face went stern. “Ji-ho just made A. Your entry evaluation scored HIGHER than his, and you’re still sitting at B. Are you telling me you don’t intend to catch him? To PASS him?”

Seung-wan looked at Ji-ho — remembered the oppa extortion in vivid detail — and something ignited. Hmph. So you reached A first. Big deal. I am reaching the DEBUT team first, just watch.

“Eonni. I’ve decided. I’m practicing hard — next evaluation, I’m making A too!”

“That’s more like it.” The smile returned — and with it, quiet satisfaction on a second front: with Seung-wan aboard, plus Seul-gi’s frequent late nights, the numbers were safe. No gossip. No strange atmosphere. Everything accounted for.

Field Notes

Irene would go on to debut and run an entire career virtually without a single dating rumor — an outcome inseparable from who she was: quiet and guarded in private, animated only among the trusted few, meticulous about courtesy and, with it, about boundaries. She was, above all, a person who always knew exactly what she wanted — clear blueprint, career first, risks pruned before they could grow.

A woman like that could be moved — briefly, genuinely — by the right gesture at the right moment. But if the man tried to convert that flutter directly into a relationship, she would refuse on the spot: no early-stage feeling was worth gambling the career for.

The bad news: slow-warmth is nearly uncourtable. The good news: once won, she is loyalty itself — the kind who wouldn’t believe a scandal about you if she heard it sworn on a stack of bibles, because by then she would simply know you better than the rumor did.

For a girl like this, Ji-ho had long since chosen the only viable road: enter through shared ground, keep the contact ordinary and well-measured, apply zero pressure, and be — for all visible purposes — a friend. Not actually a friend, of course. Hence the small, deniable trespasses, each one calibrated to deepen the account without tripping the alarm. And then, someday, a single catalytic event — and the whole relationship would change state at once.
