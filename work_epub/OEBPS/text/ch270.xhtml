Chapter Two Hundred Seventy

SaTzu

JYP Entertainment · executive reception room seven lessons purchased with failure—and three trainees waiting by the exit with an album

Steam curled above two cups of tea.

“The tea is ready, sajang-nim.”

“Thank you. Leave it here.”

Park Jin-young dismissed the staff member and poured for Ji-ho himself.

“You chose tea rather than coffee earlier. Try this.”

Ji-ho lifted the cup but did not drink immediately. He turned it once, letting the fragrance open.

“Tea begins in water and ends in the person drinking it. Some afternoons need nothing more complicated.”

Park stared, then laughed.

“You Korean-Americans become philosophers whenever someone serves you something expensive.”

“Only when the tea deserves help.”

The joke settled the room.

Park leaned back and looked through the window, expression turning serious.

“Let’s discuss America. How do you intend to attack the market?”

“Is this an examination?”

“No. I want to give you experience money cannot buy—because I already paid for it.”

Ji-ho set down the cup and listened.

“In 2003, Bang Si-hyuk and I spent months in the United States trying to sell songs. Almost no one would give us a real hearing.”

Park smiled at his younger self without affection.

“That was when I learned how easily an Asian songwriter can become invisible before the first note plays.”

“In 2007, I established JYP USA in New York. A building, renovation, staff, living costs, promotion—over time, the investment reached roughly fourteen-and-a-half billion won by my own accounting.”

He looked at Ji-ho.

“Do you know what remained?”

“Experience.”

“An expensive mountain of it.”

Park’s humor faded.

“The worst part was moving Wonder Girls away at their domestic peak. ‘Nobody’ reached the Billboard Hot 100. We had real achievements. But the extended U.S. push broke continuity at home, and Girls’ Generation and KARA took the space we left open.”

He sipped his tea.

“So I’m not offering ceremony. These are lessons I bought with a decade and fourteen-and-a-half billion won.”

PARK JIN-YOUNG’S SEVEN RULES FOR ENTERING THE U.S.

1 · Keep the home market alive. Domestic strength is not proof that America will care, but abandoning the home audience removes the foundation supporting the attempt.

2 · Do not export Korean assumptions unchanged. Radio, streaming promotion, touring, press, publishing, labor, housing, and contracts operate through different systems.

3 · Cap the budget and validate in stages. Test singles, audience data, press conversion, and live demand before building permanent infrastructure.

4 · Preserve the strengths of K-pop while localizing presentation. Performance discipline, visual storytelling, emotional clarity, and replayable hooks matter more than imitating an American act.

5 · Hire local expertise. Native English is not a substitute for professionals who understand American radio, publicity, touring, law, and retail relationships.

6 · Borrow a ship before building one. Use established label, distribution, and agency partners before funding an entire overseas operation alone.

7 · Think in years, not opening-week headlines. International careers are accumulated through repeated releases, relationships, and live audiences.

“In America, music is also business. Survive long enough for the dream to become a career.”

Park elaborated on each rule.

The Wonder Girls campaign had taught him that an Asian hit did not reproduce itself automatically in the United States. Audiences had no obligation to recognize success earned elsewhere.

He had also learned not to confuse English translation with localization. A Korean team could understand the music perfectly and still misunderstand radio servicing, tour routing, visa logistics, union rules, housing regulations, or the way financial shocks changed label budgets.

“When the 2008 crisis hit, every label cut risk,” he said. “Who disappears first from the budget? The unfamiliar overseas act.”

“That’s why you set stop-loss points. One single, then another. Small live tests. If the data works, expand. If it doesn’t, you still have a company when you return home.”

Ji-ho nodded.

Park raised a fourth finger.

“And don’t turn yourself into a poor imitation of an American singer.”

“The United States has good melodies. It does not have our exact training culture, stage construction, synchronized performance, or relationship between artist and fandom.”

“‘Tell Me’ and ‘Nobody’ traveled because they were immediate, repeatable, and unmistakably ours—not because they disguised themselves as something else.”

His fifth rule concerned people.

“Your English is native. Fine. What about the team? Who understands American radio formats, booking agents, playlist strategy, tour insurance, press cycles, and local law?”

“If the answer is no one, hire them. Saving money on the wrong person is the fastest way to waste all the rest.”

Then partnerships.

“I tried to own the office, the team, and the operation before demand existed. Wrong order.”

“Work with an established major or distributor first—Universal, Republic, whoever fits the project. Use their routes. Learn the market. Build your own structure only after the structure has work to do.”

Finally, patience.

“I spent ten years and lost a fortune. I still don’t regret trying.”

Bitterness and pride shared the smile that followed.

“Fourteen-and-a-half billion won in losses bought knowledge worth far more—if someone actually uses it.”

“America is a marathon. One release cannot prove you belong there, and one failure cannot prove you don’t.”

Park raised his cup.

“I have never explained all of that to an outsider. I’m telling you because I think you may be able to finish the road.”

The room remained quiet for a moment.

Ji-ho smiled.

“Before today, I came because I owed you a visit.”

He lifted his cup.

“Now I’m grateful I did.”

They drank.

Ji-ho had the system, future songs, and an American childhood that removed several barriers Park had faced. Not every lesson applied to him in the same way.

That did not make the lessons less valuable.

Park had recognized the younger version of his own ambition and chosen, for once, to hand over the map rather than guard it.

✦ ✦ ✦

“I should go.”

Park stood with him.

“You’re busy. I won’t keep you. Visit again.”

“I will.”

As they walked toward the elevator, two heads vanished around the corridor corner.

“He’s leaving,” Sana whispered, tugging Momo’s sleeve. “Sajang-nim is walking him out.”

“I saw.” Momo stumbled after her. “Stop pulling.”

The elevator doors closed.

Sana and Momo sprinted for the stairs.

“Third floor. Hurry.”

“You run too fast.”

Dance shoes hammered down the stairwell beneath suppressed laughter.

JYP headquarters · main entrance three trainees and one treasured album

Park Jin-young shook Ji-ho’s hand at the curb.

“Thank you for coming.”

“Thank you for the honesty.”

Park returned inside.

Ji-ho had just reached the car when hurried footsteps sounded behind him.

“Sunbaenim, please wait.”

He turned.

Sana and Momo ran toward him, breathless from the stairs. Both were still dressed for practice, hair tied back and faces flushed from exertion.

Sana reached him first, bent to catch her breath, then straightened—and forgot every sentence she had prepared.

“Take your time,” Ji-ho said.

The reassurance made her even more nervous.

Momo nudged her lightly.

“Sana. Speak.”

Sana finally brought an album from behind her back: a carefully preserved copy of SUNYE.

“I’m your fan.” Her Korean carried a clear Japanese accent. “I love your music. I watched The Girl in the Sun too, and I cried every time.”

Once the first sentence escaped, the rest arrived together.

“I started following you last year. Your songs helped when training was difficult. I thought I would miss you today, so I took a taxi to the dormitory for the album and came back.”

She finished and seemed startled by her own honesty.

“What’s your name?”

Sana remembered that she had not introduced herself and bowed quickly.

“Minatozaki Sana. I’m a trainee from Japan.”

“Sana.” Ji-ho accepted the album and pen. “A good name.”

SIGNED INSCRIPTION · SUNYE

To Sana—Keep that passion. One day, you will shine.

Song Ji-ho · Ganbatte!

Sana read the Japanese encouragement and blinked rapidly.

“You know Japanese?”

“Enough for one important sentence.”

She held the album as if the cover had become fragile.

Momo bowed next.

“Hirai Momo. I’m also from Japan.”

Then her prepared speech vanished.

Sana rescued her.

“Momo likes you too.”

“I do,” Momo said quickly. “I forgot my album.”

“Then I’ll sign it next time.”

Her eyes brightened.

“Really?”

“Really.”

Sana found one final measure of courage.

“Could we take a photograph?”

“Of course.”

She nearly screamed, caught the sound behind both hands, and pulled Momo into position.

“Wait.”

Another trainee approached at a quick but controlled pace.

Chou Tzuyu stopped, recovered her breath, and bowed.

“Annyeong haseyo, sunbaenim. Chou Tzuyu. I’m a trainee from Taiwan.”

Her voice was quiet, her composure unusually steady for fourteen.

“I’m a fan too. May I ask for an autograph?”

“Certainly.”

She produced another copy of SUNYE.

“Could you write ‘To Tzuyu’?”

SIGNED INSCRIPTION · SUNYE

To Tzuyu—May you remain gentle and remain strong.

Song Ji-ho

Tzuyu read the Mandarin inscription, eyes shining without losing her composure.

She bowed deeply.

“Gamsahamnida.”

“Let’s take one photograph together.”

All three trainees nodded at once.

Sana stood to Ji-ho’s left, Momo to his right, and Tzuyu slightly behind so everyone fit comfortably in frame. The photograph captured four public smiles and three trainees trying very hard not to look overwhelmed.

“I have to go.”

The trainees immediately stepped back and bowed.

“Thank you, sunbaenim.”

“Train well. Debut is only the beginning.”

“Ne. Goodbye.”

Ji-ho entered the car and departed.

The three remained on the pavement, studying the photograph.

Sana’s restraint lasted until the car disappeared.

“I took a picture with him.”

She grabbed Momo’s arm and bounced in place.

“Momo, look. I have the autograph and the photograph.”

“I’m looking. My head is tilted.”

“It’s fine. You look good.”

Tzuyu smiled at the screen.

“Tzuyu,” Sana asked, “you’re a fan too?”

“Mm. I like his songs.”

“My favorite is ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips.’ It always makes me sad.”

“I like ‘Strawberry Gummies.’ The words are warm.”

Recognition passed between them—the relief of finding someone who understood a private enthusiasm.

“Should we exchange contact details?” Sana asked. “We can talk about music.”

“I’d like that.”

Momo raised one hand.

“Me too. And send me the photo.”

They exchanged numbers.

“We’re all from somewhere else,” Sana said. “Tzuyu from Taiwan. Momo and me from Japan.”

Tzuyu nodded.

“It’s difficult sometimes.”

“The language. Missing home,” Sana agreed.

Momo nodded quietly.

Then Sana’s brightness returned.

“But we’ll debut. One day we’ll stand onstage and shine too.”

Tzuyu looked at the inscription on her album.

“Together. Hwaiting.”

The three trainees headed back inside with the photograph between them—a tiny alliance formed before any of them knew the name of the group that would one day bind their futures.
