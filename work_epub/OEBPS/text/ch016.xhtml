Chapter Sixteen

Apartment Hunting

2012 · Spring Jamsil Station — Seoul Lines 2 & 8 where the whole city changes trains

Jamsil Station, where Line 2 crossed Line 8 — one of Gangnam’s great arteries, and permanently, gloriously mobbed. Convenience stores blazed at every exit; street stalls sent up ribbons of irresistible smells; the whole intersection thrummed with the particular electricity of a city that never quite sat down.

Ji-ho stood at the glass front of a realty office, reading the rent listings taped inside the window. The cheapest single room started at 700,000 won.

His brow creased. The company stipend was 500,000 a month. Rent near the station at 700,000 meant bleeding an extra 200,000 monthly — and even on a strict diet of gimbap triangles, his remaining thirty-seven hundred dollars wouldn’t survive that math for long.

Cap it at 400,000. He ran the numbers as he pushed through the door. Now that he was an official trainee, the days of grinding from 8 a.m. to 1 a.m. were behind him — which meant hours had opened up for part-time work. With his skill set, feeding himself had never been the hard part.

Air-conditioning washed over him. The agent on duty rose at once — crisp suit, smile calibrated to the millimeter.

“Welcome! Looking at places nearby? We’ve got plenty of units suited for singles or flat-shares.”

“Here’s what I need. A single room within twenty minutes’ walk of Jamsil Station. Private bathroom — non-negotiable. Kitchen optional, but the place has to be clean. Budget’s three to four hundred thousand a month.”

“Please, have a seat.”

The agent noted it all down, handed over a cup of warm water, and pulled up matching listings on the computer. The photos looked fine — but Ji-ho had rented enough rooms in two lifetimes to know that photos were fiction until proven otherwise. He set down the cup and stood.

“Those six. Show me now, if you can.”

“Of course — one moment, Mr. Song, I’ll grab the keys.”

Keys retrieved, the agent led him outside, swung a leg over a small electric scooter, and off they went on the grand tour.

Reality, as always, delivered. The first unit was semi-basement — damp crawling up the walls, cold in the bones of it. The second fronted a main road that never shut up. The third had a stairwell full of garbage bags and a squadron of fruit flies on patrol… Five rooms, five different flavors of no.

His memory had gone soft around the edges, that was the problem. When he’d first landed in Korea he’d lived out of a jjimjilbang for two entire years, and by 2014 he’d found a decent place out in quiet Seongnam for 200,000. But this was 2012, and apparently a 400,000-won budget couldn’t buy a livable room in Jamsil anymore.

“Mr. Song,” the agent mopped his forehead, “we’re the biggest agency in this district, and those five were genuinely the best matches for your criteria. Seoul’s only so big, and ten million people need to fit inside it — rents around here jump every six months. Perhaps… a slightly higher budget? Or a flat-share?”

Ji-ho weighed it in silence. In his first life he hadn’t moved into a proper downtown neighborhood until 2018; he genuinely hadn’t expected 2012 Jamsil to already bite this hard.

“Under 500,000, then. Single or shared — but no more than three households in a share, and it has to be quiet and clean.”

The concession worked like a key in a lock. The agent’s eyes lit up.

“Two streets over — there’s an excellent two-household share. The previous tenant moved out the day before yesterday, so it should be freshly cleaned. You’re going to like this one, I promise.”

Three minutes by scooter brought them to a well-kept complex. Building 6, elevator to the eighth floor.

“Twenty pyeong, a compact two-bedroom.” The agent fished the key out from under the doormat with the ease of long practice. “The landlord originally fitted it out as a wedding home for his son — then the whole family emigrated, so it went up for rent. It’s less than two years old.”

— one pyeong ≈ 3.3 square meters —

The door opened onto a bright living room, light pouring through the windows. Ji-ho liked it on sight.

“The left room is a young woman — rented through me as well. Works late every day, very quiet. She won’t bother you at all.”

Ji-ho’s glance snagged on the shoe rack by her door — a few pairs of canvas sneakers and trainers, one pair of pink slippers. The floor was spotless; the dining table gleamed like it had been polished that morning. A roommate who kept things clean. Promising.

The agent swung open the right-hand door.

“Master bedroom — private bath, plus a big enclosed balcony. Use it as a breakfast nook, dry your laundry, whatever you like. Huge wardrobe, and the washer, air conditioner, and fridge are all included.”

Ji-ho inspected every corner the way he’d learned to — and the place was genuinely getting to him. New, clean, bright; far enough from the noise to actually sleep at night. He ran the washing machine, opened the taps, checked the water pressure.

“How far to the station?”

“Eight to ten minutes on foot.” The agent rattled it off like a proud parent. “There’s a produce market fifty meters from the complex gate. The shared kitchen has two gas burners — cooking for yourself is no problem. Exercise equipment on the grounds, and security here is strict.”

A beat. Then, voice dropping to a conspiratorial hush:

“And the young lady next door? Gorgeous. Single, too — currently.”

Ji-ho didn’t bite. Beautiful women? I work in a building full of them. But the apartment — the apartment he wanted. Maybe the previous five disasters had been staged specifically to make this one shine; he wouldn’t put the old sales trick past the man. Either way, it worked.

“Rent?”

“Six hundred thousand a month. One month deposit, three months up front.”

A hundred thousand over budget. Ji-ho turned for the door.

“Utilities, building management, and waste fees all included!”

He stopped. Ran the math at speed — those fees came to at least 100,000–150,000 a month, which priced the actual rent around 450,000. Inside the line.

He turned back with the first smile of the entire house hunt.

“Let’s sign.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Two days of leave meant zero time to waste. Contract signed, Ji-ho executed the move with military efficiency — his entire life fit in one large suitcase anyway. He checked out of the hostel and moved straight in.

Luggage down, he hit the neighborhood mart for a mop, a broom, ramyeon, dishes — the full settler’s kit — then scrubbed the room top to bottom three times over until he was drenched in sweat. A hot shower, a pot of ramyeon to quiet his stomach, and then one last task.

He set a box of steamed red-bean rice cake outside the left-hand door, with a sticky note on top:

On the door · Unit 802, left room

I’m your new neighbor — moved in today. A small housewarming gift. Please take care of me!

On the Peninsula, red beans carried old, warm meanings — a first hello between neighbors, a wish for harmony under a shared roof, a quiet charm against bad luck in a new home. Sharing rice cake when you moved in was the courteous way of saying: we’ll be seeing each other; let’s be good to each other. He’d learned the custom the hard way, twelve years deep in this country, and tonight — for the first time — he got to be the one leaving the box at the door.
