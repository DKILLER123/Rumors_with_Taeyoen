Chapter Ten

The Conqueror’s Instinct

Ji-ho knew this mechanic like the lines of his own palm — in Dream Life, the Romance Module was one of the game’s load-bearing pillars.

What he had never, in any timeline, expected — was to trigger “indirect kiss” with Irene.

Wait. Does that mean—? His gaze sank, very slowly, to the coffee cup in his hands. She drank from this one?

Twelve years on the Peninsula had taught him the local wiring. Among people who were close, Koreans thought nothing of sharing a drink, spearing food off each other’s plates, passing spoons around — the entire category Westerners filed under “indirect kiss” barely registered here. Anyone who’d watched enough K-dramas and variety shows knew it well.

And in fairness, Irene hadn’t done it on purpose. While sweetening the coffees she’d taken a habitual taste-test sip, her right hand had naturally picked that cup back up — and that was the hand that had offered it.

But Ji-ho had grown up in Los Angeles, where sharing a cup meant something — and no amount of years abroad had fully rewired that. His mind snagged on it, gently, insistently.

He stole a glance at her, and something subtle rippled through his chest.

She was, objectively, breathtaking. Lips like cherry blossom still glossed from the coffee; skin fine and luminous, misted with a visible sheen of effort, radiating something unmistakably, distractingly alive. Not tall — but in Ji-ho’s considerable experience, that exact height made for the most comfortable embrace in the world.

And… Bunny Girl. His imagination, entirely without authorization, began rendering Irene into the outfit spec. Ssss— okay. That is a lethal image. File that away. Far away.

For a certain kind of man, the oldest dream in the book ran exactly this way: conquer the world first, then walk through its gardens. In this game, the Romance Module’s weight was self-evident — a binding’s Celebration Pack could rocket a career forward, and every trait carried by an exceptional partner meant another massive upgrade. A brilliant partner fed the spirit and sweetened the life; and in the ugliest ledger of male psychology, the caliber of the woman beside you was the world’s receipt for your money, power, and name. The conqueror’s instinct, itemized.

Irene — glacial elegance, a face off a museum wall, and (his observational data was, by now, extensive) a figure with generosity and restraint in all the correct places. Add the future: she was destined for genuine superstardom. A combination like that would put gravity on any man, and Ji-ho was no monk.

But he was clear-eyed about the order of operations. The career came first. Build something real, and everything else in life had a way of arriving on its own schedule.

Seeing her already lost in practice again, he set down the coffee and pushed himself upright. Today’s training load had blown past every sane limit hours ago — but the human body kept strange reserves for the stubborn.

No. Absolutely not. I am NOT losing an endurance contest tonight — and I’m doing it right here in your room. I will politely outlast you if it kills me.

In truth, trainees shared rooms all the time, often in crowds — and Irene found she didn’t actually mind the company. Alone in a big empty practice room at night, she had a well-documented habit of scaring herself.

So they trained on, two sealed worlds in one mirror, disturbing each other not at all.

At 4 a.m. — finally — she stopped, breath coming rough. Her clothes had soaked through long ago and clung to her, tracing every line of a figure that would someday launch a thousand magazine covers. She bent to pick up her jacket, gathered her things, and — at the door — glanced back once.

The new kid was still going. Fully submerged. It was the first time in her life Irene had ever met someone who out-worked her.

She had joined SM in 2009, through an open audition. Born in 1991, she’d been eighteen when she walked in — the same age as Ji-ho now. In an industry where kids entered agencies at fourteen, fifteen, or younger, eighteen was unambiguously late, and she’d arrived with no training at all; what she’d had was a face that stopped traffic. (Ji-ho, with his face, would have walked into SM years ago too — if not for the small matter of an American passport.)

What she also had was a competitive streak with teeth. Surrounded by trainees younger and better-trained, she had chosen the only strategy available: soak herself in the practice room, hour after obsessive hour, and let sheer volume close the gap. The endless drilling numbed the fear, too — the ever-present terror of being cut — and that was how she’d survived the pressure-cooker years. From the day she entered SM, the combination of that face and that ferocity had made Irene a legend of the trainee floors.

Tonight, for once, she’d met her match — some rookie even crazier than she was. Let’s see how long you last, new kid.

Ji-ho registered her leaving and did not stop. He still owed the counter ten minutes.

Ten minutes later:

Won. Finally. WON.

Ji-ho went down like a felled tree, flat on his back on the floorboards, every individual muscle filing its own formal complaint, sweat still raining off him.

If Irene pulled another 4 a.m. tomorrow, he was genuinely going to have to reconsider this entire quest. Rewards were precious; being alive was more so. The system restored the body — it could not restore the sleep. Up at 6:30, at the company by 7:00, training until 4:00 the next morning… a young body could absorb one night like that. A habit of them would grind anyone into powder.

Bed. Now.

As it turned out, all that worry was wasted. Irene was human too: most nights she stopped at 1 a.m. Only on days when her choreography had gone wrong — when that ferocious pride of hers demanded blood — did she stay until four, correcting the error one repetition at a time until it surrendered.
