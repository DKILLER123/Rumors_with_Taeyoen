Chapter One Hundred Ninety-Seven

Joo-hyun’s Tenderness

May 30, 2013 Yeouido — SHIN & KIM Law Offices · the hillside villa the war chest opens, the war room assembles — and one long night before the battle

May 30. Yeouido, Seoul’s financial heart — towers on towers, chaebol headquarters, top-tier firms, international banks, and custom-suited people moving fast between all of them.

He stepped from the black sedan in a deep-gray bespoke suit that made the architecture look underdressed.

“Ji-ho — you’re sure you don’t want me up there with you?” Ryu Dong-hwan leaned anxiously out the window.

“I’m sure.” A settling smile over the shoulder. “Wait for me here.”

He walked into the tower housing SHIN & KIM, unhurried.

The elevator opened on the thirty-second floor into a lobby of dark walnut, hand-knotted Belgian wool underfoot swallowing every footstep, and one full wall of hardware: Korea Law Firm of the Year; Chambers Asia-Pacific Band 1; Legal 500 top-tier; global M&A alliance platinum member — each plaque engraved with year and deed.

The receptionist — thirty-ish, ivory suit, immaculate — rose, and something startled crossed her professional calm as she recognized the face. It lasted half a second.

“Mr. Ji-ho. Welcome. Attorney Lee Myung-ha is waiting in conference room three.”

Conference room three was a full-height glass wall over the Han River, water burning with noon light, the 63 Building standing off in the distance like a drawn silver blade.

Three lawyers rose. The lead — gold-rimmed glasses, scholarly, early forties — extended a hand.

“Mr. Song. A pleasure. Lee Myung-ha, partner, M&A practice. My team — Attorney Kim Min-ji, Attorney Park Sung-jun.”

Handshakes; seats; and no small talk whatsoever.

“You said on the phone you wish to retain us for an acquisition — the target being LOEN Entertainment?”

“Yes.”

A beat of silence. The two junior lawyers exchanged a glance whose meaning required no translation.

“Mr. Song,” Kim Min-ji began, with maximal diplomacy, “first — thank you for your trust. But before formal discussion, we need to confirm some basics. Are you aware of LOEN’s current market capitalization?”

“Roughly five hundred billion won, per public data.” He’d plainly done the reading. “The Fair Trade Commission requires SK to cut its stake below twenty percent; they currently hold 67.6 — meaning at minimum 47.6 percent must be sold.” He leaned forward, hands folded. “That block should price around two hundred forty billion. Given the stake I actually want, three hundred billion is the sane budget.”

Park Sung-jun’s stylus paused mid-stroke. This entertainer knew the numbers AND the regulatory clock behind them.

“You’ve done your homework,” Lee Myung-ha said, adjusting his glasses. “But candidly — three hundred billion won is not small. You’re a famous artist, yes, but personal assets—”

He didn’t finish. He didn’t need to. Can you actually pay?

Entirely expected. A one-year-old star announcing he’d buy a half-trillion-won company should sound like a daydream to any competent professional. He didn’t bristle. He smiled, drew a black folder from his briefcase — Italian leather, faint smell of tannery — and slid it to the table’s center.

“Attorney Lee’s doubts are reasonable. Please verify this first. Then we talk.”

DOCUMENT FILE

presented May 30, 2013 · SHIN & KIM conference room 3

DOMICILECayman Islands · registered capital $100,000,000 · shareholders: confidential · beneficial owner: confidential

POANotarized power of attorney appointing Song Ji-ho sole plenipotentiary representative in Korea — full authority over investment, M&A, and daily operations

FUNDSCitibank certificate of deposit balance: USD 420,000,000 — approximately ₩460 billion

The room went pin-drop quiet.

Kim Min-ji took the folder and checked seal grain and anti-forgery marks with a jeweler’s patience. Park Sung-jun pulled the Cayman registry on his laptop — shareholders sealed, entity real. Five minutes; two synchronized nods to the partner.

When Lee Myung-ha looked up again, the recalibration was total: not an entertainer with a fantasy — a capital player with a stage name. No wonder the Gucci and L’Oréal contracts. No wonder the collaborator list read Taeyeon, IU, Sulli. There’d been an engine under the hood the whole time.

“Mr. Song.” His voice had acquired formality and his spine an extra vertebra. “To confirm: you are formally retaining us for the acquisition of LOEN?”

“Yes. Minimum target: the fifty-five percent in SK’s hands, plus as much of the minority float as practical. Final position: at least sixty percent.”

Park Sung-jun’s fingers were already flying. “At a three-hundred-billion budget for sixty percent, we’d need roughly ten percent over market — and with the FTC deadline, SK’s motivated, but competing bidders could push the premium—”

“I’ll pay a rational premium.” He cut through evenly. “But if the team lands sixty percent inside three hundred billion, I pay a bonus: twenty percent of every won saved.” A beat. “One hard condition. The deal closes before September.”

Kim Min-ji’s pen was sprinting. Lee Myung-ha drew a breath, leaned in, hands interlocked — his known tell before large decisions. “Mr. Song. You understand what this instruction means? This will be the year’s biggest acquisition in Korean entertainment. Possibly an industry earthquake.”

“I know.” The smile carried more calm than the birthdate justified. “That’s why I came to the best firm on the peninsula.”

Lee Myung-ha stood and squared his jacket. “One moment. The managing partner needs to hear this.”

He left at speed. Kim Min-ji refilled the coffee — fresh-ground Blue Mountain. Park Sung-jun probed, intelligently: “If I may — your post-acquisition plans? Integration, I mean.”

A smart question: bottom-checking him and the deal’s survival odds in one motion.

He sipped, then answered without notes: Melon’s share bleeding to Genie and Bugs; the necessary platform rebuild; user-experience debt — point after point, and with each one Park’s eyes went a shade brighter. Not an amateur’s generalities. A diagnosis from someone who’d read the charts.

Five minutes later Lee Myung-ha returned — behind him, a silver-haired man of about sixty, three-piece bespoke, pocket square in a presidential fold, authority arriving two steps ahead of him.

“Mr. Song — our managing partner, Attorney Choi Byung-hun.”

A legend of the Korean bar: the Samsung succession case, the Hyundai split, SK’s own offshore acquisitions — casebook classics, all of them.

The handshake was broad and calloused at the pen-grip. “Young man. You’ve surprised me.”

“You flatter me, Attorney Choi.”

Choi took the head seat and skipped the pleasantries. “Attorney Lee has briefed me. SHIN & KIM accepts the engagement.” A nod to Kim Min-ji, who projected the fee schedule.

ENGAGEMENT — STANDARD DOMESTIC M&A FEE STRUCTURE
Base counsel fee — 0.3% of transaction value (at ₩300B)₩900M
Success fee on closing — 0.2% of transaction value₩600M

One point eight billion won — roughly the net of two top international endorsements.

“Acceptable.”

No hesitation. Something like appreciation crossed Choi Byung-hun’s eyes. Money AND decisiveness. In a negotiation, the second was the rarer commodity.

“Additionally—” he continued, “—I want speed. Due diligence starting today; a letter of intent drafted within three days and delivered to SK.”

“Three DAYS?” Park Sung-jun winced. “Diligence normally runs a week minimum — LOEN has a dozen subsidiaries and affiliates, the financials, the copyright contracts, the artist agreements—”

“Rush fee. Twenty million won extra — and the LOI on SK’s desk in three days.” He looked at Choi. “Time is money. You know that better than I do, Attorney Choi.”

Choi weighed it a moment. Nodded.

“Done.” Lee Myung-ha gaveled it with a palm. “We stand up a twelve-person task force today — diligence starts this afternoon. Attorney Kim: coordinate tax and IP. Attorney Park: assemble the diligence unit.”

“Understood.” In unison.

Two hours locked the remaining details. His requirements were surgical: absolute confidentiality — the buyer publicly styled “an international investment vehicle,” his name sealed until closing; the timetable — diligence May 30, LOI June 2, formal negotiation mid-June, close before September; the structure — Dreamland Capital acquiring through a Korean SPV for tax and compliance cleanliness; and a six-month post-closing integration mandate covering management transition.

Lee Myung-ha noted each item and, privately, kept revising the same assessment upward: every requirement cut exactly on the joint.

Signatures; a five-hundred-million-won retainer wired.

“A pleasure working with you.” Choi shook his hand again. “The team stands up immediately, on your timetable.”

“Thank you. And — one additional requirement.”

“Name it.”

“When the LOI is delivered — I go with it.” He held Choi’s eyes. “I want to sit across from whoever runs LOEN inside SK. Face to face.”

The room paused.

“Mr. Song,” Lee Myung-ha frowned, “at the LOI stage the buyer customarily stays invisible. We act with full authority—”

“I know the custom. This one can’t run on custom.” He leaned in, hands folded — and the posture carried its own pressure. “You’ll give me flawless law and a flawless structure. But one task belongs to me alone.” A beat, each word set down separately: “Convincing them to hand LOEN’s future — to me.”

Choi Byung-hun’s gaze changed. He re-examined the young man: twenty years old, a top-tier artist, prodigiously gifted — all previously known. What was newly visible was ambition with the nerve to match, several sizes older than the face.

“You understand what this costs?” Choi said slowly. “The moment you appear, your identity is blown. SK will know who’s buying.”

“I never intended to hide from SK — only from the press, for now.”

“Why? Exposure adds variables. It could hurt the negotiation.”

“Because astonishment is leverage.” His eyes went sharp. “When SK’s executives discover the buyer isn’t some faceless offshore fund but the artist currently printing LOEN’s money — what’s their first thought?”

Lee Myung-ha caught it instantly. “They re-price you. Not just artist value — the mystery capital behind you.”

“Right. And the second thought: sell to me, and they keep both the cash AND the core asset. Sell to anyone else — that asset walks, and LOEN’s valuation walks with it.”

Kim Min-ji was writing fast, eyes bright. “That’s a live negotiation lever — your retention directly moves their price.”

“And more than that.” He kept going. “Appearing in person transmits one more signal: I intend to win this — and I can RUN this. Attorney Choi, you called it the year’s biggest entertainment acquisition. Then let it be legendary outright: a twenty-year-old artist buying the company he records for. That story carries its own gravity. For SK — a future second shareholder earning more from my operation than from any fund’s cost-cutting, from an artist with LOEN blood in him. For LOEN—” a pause, and the voice settled, certain, “—it means a new era with a founding date.”

Silence, wall to wall.

These three had processed hundreds of buyers: chaebol heirs, offshore funds, industrial giants. A buyer who treated the acquisition itself as a performance, with every beat blocked in advance — that was a first.

Choi Byung-hun was quiet a long moment. Then he laughed — genuine, unguarded.

“Mr. Song. I begin to see how you got this far this fast.” He rose, hand extended. “Agreed. In three days we’ll arrange your meeting with the head of SK’s digital media division — Executive Director Kim Sung-hwan.”

“Thank you.”

“One caution.” Choi’s tone went dry and serious. “Kim Sung-hwan is among SK’s youngest executives — division head at forty-five, famously sharp, famously difficult. Expect hard, oblique questions engineered to find your floor.”

“I’ll be ready.”

“Good.” Choi clapped his shoulder — an intimacy that visibly startled his own partners. “You’ve chosen the best team on the peninsula. Believe that.”

“I do. It’s why I’m here.”

✦   ✦   ✦

One p.m. Back at the car, Ryu Dong-hwan visibly decompressed.

“Ji-ho — what business do you have with a law firm? For THAT long?” The worry was naked. “You’re not — planning to terminate your contract?”

It was the only hypothesis his manager’s world offered for a firm of that caliber.

He buckled in, smiling. “Hyung. You’ll know soon. It’s a good thing.”

“A good thing?” Half-convinced, fully aware pressing was useless. “Where to now?”

“The company. When does film promotion start?”

Work mode: instant. The tablet came out. “The Girl in the Sun campaign opens June 1. First wave: production stills and set-diary teasers on the official channels. June 3: your account posts stills and shooting reflections, synced with Sulli’s. June 5: production conference at the Shilla — director and leads, full press. Then two weeks of variety — Running Man and Infinite Challenge — theater stage-greeting tours, fan signings, the couple-concept pictorial, and sit-downs with Cine21 and Movie Week.”

He nodded along. The domestic release pipeline ran mercifully fast: ratings review in five to seven working days, thirty at worst; a pure-love film with no VFX, a 7:1 shooting ratio, and an edit Kwak had substantially finished; CJ’s distribution channels already in the family. July release, at the latest.

As the car dropped into the LOEN garage, his phone buzzed — Lee Myung-ha: team assembled — three M&A lawyers, two tax, two antitrust, two IP, two corporate, one PR consultant; first project meeting 4 p.m., remote access available.

He replied: I’ll attend. And send me a full file on SK’s digital media division and Executive Director Kim Sung-hwan — career, temperament, negotiation style, personal interests.

“Understood. Two hours.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Four p.m., his LOEN office: twelve faces in a video grid, all suits and firm-branded backdrops.

The hour ran like machinery. Diligence lead: five years of filings being pulled, non-public financials requested, priority on Melon’s monopoly exposure, copyright chain integrity, artist contracts — first report inside forty-eight hours. Valuation, under Kim Min-ji: DCF, comparables, precedent transactions — price-band recommendation by June 2. Structure, under Park Sung-jun: Korean SPV, Dreamland 100% — tax-efficient, clean funds trail, simple integration. And strategy — a young attorney named Lee Soo-yeon, rimless glasses, scalpel eyes — pulling up a photo of a square-faced man with a severe mouth.

“Not his preference—” He repeated it, fingers tapping the desk. “Meaning he might want the RIGHT buyer. Not merely the highest one.”

“Possibly. It needs testing.”

Lee Myung-ha closed the session: “Mr. Song — for the meeting we’ll prepare a full script, Q&A trees, and floor positions. But the live variable is you appearing at all. We can’t fully model their response.”

“Understood. You handle the professional layer. The HUMAN layer — leave to me.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Nine p.m., the hillside villa — the living-room lamps still warm.

Joo-hyun rose from the sofa in a pale-pink silk robe, hair loose on her shoulders, the sash tied carelessly enough to leave collarbones and a pale line of throat on the record, skin pearled under the lamp light, bare face immaculate.

“Busy day?” She held out his slippers.

“Mm. Preparing something big.” He rubbed her hair, fingers sliding through the soft of it. “I’ll tell you in a few days.”

She didn’t press. “However busy — you eat properly. I kept soup warm. Kitchen.”

In a world running on angles and interest, her particular unconditional gentleness kept startling him with its worth.

After dinner, instead of their usual film, he went straight to the study — where SHIN & KIM’s afternoon delivery already stood in drifts: a fifty-page SK organizational chart; five years of LOEN financials; digital-content market analyses; the antitrust compendium; and Kim Sung-hwan’s full file, down to university club records and conference papers.

He made coffee and read like an actor building a role — not the numbers first, but the man. The career arc: crisis postings, every one landed clean. The project record: rigorous models, exhaustive risk memos. The public voice: precise, logical — and, every so often, a jazz lyric smuggled in as metaphor. The sparse social trail: a handful of followed jazz musicians; a few reposts.

By two a.m., the last folder closed, and the man stood assembled in his head: a rationalist with an artist locked in the basement; a proud operator still bleeding quietly over an unbuilt cathedral; an executor forced to sell the thing he’d meant to raise.

Then the strategy wrote itself. Don’t sell him a price. Sell him a succession — the vision he never got to build, handed to someone who could. Not a sale. A transfer of the flame.

He opened the laptop and drafted — not clause language, but the other kind:

Draft · talking points (not for counsel review)

Director Kim — I know what LOEN meant to you. Three years ago you planned to make it SK’s cultural flagship. I remember that vision. Now strategy and regulators force your hand, and I understand what that costs. But LOEN shouldn’t be an asset a chaebol holds. It should be an engine — and Melon a bridge between creators and listeners, not a tollbooth. You can be the founder of all of that — not the seller. The one who passed it to someone who understood. That someone is me.

Halfway through, the study door eased open. Joo-hyun came in with hot milk, the silk moving with her, hair slightly sleep-mussed, lips pressed in soft concern.

“Still not sleeping?”

“Soon.”

He drew her down onto his lap instead. She settled without resistance, warm and gardenia-scented, fingers idly finding his shirt buttons.

“Joo-hyun-ah. A question.” His chin rested on her hair. “If you had something you treasured — and for reasons beyond you, you had to let it go — what kind of hands would you want it to land in?”

She thought about it, lamplight making the profile faintly unreal.

“Hands that… truly knew how to treasure it.”

“Even if those hands didn’t bid the highest?”

“Mm.” A small nod, softer still. “Because some things aren’t priced in money. Like — like your birthday present to me.”

Her hand rose to the star pendant at her throat.

“It might not be the most expensive thing. But you knew what it meant — knew I love blue, love simple lines. So to me it’s priceless.” She turned. “Why do you ask?”

He kissed her hair. “Because in three days, I have to convince a man that I’M the one who knows how to treasure something.”

She didn’t know the referent. She knew the look — the one he wore writing songs, the one he wore rehearsing until form broke and re-set.

“I believe you.” Her fingers traced his cheek. “You’ve always been — the one who treasures things. The stage. The fans. Me.”

He tightened his arms around her, her warmth and heartbeat doing what no strategy memo could.

She studied the tiredness in his face and ached with it — the film, and now whatever enormous thing he wasn’t saying.

“I wish I could help somehow,” she said, self-reproach creeping in at the edges.

He looked at her a long moment — and the late hour, the warmth of her, the gardenia — and leaned to her ear with a suggestion, low.

Her ears went scarlet in a single frame, the color running down her neck.

“You— don’t even THINK about it!”

The pinch she delivered had no force in it at all. He sighed, theatrical, defeated. “All right. Go sleep, then. I’ll work a while longer.”

He turned back to the screen, brow refolding around the strategy. And she stood there watching his profile in the lamplight — the man carrying more than she could see, grinding himself down for it — and bit her lip through a long private war.

Then, face burning, in a voice barely findable:

“Just— just once.”

The rest of that hour belongs to the two of them — and to a study lamp that kept its own counsel.

What can be recorded is this: the tension went out of his shoulders by degrees; the strategy documents lay unread for a while; and at some point he closed his eyes, one hand moving gently through her hair, and said, very quietly, “Thank you.”

For the tenderness. For the understanding. For being, on the eve of the war, the one place that wasn’t a battlefield.

Afterward, the study light burned a while longer over the SK files.

Fair Winds humming in his blood; the system’s power braided into his own intent. There would be turns and shocks and variables no model caught. It didn’t matter. He had the system, the capital, the foreknowledge, the team — and the people who treasured him.

Three days. SK Group headquarters.

See you on the field.
