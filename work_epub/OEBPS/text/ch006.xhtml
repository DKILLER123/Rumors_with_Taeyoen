Chapter Six

The Grind

Eight a.m. sharp, class began.

New provisional trainees trained a minimum of thirteen hours a day — dance, vocals, rap, body conditioning, the full battery. In the early phase everyone took everything, in large shared classes, in shared practice rooms. Ji-ho did a quick head count: a dozen or so in this one.

A rookie’s first dance class was never real choreography. It was “assessment and fundamentals” — root-testing, potential-mapping, foundation-pouring.

The dance instructor opened with the law of the land, face carved out of granite.

“Four new faces this round, so listen up. In my class you wear clothes you can dance in. Nothing baggy, nothing dragging — it kills your lines and it will get someone hurt. And time. Time matters here. Come late, you take the punishment, or you stand at the wall and watch other people get better than you. Your choice.”

Then the four newcomers were told to introduce themselves and declare their physical condition — prior training, old injuries — so the intensity could be tailored.

When his turn came, Ji-ho drew a breath and spoke with easy composure.

“Hello, everyone. My name is Ji-ho — I’m Korean-American, from Los Angeles. No formal dance training, no old injuries. Please take care of me.”

He dipped into a small bow and let a polite smile follow it.

Placement came next. No surprises: the new kids went to the back row and the edges, where they could shadow the senior trainees and absorb the rhythm of the room. Ji-ho set his feet, swept his eyes once across the mirrors — and found no familiar faces. So none of this dozen makes it, in the end. The thought landed heavier than he expected.

Warm-up ran thirty to forty minutes. The instructor demonstrated while narrating.

“Stretching is not optional. Shoulders, neck, waist, core, legs, every joint — all the way through, every time. Skip it and the injury finds you, not the other way around.”

He came down off the platform and threaded between the trainees, correcting angles one body at a time. Ji-ho followed every instruction with monkish focus, stretching each muscle group awake, feeling his eighteen-year-old body come online like a machine fresh off the assembly line.

Then basic conditioning: jumping jacks, high knees, planks, one station after another. Ji-ho knew exactly what this layer was — the bedrock everything brutal would later be built on — and gave it nothing less than everything. Sweat ran off his forehead and darkened his hairline, but his eyes only steadied, every rep placed with care, endurance and coordination both showing clean.

Posture and form took the next forty to fifty minutes.

“Chin up, chest open, core locked! Stage posture is built now, in this room, today — I don’t want to see one more rounded spine!”

Ji-ho adjusted, memorized, adjusted again.

Basic steps followed — jazz fundamentals, hip isolation, arm articulation. The instructor broke each movement down to the bone and pointed patiently at the engine of it:

“Watch — the hip groove drives from the core. You don’t just wag the hips. Power comes from the center; that’s what makes it strong and makes it beautiful.”

Ji-ho listened with total attention, drilling the motion over and over, hunting for the subtle shift in where the force was born.

Rhythm training, too — the instructor put on a bare drum loop.

“Clap on the beat. Step on the beat. Build the sensitivity now — if you can’t even hold tempo, you’ll be rushing or dragging counts for the rest of your career.”

Ji-ho chased the drum, coaxing his body into the pocket. He knew better than most what living outside the beat cost a performer.

Last came a short combination — a simple three-to-eight-count phrase, high repetition, no tricky transitions, built purely to test mimicry and learning speed. Ji-ho’s eyes never left the demonstration, filing away every detail down to the fingers.

When the groups presented, his went up in turn. One breath in — then he let the freshly learned phrase flow out of him, smooth end to end. He could feel the instructor’s gaze tracking him, measuring absorption, coordination, ceiling. He didn’t flinch from it. He leaned in.

Afterward, the corrections came down one by one. His read:

“Ji-ho. There’s some foundation in there, but you’re stiff — wooden. Basic steps on repeat at home, stretch those ligaments, and attack your weak points. Every day.”

He nodded and filed away every word. The truth was, he had studied dance hard when he first landed on the Peninsula — before the rejections piled up and he let it all rust.

The instructor closed with the schedule ahead and one heavily underlined word — practice — the subtext unmistakable: underperform, and the door was right there. Ji-ho understood perfectly. This was a war of attrition, and something in his chest had already caught fire. If you want to shine in front of people, you bleed where they can’t see you.

He wiped his forehead, rested twenty minutes, and the next block began — vocals, rap, conditioning, one after another. There was supposed to be an hour of language class as well, but a native English speaker with flawless Korean had nothing left to learn in that room. He was excused.

✦   ✦   ✦

19:00.

His hoodie had soaked through, dried stiff, and soaked through again — a damp, clammy misery against his skin. Note to self: bring spare clothes. Plural.

He was halfway to the door, dreaming of a hot shower, when a voice caught him.

“Ji-ho!”

He turned — the vocal coach, Kim Mi-yeon. He snapped into a proper bow.

“Kim seonsaeng-nim. Good evening.”

She took in his sweat-flattened hair and nodded, smiling, switching to English for his benefit.

“I’ve been hearing things. You practice like your life depends on it. That’s good.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re the seed of this intake — the face and the voice are both far above the line. So your evaluation is being moved up. I’ll notify you of the exact date separately.”

“Starting today, after the group classes end, you’ll do two extra hours of vocal training with me. Every day. Any problem with that?”

“None at all!” The answer came out like a hammer strike. “Thank you, seonsaeng-nim. Truly.”

Kim Mi-yeon taught the way surgeons operate — precise, methodical, wholly present. She began by mapping him completely: range, timbre, breath capacity, existing foundation, and where he dreamed of ending up. Only then did she build the curriculum — cut to his measurements exactly.
