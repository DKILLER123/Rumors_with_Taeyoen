Chapter Sixty-Three

The Contrarian Heart

The 2012 Taeyeon hadn’t yet become the gym devotee of later years — no trained-in abs, just a stomach flat when standing and honestly, softly human when fully at ease in a chair. Any ordinary girl caught mid-tummy by a man she liked would want to combust. For Taeyeon, combustion was insufficient; she wanted teleportation. Hands over burning cheeks, she spun for the door—

—and a hand caught hers.

“Nuna. Where are you going? Weren’t you dying to hear the new song? The demo won’t record itself.”

She stopped, face like a peach blossom at full flush.

“Then— then let go. And no more weird behavior. There’s a CAMERA in here.”

“Business. Now.”

…So without a camera, weird behavior would be permitted? He wisely did not ask. The dome camera in the corner existed for equipment protection — nobody pulled footage unless something got broken — but its supervisory presence was, tonight, apparently useful to everyone.

He released her, guided her into the chair by the shoulders, and settled the headphones over her ears.

“Something I’ve been writing for a while.”

Performance

— playback: the intro opens on a steady alternation of quarter and eighth notes, hi-hat and feather-light snare cutting in — a pulse built deliberately to mimic a heartbeat: restrained, insistent — Her eyes flick up at him, already startled. Why can this man ALWAYS write an opening that grabs you by the collar? He sets the lyric sheet in front of her. Drums fold into clean broken-chord piano — simple, bright-toned, the harmonic skeleton laid bare — establishing that fresh, feeling-forward palette.

She began humming along, tracking the melody down the page — and somewhere in the second system, the words started registering as content.

It was a song about exactly one thing: two people stranded in the space before a beginning. Mine-but-not-mine, yours-but-not-yours; the maddening arithmetic of a relationship that behaves like love and refuses to say the word; the plea to stop playing dense, stop hiding behind tiredness, and just say it plainly.

This is… an ambiguity song. HE wrote it. Which means the woman in this song is—

Her pulse picked up speed. Her mouth went inexplicably dry.

Field Notes

The arrangement, as she heard it: no dense sections, no impact-chasing — atmosphere-driven from bar one, everything serving the voices, breathing room deliberately left everywhere. Structure as subtext: the spare arrangement was the unspoken distance between two people; the level tempo and reined-in drums were a heartbeat under control; the occasional bass slide, guitar harmonic, synth glint — those were the flutters that get past the guard.

A subtraction aesthetic — piano and guitar at the core, electronics only as seasoning — abandoning K-pop’s usual maximalism to build a room where nothing exists except two voices and the thing they aren’t saying. It was, note for note, engineered for a listener exactly like Taeyeon — and one listen in, she was gone.

How does he write like this? How is he THIS— he took the thing between us and made it a SONG, and the song is this good, and the words and the arrangement agree with each other perfectly, and—

The lyric sheet was trembling faintly in her hands. Because in her understanding — in the only understanding available to her — this was a song Ji-ho had written to her. And against that, no woman’s composure held.

“Ji-ho, you—”

He lifted the headphones off her ears, glanced once — meaningfully — at the camera dome, and smiled with visible regret at the timing of institutions.

“Nuna. Shall we run it through once?”

She followed his glance, packed the flutter down under her ribs, and nodded.

“…Okay.”

They stepped into the booth together and dialed in the equipment. She prepared with total seriousness, pretty face drawn tight with focus.

Performance

— his opening verse: that warm, dense timbre on a confession of unexplained restlessness — of a heart that hasn’t changed and can’t say so — Then her entrance: the wide range, the low notes weighted with a faint ache — a voice alone in an empty room, circling the same unanswerable question. Piano answers guitar; male line answers female line; the whole arrangement is itself a conversation neither party will finish. Verse by verse they trade the song’s accusations — stop locking me in your heart and ignoring me, stop hiding behind “I’m tired,” say the actual word — and the duet renders the entire arc of an almost-relationship: probe, approach, restraint, surrender. By the last phrase, the booth is very quiet, and neither of them is entirely performing.

She stood with her eyes shut, breath quick, refusing to look at him — something rising in her chest and being pinned down by main force, over and over.

“Nuna.” His voice broke the silence gently. “The song. What’s your honest verdict?”

The verdict was OBVIOUS. Her ear was excellent — one pass through a song and she could price it within a decimal. Excellent, and also brutal: this was, after all, the woman famously unmoved by “Gee,” allergic to empty-calorie hooks — but look at what she chose whenever she sang solo, and her actual taste was plain. This song she loved. And the man who had folded their private static into three minutes of it — her heart was doing embarrassing things about him.

But Taeyeon’s tongue had a firmware bug, documented and incurable: the closer the person, the harder the contrarian lock. In front of strangers, fine. In front of him — the want-to-praise and the refuse-to-praise collided head-on, and what fell out of the wreckage was:

“Mm. It’s okay.”

“…Just ‘okay’?”
