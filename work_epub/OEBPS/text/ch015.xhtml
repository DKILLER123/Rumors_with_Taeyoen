Chapter Fifteen

Professional-Grade Ukulele

He let the word hang, then turned it over with meaning, eyes steady on Ji-ho.

“…If your performance is exceptional enough, the company will weigh your potential before official debut and optimize your terms accordingly.”

File that under “things companies say.” The overwhelming majority of trainees had zero leverage at debut. There was exactly one exception to the rule, and Ji-ho knew it by heart: the singer-songwriter.

In every country on earth, the biggest money in music never went to the singer — it went to the company and the producer. But the Peninsula had a peculiarity: piracy barely existed here, which meant that for an artist who wrote, royalties weren’t a bonus. They were the long, stable, compounding core of a fortune.

A gifted young soloist with a storyteller’s voice, an exacting creative instinct, and a playful intelligence that rarely misses anything.

Park Chae-young — Rosé — parlayed four Grammy nominations into everything from a presidential commendation to global brand deals: a self-reinforcing commercial engine.

Park Jin-young. Psy. Kang Hee-gun. Jeon So-yeon… Set aside every other title they held — on writing credits and copyrights alone, each commanded standing and income most idols never touch.

Every agency on the Peninsula treasures the ones who can write. Show the gift, and the contract terms have a way of finding you.

Ji-ho’s raw Composition stat was nothing to brag about — yet. But with the system in his corner and a decade of future hits on call, his roadmap had been fixed from day one: solo debut, singer-songwriter lane, the whole “young genius” architecture. Which was exactly why the brutal trainee terms didn’t especially move him. Plenty of trainees signed here and finished elsewhere anyway.

His eyes dropped to the final section — the stipend.

SM Trainee Stipend · Monthly
Domestic trainees₩90,000 (~$80)
Overseas trainees (new)₩500,000 (~$440)
Pre-debut team members₩700,000 (~$620)

Half a million won a month covered a frugal single life with a little left over for small pleasures — or savings. Good enough. No more questions. He picked up the pen and signed the last page in clean, practiced strokes.

Ryu Seok-min collected one copy with visible satisfaction and slid the other across.

“Welcome to the SM family. From tomorrow, you attend all scheduled training. Your course plan and assignments will come through your designated instructor.”

“Thank you for the opportunity.” Ji-ho rose and bowed, precise as always. “One request — I’d like two days’ leave. I need to find an apartment.”

A considering pause.

“My honest advice is the dormitory. Cheaper, and it builds the habit of communal living. If you make a pre-debut team later, the dorm becomes mandatory anyway.”

“Thank you, but no.”

Flat refusal. Dorms existed to forge group chemistry and simplify management — sensible policy for future boy-group members. But Ji-ho had no intention of debuting in a group, and a man who opened glowing screens out of thin air had excellent reasons to want a door that locked. Ryu Seok-min didn’t push; in the early phase, housing wasn’t compulsory.

The sunlight was perfect when he stepped out of the SM building.

He stood at the curb, looked back once at the structure that warehoused so many thousands of dreams, and let out a long, slow breath. The road ahead promised nothing easy. But the first step — the step twelve years in the making — was behind him now.

Grapefruit still ghosted faintly at the edge of memory; “Hwaiting!” still rang somewhere behind his ear. The corner of his mouth curled. He flagged a taxi and dissolved into the churning river of Seoul traffic.

In an instant, whole libraries unpacked themselves inside his skull — ukulele technique in its entirety, and the full architecture of advanced rap: writing, flow, breath mapping, stage delivery.

The ukulele: guitar’s small, sunny cousin — four nylon strings to the guitar’s six, painless on the fingertips, chords a child could learn in an afternoon. A sound built of sweetness, warmth, and mischief — tailor-made for bright songs and acoustic charm. For an entertainer, it was a quietly lethal tool: fast to deploy, adorable on camera, perfect for variety shows and short clips, a heat-seeking missile aimed directly at the female audience’s collective heart.

And rap — rap needed no sales pitch. It was the industry’s reliable detonator: one hard verse and a room full of strangers became fans on the spot.

But the crown of it all: the writing. He could start drafting his own verses now — film them, post them, and pour concrete under the “young songwriting genius” persona he’d been architecting from day one.

Every brutal hour this week — worth it. Every single one. God, this feeling. Getting stronger is the best drug on the market.

Cheating for a day feels great. Cheating forever feels better.

In the rearview mirror, the taxi driver studied the teenager grinning helplessly at nothing in his back seat. Lottery winner? In this traffic? Kids these days.

“Young man, traffic’s bad up ahead. Jamsil’s going to take thirty minutes at least.”

Ji-ho descended from the high of the settlement screen, watched the river of brake lights crawl outside the window, and found that today, even gridlock was beautiful.

“No problem at all, seonsaeng-nim. I’m in no hurry today.”

Today’s mission: an apartment with an honest price tag.

SM sat in Apgujeong, Gangnam — core-district rents there were open robbery. But twelve years on the Peninsula had taught Ji-ho where the value hid: the secondary streets ringing the core, full of goshiwons and compact single rooms at 200,000–400,000 won a month — tiny, but complete, and within a short walk or a couple of subway stops of the company. Trainee-perfect.

Better still: the neighboring districts, Samseong and Jamsil. A few subway stations out from Apgujeong, dramatically cheaper, stocked with small single and shared apartments — 300,000–500,000 won for a single room, some with modest kitchens attached. Cook your own meals, save real money, keep the commute humane. Weighing it all, Jamsil won on points.

Seoul’s weekday traffic performed exactly as advertised: the promised thirty minutes to Jamsil became forty-five. Should’ve taken the subway. That’s what euphoria does to a man’s planning.

He paid the fare, thanked the driver, pushed open the door, and stepped out.
