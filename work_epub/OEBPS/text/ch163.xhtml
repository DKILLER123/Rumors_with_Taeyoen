Chapter One Hundred Sixty-Three

Apink — “NoNoNo”

January 15, 2013 · Afternoon LOEN Entertainment — the conference room two demos, and a commission from CUBE

By the fifteenth, Seoul’s snow had been pushed into gray-white banks along the roadsides, and the air ran cold and clean.

In LOEN’s conference room, the heating worked hard while three people sat very still: Kim Si-won, Ryu Dong-hwan, and the newly arrived Lee Hyun-woo — all just finishing their second listen to the two demos he’d brought.

The first ran cinematic and surging — dream-pop textures with real chart teeth. The second built like a story being told: a low conversational verse climbing, stage by stage, into a full-throated release that hit the sternum. Neither sounded like a B-side. Both sounded like flagships.

Silence held the room.

Kim Si-won’s fingers drummed without his permission. Dong-hwan opened his mouth and closed it again. Lee Hyun-woo stared at the player with the expression of a man revising every plan he’d written that week.

This meeting was supposed to be about a film’s INSERT SONGS. He just put two Western-market warheads on the table. At twenty.

“Ji-ho-ya.” Dong-hwan found his voice first, disbelief still in it. “I know writing comes easy to you. But these two — for your FIRST film’s theme and insert? Isn’t that a little… extravagant? And you’re not even singing the first one.”

He cradled his coffee, steam softening the excessive face.

“Not extravagant. The film needs real music behind it — and these two are also the sounding line for my second-half plan.”

“Sounding line? You mean… the Western market?” Hyun-woo looked up. Instinct said counsel caution; the melody still ringing in his ears overruled the instinct.

“Yes. After The Girl in the Sun opens, my center of gravity moves back to music. These songs are step one.”

“The songs are exceptional. And the singers?” Kim Si-won cut to it.

“The first goes to Tiffany. The second is a duet — Taeyeon and me.”

“Why them? If we’re testing the West, why not go straight to Western artists? Bigger channels, bigger reach.”

“Walk a demo to a Western A-lister today and we won’t get past the manager’s assistant.” The smile stayed mild. “We need a springboard — internationally recognizable AND able to sing the material to death. Girls’ Generation has global recognition. Tiffany’s American — native English, bright presence; the first song’s emotional register fits her exactly.”

“And Taeyeon — she’s my sunbae from the SM years. Her tone and technique can carry that duet’s whole narrative arc. She’s someone I trust completely.”

Cards on the table: the relationships were close and the logic was airtight, and LOEN — famously loose-reined with artists, doubly so with its crown asset — nodded it through.

“Understood.” Hyun-woo pushed up his glasses, already recalibrating. “If there’s a firm overseas expansion in the second half, the whole promotional architecture shifts. I’d suggest—”

The meeting turned to machinery: shoot schedules, pre-release publicity, fan events, endorsements, and protected creative time for the score. It ran efficient; his calendar came out balanced — exposure secured, composition hours defended.

✦   ✦   ✦

Afterward, in the corridor, Kim Si-won caught him before the elevator.

“One more thing. CUBE Entertainment sent a commission — they want you to write the title track for Apink’s third mini-album.”

“Apink?”

He turned the name over. The innocent-concept group — debuted a while now, never quite ignited. Yet.

“The terms—” Kim Si-won handed over a summary sheet.

CUBE ENTERTAINMENT · COMMISSION TERMS
Composition fee₩100,000,000
Digital revenue share15%
Album sales share10%
Performance revenue5%
Karaoke licensing50%
Overseas publishing30%

A five-percent performance share for an outside writer was open-handed by any industry standard — the number itself an admission of how much CUBE needed this to work.

“I’ll take it. What’s their situation?”

“Internal turbulence, from what I hear — one member’s conflict with the company has gone nearly public; she may leave. And the hiatus has run long. This comeback is close to a last stand for them.” He paused. “Auditions for the film start next week — meet the Apink members before then. Talk concept, get a read on each of them. We’ll host it here; I’ll arrange it.”

✦   ✦   ✦
Three days later LOEN — the conference room six girls, one last stand

He arrived early, notebook open to the sparse public record and one penciled word: healing.

A soft knock, and six girls filed in behind their leader — and the room seemed to brighten a full stop by their arrival.

Park Cho-rong first: delicate features, skin nearly translucent, the particular warm steadiness leaders grow into. Spotting him, surprise flashed and resolved into a contained smile; she nudged Yoon Bo-mi with an elbow — “it’s really him.” Bo-mi promptly went pink, being the shy-wired one, fingers finding her hem, eyes taking their measurements of him strictly by periphery.

“WAH! Ji-ho-ssi!” Jung Eun-ji’s clear voice snapped the shyness like a wishbone. She stepped up with a full-wattage grin. “I’ve seen you at the music shows a hundred times — first time in person! I’m a FAN!”

The room’s temperature rose to normal on her energy alone. Son Na-eun followed her in — cool-toned, sweet-featured, distance built into the posture; she offered a polite nod, betrayed only by the faint pink at her ear-tips. Kim Nam-joo watched him with bright, curious eyes over an unmistakable seam of stubbornness. Oh Ha-young held the tail of the line, calm, watching everything.

At Cho-rong’s cue they bowed in unison.

“Annyeonghaseyo, we are Apink!”

“Hello, Apink. Ji-ho. Today’s just a conversation about music — please relax.”

They settled. He didn’t open with his own ideas; he opened with the leader.

“Cho-rong-ssi. I’ve read the initial plan — ‘healing’ is a strong direction. You know your members best. What should Apink’s healing SOUND like?”

She met his eyes and gave it several honest seconds.

“We think… like afternoon sunlight. Or the first green shoots in early spring. Clean, natural, carrying a little warmth and a little hope. The melody doesn’t need to be complicated — but it has to be sincere. The kind that touches people gently.”

The signature dimples appeared with the smile, quiet and disarming. He wrote as she spoke, nodded, and turned — deliberately gently — to the girl still holding her own hem.

“Bo-mi-ssi. No pressure. I’d love your read on the album — especially how you understand your own voice. It helps me make your parts shine.”

She looked up, caught the encouragement, and went a shade pinker.

“I— my voice might not be the powerful kind. But maybe… it suits the delicate passages. The soft ones. Like Cho-rong eonni said — the kind that makes the listener’s heart go quiet.”

“Delicacy and softness ARE power.” He meant it, and wrote: sensitive, interior, narrative.

“Ji-ho-ssi! Me, I’m the OPPOSITE!” Eun-ji had waited exactly as long as she was capable of waiting. “This voice, this personality — give me the bright parts! The ones that make people want to jump up and shake their problems off!”

The gesture that accompanied it cracked Nam-joo up beside her.

“Eun-ji-ssi’s energy could carry a stadium. That voice and that grin are weapons — I’ll build sections that fire them properly.”

He turned to Na-eun.

“Na-eun-ssi reads unlike anyone else — cool and sweet at once, like a painting. What kind of melody or lyric expresses you best, in your own view?”

Unused to direct questions, she took a moment, lashes lowered.

“My voice is… pale, maybe. I can’t describe it well. But I’d want it to feel clean. Comfortable. Like wind passing.”

“Clean comfort. Like wind.” He wrote it verbatim. “That texture is rarer than you think.”

Nam-joo straightened her spine, eyes forward.

“I want my parts to show what’s underneath the sweetness — the stubbornness. Healing doesn’t have to be soft. It can have a spine.”

“Tenacity under tenderness. Extremely memorable.”

Ha-young closed it out, composed beyond her years.

“For the healing concept overall — I think the tempo shouldn’t rush. Flowing, unhurried. Let the listener sink in slowly.”

He shut the notebook and looked around the table at six young faces wearing hope over pressure.

“Thank you — all of it’s noted. Clean, natural, warm, delicate, bright, tenacious, flowing. Those are my compass points now.”

They nodded, and the trust in the room was real — and so was the weight under it. Fourteen months of silence. An internal conflict edging into the press. A comeback that could not miss. The company had bet its biggest check on the writer in front of them — the one fresh off handing Girls’ Generation a perfect score. Every one of them was strung tight with wanting it.

“Music is a bridge,” he said, slower, steady. “I’ll take everything you’ve told me and write a song that belongs to Apink — and we’ll make sure people hear it. Together.”

✦   ✦   ✦

When the door closed behind them, he reopened the notebook, looked at the keyword list — and the answer was already standing there, fully formed, from the other timeline.

“NoNoNo.” The bright synth intro that grabbed on the first bar; the chorus that installed itself permanently; the hand-choreography a nation would copy; sunlight and hope engineered into three and a half minutes. Distinct enough to cut through the era’s escalating concepts, warm enough to close the distance instantly. In the other timeline it had been the turn of Apink’s whole fate — the key that unlocked national-girl-group status, the first act of the innocent trilogy. Here, at their most desperate hour, it would simply do it again.

He drew a circle around the word “healing,” and the smile arrived on its own.

And after this one: “Mr. Chu.” “LUV.” The whole trilogy, waiting in the vault. Money and goodwill, walking to my door and knocking politely.
