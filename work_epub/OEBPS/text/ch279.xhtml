The elevator was glass on three sides, which had seemed like a good idea in the building drawings and was, in September, a mild act of exposure: the whole of Mapo going about its business below a man who had been on every portal front page for thirty hours. Ji-ho stood facing out of it with his hands in his pockets and his reflection laid over the city, and behind him Chae Soo-bin held two folders against her chest and did not look at the floor indicator once, because looking at it was what a person did when they wanted a thing to be over.

It stopped where it always stopped, one door short of the executive floor, and the doors opened onto a small lobby with a plant in it and a man standing beside the plant as though he had been installed there.

“Hoejang-nim. Welcome back.” Lee Hyun-woo inclined his head, straightened, and did not let the second half of his sentence arrive at the same speed as the first. “Sajang-nim is already in the conference room. And — there is a guest from CJ.”

Ji-ho’s foot paused on the threshold. Not surprise, exactly. The sound of a name he had expected on a different day.

“CJ?”

“Yes.” Something moved across Hyun-woo’s face and was put back in its box — the expression of a man reporting weather from a neighbor’s garden. “Director Lee came herself. Kyung-hoo-ssi. She said she is here to introduce you to an overseas distributor.”

“Understood.”

He walked, and the folders rustled behind him, and the conference-room door was already three steps away, and if the chairman of a company with thirty-odd cameras pointed at its front door could be said to have a gait on a Wednesday morning, this one had it.

Chae Soo-bin got there first, both hands on the handle, and put her weight into it — the door swung, and forty-one degrees of noise died into carpet, and the room looked up.

Cho Young-chul rose from the near side of the table with the careful speed of a man whose knees had been in a chair since before breakfast, and there was a smile on him, and the smile had earned its place.

“Hoejang-nim. CCM is in hand. Nobody has been let go without being paid and the transfer documents are with the lawyer, so the handover begins on Monday. We did not waste the money on it.”

“You have had a bad ten days, Sajang-nim. Sit down. This one is on me, and I am not a generous man.”

Cho laughed once, quietly, and sat.

Across from him, at the far end of the table, Lee Kyung-hoo had not stood up, because Lee Kyung-hoo did not stand up for anybody and had made that clear to three chairmen and a vice minister in the last eighteen months. She had, however, looked at the doorway, and the look had changed the temperature of her face. My bias got better-looking, she thought, with the full disgust of a woman who had paid for a front-row seat. Two days out of the country and he came back better-looking.

Chae Soo-bin noticed her the way she noticed everybody, which was to say immediately and against her will: a woman of twenty-seven or eight, ivory suit cut close enough at the shoulder to have been argued for, short hair, makeup done to the standard of somebody who has had it done since she was nine years old, hands flat on the tablet in front of her. Nothing in her posture was for the room. Everything in it was from a house where the posture had been installed at the same time as the swimming lessons.

“Song Ji-ho.” She stood on the word as though it were a coin she was flipping. “Your little trip made a noise I could hear from Yeouido.”

“I’m sorry about the noise.” He took her hand for the length of one count and let go. “What can I say? A face like this doesn’t lie low.”

“Pfft!” The laugh got out of her before the training could stop it, and she put two fingers over her mouth and looked at him with real interest, the way you look at an animal that has done something unexpected in a documentary. “Where is she, by the way? Park Ji-yeon? The whole internet says Chairman Song is about to start a war for his girlfriend and I would very much like to see the woman who can do that.”

“She’s asleep,” Ji-ho said, and smiled at nothing in particular, which is the smile a man uses when he is not declining to answer. “Nuna came here for something other than my love life.”

“Of course not.” The amusement went off her face like a switched lamp and what was left was the part of her that sat on boards. “I came to bring you a gift.”

They sat. Chae Soo-bin laid the folders, moved the water glasses by exactly the same distance at each setting, and poured coffee from the carafe in the corner because there was no one else in the world to do it and she had learned by the second week that waiting for someone made it worse. She set the last cup down at Kyung-hoo’s hand, received a single examining look in return, and went to stand behind and slightly to the left of the chairman’s chair, where she had decided to spend the morning.

Kyung-hoo woke the tablet, turned it face-up on the table with two fingers — an old habit of the family’s, she had once explained, from the days when a contract was slid rather than spoken — and got to it without ceremony.

“I’ve heard the new album.”

Ji-ho’s eyebrow moved half a centimeter.

“Don’t look at me like that. Nothing was stolen.” She nodded at Cho, who did not defend himself. “Sajang-nim sent me the demos, three of them, on a Friday night, which is either courtship or desperation. I’m told it’s both.” A pause held, and the joke drained out of her own face while she watched it go. “I stopped at the first one and sat in my kitchen for twenty minutes. By the third I had one thought, and it isn’t a flattering one for anyone in this room: this record deserves to be heard by people who don’t live here.”

“So?”

“So I’m here with partners.” She pushed the tablet toward him. “CJ has run the Western distribution and the physical supply for Korean acts since before you were born, and I have spent ten years being introduced to the rooms that decide who gets shelf space in London and who gets a radio date in Dallas. “Domestic and Asia stay with us,” Cho said, from the side, in the tone of a man reading a footnote he had already checked twice. “The territory we cannot do properly, we should not pretend to.”

“That,” said Kyung-hoo, “is the most sensible thing anyone from this building has told me all month.”

Top · the cropped beige knit, held at the waist by a body that has stopped resisting it

Outerwear · pale khaki trench · mid-calf · previously forbidden on the grounds that it belonged to a taller woman

Previous verdict · “child wearing a parent’s coat” · “shrinks her by a fifth” · “do not bring this to the fitting”

Current verdict · the line falls from the shoulder to the ankle without pausing for breath, and the belt closes on the narrowest point of her waist as if the coat had been waiting there

Long skirts: cleared for re-entry. High-waist mini: not previously unsupported, merely slandered. A woman who has never been able to wear an overcoat is now being sold an overcoat by a shopkeeper who has recognized her from somewhere.

She tied the belt, made one knot and then a second one over it because she did not know what else to do with her hands, closed the collar to whatever height it wanted to be, and looked up into the glass.

The person in it looked back at her.

The coat had not swallowed her. It had gone down her the way water goes down a channel somebody had finally dug: the shoulder sat, the waist arrived, the hem brushed the top of her foot, and she was — there was no other word for it in a girl who had spent her life making herself small enough to fit inside other people’s judgments — long. She stood in a fitting room in Myeong-dong in a borrowed coat and looked like a woman on the front of something.

“AAAAAH!” Seung-wan went up off the floor with both hands on her head, and a shop assistant came round the rail at speed expecting a medical event. “I knew it! I knew it! I said the coat was for her!”

“Stand still — stand right there — don’t move, the angle, hold the collar — YES, that, hold that—”

Seul-gi took eleven photographs in forty seconds with the cracked phone and looked at every one of them and did not say a word, which for Seul-gi is a form of applause nobody else gets.

Joo-hyun looked at herself again, and again, and felt the thing she had been feeling since the pencil line on the wall, which was not vanity and was not surprise and was harder to name than either: she had known about her own face since she was old enough to be told about it. People who said she was pretty had queued, in Daegu, in Seoul, in the practice-room corridors, in the hall of a broadcasting station where she had once been stopped by a cameraman who wanted to know her name. She had also always known there was a screw loose in it somewhere — that the picture was right and the mounting was wrong, and that she was one of those girls who only survives in a heavily edited photo, in the close crop, in the shot where nobody can see her feet.

Three centimeters. The crop and the whole body were now the same person. It was like watching a camera find its focus after years of a soft image: nothing in the frame had changed, and everything in it had.

“Unni.” Seung-wan came up beside her and got religion into her face. “Do you know what you look like now?”

“What?”

“An actress. One of the cold ones. The kind people mistake for an actress in the street and get followed by a scout for it.”

The front of the shop went faintly loud at that exact moment, which is the sort of timing that would later be described on a fansite as evidence of something.

Two men in suits had come in past the door and had been standing in front of a rail of cardigans for a while, looking — not at cardigans. One of them had a lanyard in his pocket and the other was holding a card case open in both hands like an offering, and they had both picked the same target, and their eyes were on the fitting mirror, and the target in the mirror was wearing a trench coat at half past two on a Wednesday and had no makeup on beneath it worth the name.

“Excuse me! Sorry to bother you—” The cardigan man crossed the shop at a jog he did not commit to, stopped an arm’s length away, and put out his card with both hands, at the angle you use for an elder. “I’m a scout with SM Entertainment. I saw you from the street and I couldn’t — we do have a casting month, if you’d consider—”

Seul-gi turned her face into her shoulder, and the sound she made went somewhere else in her body to be dealt with privately.

Seung-wan jabbed a finger into Joo-hyun’s ribs and said, at a volume that reached the cardigans, “Unni. Your old company’s come to take you back.”

Joo-hyun took the card, because refusing a card is ruder than refusing an offer, and she nodded with the courtesy the years had put into her by hand, and the courtesy had always been excellent, and had never in her life been more devastating.

“Thank you. I already have a company.”

The scout’s face went through the seven stages of a man who has heard that sentence before and had expected not to hear it today, and then he looked at her — really looked, with the card still out, the way his job is done — and the color left the top of his face and came back as a flood.

“Ah — you’re — Bae Joo-hyun-ssi? Irene-ssi? I’m so sorry, I didn’t — I’m sorry, I had no idea—” He bowed, and bowed again, and then stopped, and looked at her a third time, and what came out of him then he clearly had not decided to say, because it came out with the honesty of a man who has just been shown the thing he is supposed to be good at seeing. “I couldn’t tell. From behind, honestly I couldn’t tell. Today you look — different. Really different.”

He was out the door with his colleague in front of him, apologizing to a rail of cardigans on the way.

“Even the SM scout couldn’t recognize you,” Seul-gi said, at last, free, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. “Unni, those were the best three centimeters ever bought.”

“They weren’t bought by me, so stop—”

“Aigoo, and the way you said ‘thank you, I already have a company’! You did the courtesy! On purpose! You’re terrible!”

Joo-hyun flicked at her and missed and laughed, and then — because she was a girl with a mirror in front of her and two friends who would never let her be — she looked at herself once more.

The person in the mirror was smiling at her.

What followed was not shopping. It was a change of weather. On a normal day the three of them down the middle of Myeong-dong collected roughly sixty percent of the passing heads — three pretty girls in an alley will do that and the city gets over it — and on this afternoon the woman walking in front of them, in a borrowed overcoat and a low ponytail, took ninety and did not appear to know it.

It wasn’t only the turning of heads.

Phones came up under sleeves and behind menus, and the small surreptitious photography of the street went on in a soft click-click along the whole length of the block. Two girls in matching jackets stopped dead, turned around, stopped again, and walked half a lane after them. A man with a shopping bag pulled his friend’s sleeve and said something in a low urgent voice, and what he said carried, because the alley is not large and the coats are thin: is that an idol? It has to be an idol, look at the walk.

Halfway down a fourth man came out of a doorway with a folder, watched them pass, then reversed his direction and ran the wrong way along the alley for half a block, arriving beside them with his shoes going and his card already out.

“Excuse me! JYP Entertainment, scouting — I don’t mean to bother, but would you consider—”

Seung-wan made a sound like a kettle and went down on one knee against a shopfront, laughing too hard to stand, and had to be held up by a coat rail.

Seul-gi did the honors, sweetly, still grinning, in the tone she uses for a bus timetable: “I’m sorry, she already has a company. LOEN Entertainment. Have you heard of it?”

The man’s face went through three countries. Amazement. Shock. Regret, at a depth that suggested a report he would have to write.

“…Of course she does. No, of course. Sorry. I’m sorry to have bothered you. That explains — at that level, you can’t be — sorry.”

He bowed to the folder, and to Seul-gi, and to the general alley, and left at a speed that a man of twenty-six does not normally have in him before an audition.

“Can you two please stop?” Joo-hyun said, and there was nothing whatever in it.

“No,” Seung-wan said, wiping her face with two fingers and coming upright on a coat rail. “Unni, honestly. It’s pressure. Walking behind you is pressure.”

“What pressure?”

“This pressure. Before, three girls walking down Myeong-dong were three pretty girls. Now there’s one actress walking in front at two point eight meters of aura, and two of us behind her carrying the shopping.”

Seul-gi nodded with such weight that her ponytail moved.

“You two really are—” She gave up on it and went on walking, and the corner of her own mouth was not in agreement with the complaint at all. “Come on. You said you were buying me clothes.”

“Yes! The other shop — there’s a trench in front, and — and a high-waist mini skirt!”

“A mini.”

“Before, a high-waist mini on you looked like a mistake the tailor made. Now it’s just a mini skirt. Come ON.”

They got her each side under the arms and walked her forward down the alley, and the passersby made room for three girls and did not know why, and Joo-hyun let herself be marched, and the smile on her had gone past the point where it could be argued with.

Three centimeters. Only three centimeters, and she had a whole sky attached to it.

Song Ji-ho. Song Ji-ho. How did you do it?

Not how did you know. Not how could you — the sentence was not a complaint and she had decided before she finished thinking it that she would not make it one, and that she would say nothing at all about it, and that she would keep the milk glass, and drink whatever he put in it next time, and be furious about that for one evening out of a year.

Wednesday · 23:40 Seoul · the hill, and a kitchen light two things, both in the same evening, both paid for

The tower had been busy until nine and quiet after that. Cho had taken the WME thread in hand himself, at half past eight, and had sent one line to Kyung-hoo at eleven: the Asia chief says Thursday, and he asked me to tell you that he has never once regretted a lunch you forced on him. Lee Hyun-woo had a schedule change and a hotel booking and no idea he had been given an errand. Somewhere below the hill, in a two-door annex with a kettle on a counter, a girl with a pencil line on her bedroom wall had written four words in a notebook and then torn the page out and kept the page.

Ji-ho stood in his own kitchen with the light off and the city coming in through the window, and he had two things on the counter in front of him: a printed copy of a term sheet he was going to read in English first and in Korean second, because the English version had the definitions and his father had taught him that much across a table in Los Angeles with a legal pad; and a glass jug with a finger of milk in it, portioned, capped, and labelled in his own handwriting with a date two weeks forward.

On the third of October the record goes out, he thought, and in the space of the same breath, without a seam between them — two weeks, and she’ll stand at 162 in a room where nobody knows why, and the WME man will fly in on a Thursday, and somebody in Gangnam is going to arrive at a lawyer’s office with nine billion won that isn’t his — and the day will belong to whichever of those four he has decided to be about.

Out on the ridge road, an SUV with a driver who had been told to take the night off went up the hill at a speed that suggested the driver had not, in fact, taken the night off, and Chae Soo-bin got out on the passenger side and around the back of it with a bag of convenience-store kimbap and one paper bag from a shop in Seongsu-dong she had walked into twice, and she rang the bell at eleven forty because the chairman had asked her to bring the folders and had not specified an hour, and that — she was aware, standing on a step in the dark with a bag of rice — was the exact problem with working for him.

He let her in, took the folders, saw the kimbap, said “you didn’t eat”, and put a glass of water in front of her at the kitchen island before she could answer, and she stood there in the light with her coat still on, doing the arithmetic of the situation and finding, as she had found eleven times in eleven weeks, that the man had known what she needed an hour before she did and had never once mentioned it.

“Hoejang-nim. Is it really all right that I — I’m the one you sent for food at midnight, is that — is this an allowance now?”

“It’s kimbap.”

“It’s kimbap at midnight, that’s different, that’s a category—” She stopped herself, and put the bag down, and moved the box of it two centimeters to the left for no reason at all. “The earrings. I wore them to the office today.”

“I know. Nobody in that room had the right to notice and one of them did.”

Her ears went hot enough to be embarrassing and she looked down at the counter to hide it, and while she was looking down at the counter, she saw the jug with the small paper label on it, in his handwriting, with a date on it; and she looked up again to ask what it was, and found that he had put a second glass out, and that the second glass was not at his place.

“…Whose is that?”

“Yours, for tonight only.” He tore the seal off a small carton with his thumbnail, and did not look up, and the tone of him had gone entirely domestic, which was worse than any of the other versions. “It helps you sleep. Don’t ask me what else it does and don’t drink it twice.”

That, at any rate, was answered, and answered completely, by the way he said it — and Chae Soo-bin, standing in a kitchen in the middle of the night with her coat on and a pair of silver grape leaves she had not bought catching the light, put her hands flat on the counter and thought, with total clarity and no room for argument at all: he gives that to people. Not the milk. The other thing. The exact sentence she needed, in the exact kitchen, at the exact moment when she was least able to hold her own mouth straight.

Three rooms, one city, one night: a kitchen up on the hill, a ninth floor on Teheran-ro, and a hallway on the third floor of an annex where a kettle sat cooling on a counter. On the third of October, four thousand people who had written paragraphs about a man with no line would find out that a line is not a promise you make in a comment section. In about a week, a company would be sold back to a man who had not been told he no longer owned it. And a girl asleep with one foot outside the blanket had grown three centimeters, and had been photographed in a borrowed coat by two friends who loved her, and had not been told either of those things was going to happen to her.

None of it started that night, and none of it would be finished by the end of October. What the night had, and kept, was a second glass put out for somebody in a kitchen at midnight, a shopkeeper’s pencil line on a wall in Myeong-dong, a chairman at an island with a term sheet he would read in English first, and — somewhere in Gangnam, on the far side of three dinners and two rounds of golf — a man still explaining to anyone who would sit still for it that buying his own company back was a formality.
