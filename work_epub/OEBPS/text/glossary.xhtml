How to Read the Terms

Korean address changes according to age, intimacy, rank, profession, and setting. English translations are therefore approximate. A term that sounds affectionate in one relationship may feel presumptuous in another.

The edition’s preferred romanization uses eonni, nuna, and Ji-ho-ya. Variants such as unnie, noona, or spellings without hyphens may appear in quoted material or older passages but carry the same basic meaning.

01 Name Suffixes & Respect Markers

-nim 님

A high-respect suffix attached to titles, roles, or occasionally names.

Used for: executives, clients, teachers, respected elders, formal professional address.

Hoejang-nim · Sajang-nim · Sunbae-nim

-ssi 씨

A polite, neutral suffix used with a full name or given name.

Nuance: respectful but less elevated than -nim; it may sound distant if used with someone very close.

Ji-ho-ssi · Soo-bin-ssi

-ah / -ya 아 / 야

Familiar vocative endings used when calling someone of equal or lower status.

Grammar: -ah follows a consonant; -ya follows a vowel.

Taeyeon-ah · Ji-eun-ah · Ji-ho-ya

-i / -ie 이

An affectionate or grammatical attachment often heard after a consonant-ending given name.

Note: not inherently honorific; it can make a name sound familiar or natural inside a sentence.

Ji-eun-i · Sooyoung-i

02 Age, Gender & Seniority

Oppa 오빠

Used by a woman for an older brother or an older man with whom she is close.

Nuance: may be familial, friendly, flirtatious, or romantic. Context decides; it does not automatically mean “boyfriend.”

Eonni / Unnie 언니

Used by a woman for an older sister or an older woman with whom she is close.

Taeyeon eonni · Hyomin eonni

Hyung 형

Used by a man for an older brother or a close older man.

Industry use: common between male performers, managers, producers, and friends.

Nuna / Noona 누나

Used by a man for an older sister or a close older woman.

Taeyeon nuna · Tiffany nuna

Sunbae / Sunbae-nim 선배 / 선배님

A senior who entered a school, profession, company, or entertainment field earlier.

Sunbae-nim adds formal respect. Age alone does not determine seniority.

Hoobae 후배

A junior who entered the same institution or professional field later.

Opposite: sunbae.

Dongsaeng 동생

A younger sibling or a younger person treated with sibling-like familiarity.

Can be specified as namdongsaeng (younger brother) or yeodongsaeng (younger sister).

Maknae 막내

The youngest member of a family, team, or idol group.

Often carries expectations of affection, teasing, or special treatment.

03 Workplace & Entertainment Titles

Hoejang-nim 회장님

Chairman or chairperson; the highest-ranking corporate title commonly used for Ji-ho at LOEN.

Sajang-nim 사장님

Company president, chief executive, proprietor, or business owner, depending on the organization.

Also used respectfully for the owner of a restaurant or shop.

Daepyo-nim 대표님

Representative director or company representative; often translated as CEO.

Isa-nim 이사님

Corporate director or executive director.

The precise authority varies by company structure.

Bujang-nim 부장님

Department head or senior manager.

Chajang-nim 차장님

Deputy general manager — the rank that sits under a department head and above a section manager.

Nuance: senior enough to be addressed with -nim by juniors, junior enough to be sent to the airport.

Siljang-nim 실장님

Head of an office, studio unit, planning room, or specialized division.

Teamjang-nim 팀장님

Team leader.

Biseo / Biseo-nim 비서 / 비서님

Secretary — the title carried by the person who controls a chairman’s door, diary, and documents.

Nuance: formally a support role, but proximity to power lends it weight; juniors add -nim without thinking twice.

Seonsaengnim 선생님

Teacher, instructor, doctor, master, or respected professional.

Broader than the English word “teacher.”

04 Family & Affection

Appa 아빠

Dad; intimate family speech.

Eomma 엄마

Mom; intimate family speech.

Abeonim 아버님

A highly respectful form of “father,” often used for another person’s father or a parent-in-law.

Eomeonim 어머님

A highly respectful form of “mother,” often used for another person’s mother or a parent-in-law.

Halmeoni / Halmeonim 할머니 / 할머님

Grandmother; halmeonim adds deference when addressing someone else’s grandmother.

Yeobo 여보

“Honey” or “dear,” traditionally used between spouses.

Using it before marriage can sound especially intimate or teasing.

Jagi / Jagiya 자기 / 자기야

An affectionate “baby,” “honey,” or “darling” between romantic partners.

Imo-nim 이모님

“Auntie,” with respect: a mother’s friend, or the mother of one’s own friend.

Used by a young woman addressing her friend’s mother whether or not the two families are close; it turns a household into a relationship, and it is the form an unmarried girl of nineteen would use in a corridor at ten at night.

05 Social Address

Chingu 친구

Friend. In traditional Korean usage, it often implies someone of the same age.

Jeolchin 절친

Short for 절친한 친구 — an extremely close friend; best friend, the inner circle.

Nuance: casual and affectionate, used between peers. It carries an assumption of total candour: what a jeolchin is told, a friend is not.

Agassi 아가씨

Young lady or unmarried woman; also used by household staff toward a family’s daughter.

Tone and social context can make it courteous, old-fashioned, or hierarchical.

Ajumma 아줌마

A familiar term for a middle-aged or married woman.

Can sound warm, blunt, or rude depending on speaker and context. Ajumeoni is more polite.

Ajusshi 아저씨

A familiar term for an adult man, roughly “mister” or “uncle.”

Not necessarily a relative.

Hanwoo 한우

Domestic Korean beef, from cattle raised in Korea; the cut a host buys when an evening is meant to be a reward.

Used for: celebration meals and business dinners. Ordering it says the host is not counting, and it is the plainest way in the country to say so.

a room booked at the beef place round the corner · the good cut, brought out

TaeTi 태띠

A fandom diminutive of Taeyeon’s given name, formed by the people online who treat her as small and theirs rather than famous.

Used for: fan comments, thread titles, and ship tags about Kim Taeyeon. Nobody in the room addresses her with it.

the diminutive of the diminutive · about eleven million people online

06 Recurring Courtesy & Reaction Phrases

Annyeong haseyo 안녕하세요

Polite “hello.”

Yeoboseyo 여보세요

The standard greeting when answering a telephone call.

Ne 네

Yes; also used to acknowledge that one is listening.

The house spelling is ne. Older variants may appear as nei.

Ani / Aniya 아니 / 아니야

No, not so, or “that isn’t it.” Aniya is informal.

Arasseo 알았어

“Okay,” “I understand,” or “got it,” in familiar speech.

Mianhae / Mianhamnida 미안해 / 미안합니다

“I’m sorry.” Mianhae is intimate; mianhamnida is formal and polite.

Aigoo 아이고

An exclamation expressing affection, fatigue, disbelief, pain, or exasperation.

Aish 아이씨

A frustrated exclamation, milder than a full swear but not always polite.

Daebak 대박

Amazing, incredible, a jackpot, or a major success.

Hwaiting 화이팅

“You can do it,” “good luck,” or “let’s go.”

Omo / Ohmo 어머

“Oh my,” expressing surprise or shock.

Gamsahamnida 감사합니다

A formal, polite “thank you.”

Nuance: appropriate for seniors, strangers, professional settings, and moments of sincere gratitude.

Bok 복

Fortune, blessing, or luck in the moral sense — something that can be earned, shared, or wasted.

Common phrasing: 나눠 먹으면 복이 온다 — “share your food and luck comes.” Said to children, at markets, and across tables.

Akpl 악플

A malicious comment: ak (evil) plus the English word “comment.” Written under news, on portals, and in the replies to a stranger.

Nuance: a recognised hazard of the job rather than a private cruelty. Celebrities read them; agencies log them; fandoms are taught to report them and not to answer them.

Taldeok 탈덕

Leaving fandom: tal (to depart) plus deok, the absorbed devotion of a deokhu. Said of oneself, out loud, like quitting a religion.

Nuance: an announced withdrawal, not a quiet drifting-off — and therefore a statistic that agencies count.

Maejang 매장

Literally burial. The industry’s word for an artist who is kept on the roster and given nothing to do — no schedule, no comeback, no conversation about a renewal.

Nuance: never announced and almost never denied; it is recognised only by what stops arriving, which is why it is so hard to prove from outside and so easy to feel from inside. Fans say it of groups they love and agencies deny it in identical sentences.

Nunchi 눈치

The read of a room: knowing what is going on, who is affected, and what you are permitted to want in it — without anything being said.

Nuance: the compliment is nunchi is good; the insult is that someone had none. It is the skill an employee, a daughter-in-law and a trainee all need, and it is the reason a refusal can be delivered as a favour. Nobody thanks you for using it.

눈치가 빠르다 — “quick at reading a room” · 눈치가 없다 — “has no idea what the room is saying”

Gojin-gamrae 고진감래

“Bitterness first, sweetness after” — the four characters Koreans use for an endurance that pays out at the end.

Nuance: said to children practising scales, to trainees, and to oneself at three in the morning. It is not comfort about suffering; it is a promise about timing, which is why it is also the phrase a man uses when he has already decided when the turn comes.

The companion idiom in the same mouth: siri-ani-hmyeon ttok-bari-oda — “if you don’t sit down, you can’t get up from the floor.”

Mwo 뭐

“What?” — the short, blunt form of “what,” used alone as a challenge or as the sound of a person rebooting.

Used for: astonishment, disbelief, demanding that a sentence be said twice.

“Mwo?? Mworago??”

Mworago 뭐라구

“Say that again?” — Mwo with a reporting ending; less a question than a dare.

Used for: when repeating the words would be more dangerous than asking.

“Did you know Sica is my girlfriend now, too?” · “Mwo?? Mworago??”

Hyeongbu 형부

A woman’s term for her older sister’s husband — intimate, in-law warm, and half a proposal in one word.

Used for: teasing a sister’s boyfriend by talking as if the wedding had already happened.

“Seonsaengnim? Hoejang-nim? Hyeongbu— or ‘Song Ji-ho’?”

Yeo-reo-bun 여러분

“Everyone!” — the stage-and-broadcast address; how an idol gathers a room, a crowd, or six bandmates to a scandal.

Used for: rallying listeners, making an announcement, crying news across a dormitory.

“Yeo-reo-bun! Im Yoon-a is DATING!!”

Tteokbokki 떡볶이

Rice cakes simmered in gochujang — street-cart heat, student prices, the taste of being twenty-two and broke together.

Used for: casual meals, nostalgia, the food of friendships that predate fame.

a teacher who once ate tteokbokki with his students

Noraebang 노래방

A private karaoke room rented by the hour — soundproofed, unglamorous, and the closest thing the industry has to a confession booth.

Used for: unwinding; the singing nobody grades; two in the morning with people who already love you.

“Sing it like you’re in a noraebang, not like you’re being judged.”
