Chapter Seventy-Seven

Sulli’s Memories

Sulli looked up at the cold set of his profile, the chill not yet gone from his eyes — then at the disheveled, snarling figure on the sofa — and felt the boulder that had sat on her chest for over a decade crack down the middle under the weight of everything he’d just said.

The hurt was still there. The anger, the grief. But through the fissures, something she had never once felt was pouring out — a stinging, painful, magnificent lightness.

She nodded hard, and gripped his hand.

They turned for the door.

“DON’T YOU DARE! Sulli — walk out with that stray and you are NO daughter of mine!”

The mother scrambled up off the sofa like something unhinged, lunging to grab and drag — and forgot entirely about the glass she herself had shattered across the floor that morning.

A heel turned. The whole body pitched forward.

“AAAH—!”

The scream tore through the apartment. Her hands broke the fall on instinct, knees slamming down after — directly into the field of shards. Blood welled instantly, staining palms and expensive stockings; glass bit deep, and the venom in her mouth dissolved into raw, gasping wails.

Sulli’s step faltered. Her head began to turn.

Ji-ho’s hand tightened around hers — and his stride never broke. He walked her straight out of that suffocating house, and shut all of it — the chaos, the shame, the whole bleeding past — behind the door.

Out of the dimness, the sunlight came down sharp enough to sting. She squinted into it.

His grip was warm and completely steady on her wrist. Behind her, muffled through the door, her mother’s frantic sobbing rage and her brother’s anxious voice tangled together and faded. And in her chest, the pressure of years surged, slammed against its old walls one last time — and began, quietly, to drain away.

It hurts. It hurts — and GOD, it feels good.

Something reckless and bright kicked up inside her. She had never in her life been loved without conditions — had spent that life terrified that one wrong word would cost her even her mother — and the pressure of all that swallowed silence had finally, FINALLY found a spillway.

So THIS is what it feels like. Saying the thing you actually mean. Doing the thing you actually want. THIS is what it feels like!

The most liberating day of her entire life, she decided, was today.

She squeezed his hand back — hard — and stopped walking. He turned. Her eyes were still wet at the corners, glittering, pitiable — and then her whole face flipped, grief to light, like weather changing over the sea.

“Oppa. Will you come somewhere with me?”

“Okay.” He smiled back. “But first — a call to Qian-jie. She’s worried sick about you.”

“Mm. I’ll call her myself.”

Late Afternoon Huinnyeoul Beach Road — Yeongdo, Busan where the coast keeps secrets

New phone bought and set up, the two of them walked the winding coastal path side by side. The sea wind carried its faint salt bite through their hair; beyond the railing lay blue water and scattered islands, below it jagged rocks and the white lace of breaking waves. Almost no tourists — only the occasional sun-blackened fisherman passing with his gear, sparing them one glance and no more.

The quiet worked on her by degrees. When she was sure no one was near, she reached up and carefully removed the mask that had hidden half her face — leaving only the round retro sunglasses perched on her nose, covering the swollen eyes, and letting the pale nose-tip and bloodless lips back into the light.

She pulled the sea air all the way down into her lungs.

“Oppa.” Her voice was still husked from crying, but level now. “When I was little… my family was poor.”

She began, eyes on the far waterline, dredging the scattered pieces up out of all that blue.

“The neighbor kids all went to kindergarten. Only me — my mom sent me to a performance academy instead. I was too young to understand why I was different. The result was… nobody wanted to play with me. I was the weird kid.”

Her mouth pulled sideways — half a smile, none of it warm.

“And my parents — from my first memories, I don’t think there was a single day they didn’t fight. The air in that house was always wrong. You couldn’t breathe it. So the second school ended, I’d run anywhere that wasn’t home.”

Memory · Busan, a narrow alley, many years ago

A very small Sulli, schoolbag too big for her back, trudges home down the shabby familiar lane — and stops before the door, because the sounds are already coming through it: the sharp, practiced voices, the bright crack of breaking china. She presses her lips together, turns without hesitating, and runs the other way.

Her wandering takes her to the little neighborhood park, where children her age are shrieking with laughter over a jump-rope elastic. She hides behind the old locust tree and watches. She doesn’t actually care about the game. She cares that behind the tree there is nobody, and at home there is smoke.

“Can… can I play too?”

The children stop and look — curious, and a little bit walled. A girl with pigtails: “Do you even know how? We play FAST.”

Her heart hammers. She nods hard, and makes her voice sweeter and softer than it has ever been: “I love this game! I learn really fast — really!”

It is the first lie she ever tells to be allowed inside a circle.

That evening she jumps clumsily on the beat, falls, skins a knee that burns like fire — and when she finally clears the highest height and the others gasp “Wah, Jin-ri, you’re AMAZING,” something warm and sour and sweet floods her whole small body at once. She has “friends.” Even if only for tonight. Even if the entry fee was pretending.

At home, the fighting has paused. Her mother sees the dirty trousers and the ruined knee, and frowns, and the voice is tired and not gentle: “How did you manage that? A girl should be quiet. Be obedient — understand? Everything Mom does is for your own good. Be good. Make people like you.”

Be obedient. Be good. Make people like you. The words go into the ground of her, alongside the afternoon’s discovery, like seeds.

And dimly, at that age, she touches the shape of a survival law: shrink a little, then a little more. Fold the real likes away. Wear whatever face they might love. Be good enough, sweet enough, WANTED enough — and you will be given attention, smiles, the thing shaped like love that you are starving for. Some corner of her already knows a love bought this way is counterfeit and unstable. But counterfeit beats nothing. It always beats nothing.

So she learns the roles — good daughter, sweetheart, SM’s little princess — and performs them tirelessly, because the fear of losing love has already grown into her bones.
