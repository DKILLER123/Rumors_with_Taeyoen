Chapter Forty

When I Learn to Love Myself — Can I Love You Too?

Halfway through the meal she looked up suddenly, eyes shining.

“Oppa.”

“Hm?”

“Thank you. Not as— not as the person who saved my life. Not as someone I need to keep happy. Just… thank you.”

He raised his water glass and touched it gently to hers.

“You’re welcome. And note for next time — you don’t have to thank people for being good to you. You’re worth being good to. That’s the whole transaction.”

Something shifted behind her eyes — like the first thread of meltwater finding its way out from under a winter’s ice. She nodded hard, and the smile that followed carried none of the practiced sugar. Just clean, unguarded warmth.

Deep in the alley, the barbecue house’s lamplight burned late. And for Sulli, the night was more than a long-overdue good meal. It was the first time it had ever occurred to her that — maybe, just maybe — she could stop working so hard to please the entire world, and still be held warmly by it.

Two portions of beef later she was perfectly, roundly full, slumped back against her chair with a hand on her stomach.

“Beef really is the best~”

“It’s getting late. Come on.”

She paid the bill — her treat, defended fiercely — and they stepped out into the cool of the night, the air a blend of grill smoke and early-summer green.

“Oppa, I’ll walk you to the subway station.”

“The escort service runs the other direction.” He moved to the outside of the pavement without breaking stride. “Patients should develop some patient self-awareness.”

The alley ran narrow, pressing them close; she caught the faint soap-clean scent of him under the lingering barbecue smoke, and found the combination strangely, deeply steadying. Near the alley mouth, she spoke suddenly, softly.

“Oppa.”

“Hm?”

“If… if I try being less ‘mature’ from now on. Less good at reading the room…” A hesitation, doubt flickering audibly. “…will people still like me?”

He stopped and turned to face her. The streetlight at the alley mouth laid a soft ring of gold across her face — those eyes that smiled by default, holding out their small careful hope with both hands, terror folded neatly underneath.

He didn’t answer. He asked instead:

“Sulli-yah. Do you like yourself?”

The question landed out of nowhere. She froze, lips parting around an answer that didn’t exist.

Like… myself?

The idea was so foreign her mind simply whited out. All these years, the only question had ever been how to make others like her — Mom liked obedient daughters; the company liked hardworking idols; the fans liked flawless goddesses. Not once — not one single time — had anyone asked, and she had never asked herself: Sulli. Do you like Sulli?

Under the streetlight her shadow stretched long and thin across the flagstones, alone.

He didn’t rush her. Some questions needed time; some answers needed courage.

At last she shook her head — slowly, honestly.

“I don’t know. I’ve… never thought about it. Not once.”

“Then start today.” His voice stayed gentle. “No deadline on the answer. Just… give yourself the chance to meet her. The Sulli who doesn’t have to please anybody.”

“She might feel like a stranger at first. She might not be perfect. But my guess?” A small smile. “She’s very much worth liking. By you, specifically.”

✦   ✦   ✦

At the hospital entrance, the goodbye arrived.

She stood in place, watching him descend the steps — and suddenly called out:

“Oppa!”

He turned.

“Next time…” A deep breath, gathering. “Next time we meet — I’ll bring HER. That Sulli. I’ll… I’ll work hard to find her.”

His smile was very bright in the dark.

“I’ll be waiting. No rush. Take the long way if you need it.”

She watched until he vanished past the corner.

“Ji-ho oppa. Thank you.”

Walking back toward the hospital doors, her steps came lighter than they’d left. The path lamps laid silver across the flagstones like a road made of moonlight — destination unknown. But tonight, at least, she’d taken the first step: the girl who had spent her whole life pleasing the world had stopped, for one evening, and listened for her own voice instead.

And the voice was faint — but in the quiet dark, unmistakable.

Try, it said. Just try — live once, for yourself.

The night wind carried some far-off flower’s scent. She tilted her head back — and discovered, for the first time, that Seoul’s sky did hold stars. Sparse ones. Dim ones. But there, and quietly shining.

Like certain forgotten things that had always been hers.

Maybe those could be found again too.

And then — glancing back down the road he’d taken — a shy heat climbed her face, and a thought arrived with a small stab of panic: but oppa is so… HIM. Will someone like that really still be standing there by the time I find myself?

Her legs decided before she did. She ran.

✦   ✦   ✦

Ji-ho walked the empty road past the hospital, nothing for company but the still ornamental trees lining both sides — and a knot of feelings he couldn’t quite untangle.

The honest ledger: he’d come here tonight fully intending to weaponize the makeup and charm the girl senseless. And then she’d started talking about her childhood — and every word had landed on his own scar tissue. Eight years old, a father who left without a sentence of goodbye. Middle school, a mother who followed. An ocean crossed alone chasing a dream, so many years out here that he’d have to work, now, to reconstruct either of their faces.

That was why his hand had gone out to her. Not the plan. The recognition. Change the shape of her — and maybe the shape of her fate changes with it.

“SONG JI-HO!!”

He stopped. Turned.

She was running flat-out toward him — hair streaming loose in the wind, skirt hem swinging bright with every stride, the whole girl bobbing toward him like a peach caught in a current. No hesitation. No brakes. She crashed straight into his chest, arms wrapping hard around his waist, face buried against him.

When her breathing finally slowed, she tilted her head back. The round eyes had curved to crescents; fine sweat glittered at the tip of her nose; her brows sat high and reckless, and the smile underneath them was pure daylight.

“Ji-ho oppa. When I’ve found myself — when I’ve learned to love myself — can I love you too?”

He looked down at her — radiant, upturned, entirely alive — and this should have been a simple, happy thing. His mouth opened. Nothing in it knew what to say.

“You don’t have to answer now.”

She wasn’t waiting for one anyway. She rose onto her toes and kissed him.

Barely a kiss at all — the lightest press, soft and cool, tasting faintly of sweet lip balm. Then she pushed back off his chest, and the smile she gave him was shy and utterly unafraid at the same time.

“That’s your thank-you gift for tonight. Goodnight, oppa.”

Some people don’t leave because you weren’t good enough. They leave because they never learned how to stay.
