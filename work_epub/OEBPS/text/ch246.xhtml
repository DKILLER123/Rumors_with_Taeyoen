Chapter Two Hundred Forty-Six

The Second Wish

LOEN Entertainment · executive conference room five transferred contracts, one watchful senior—and a chairman learning how much power costs

Five trainees sat in a precise row along one side of the conference table: Bae Joo-hyun, Kang Seul-gi, Son Seung-wan, Park Soo-young, and Kim Ye-rim.

A new contract packet waited in front of each of them. The covers were uncreased, their corners sharp enough to catch the light.

Today was their first official day at LOEN.

The door opened.

All five rose and bowed on instinct. When they straightened, the movement stalled almost in unison.

Ji-ho entered in a dark, close-cut suit that sharpened the line of his shoulders. His pace was unhurried, his expression composed behind a pair of gold-rimmed glasses. They had seen him exhausted after practice, laughing in hallways, and soaked in sweat beneath rehearsal-room lights. This version of him belonged to another world entirely.

He looked less like their former fellow trainee than the young head of a chaebol.

Irene swallowed.

The glasses were unfair. Completely unfair.

Seulgi, Wendy, Joy, and Yeri seemed equally unsure where to look.

IU followed him into the room. As LOEN’s best-known artist, she needed no introduction.

“Sit.”

Ji-ho took the head chair. IU sat beside him and let her gaze move quietly across the five trainees opposite.

Every one of them was attractive in a different way.

The eldest had a cool, almost untouchable beauty—except for her petite frame, which softened the force of it. Beside her, the monolid girl carried an athletic confidence. The short-haired girl had bright eyes and an open smile. The lively one seemed ready to speak before anyone asked. The maknae kept her gaze lowered, still wary of the unfamiliar room.

IU filed away each impression without changing her expression.

“I kept my promise,” Ji-ho said. His tone was cordial but unmistakably official. “From today onward, LOEN is responsible for your contracts and careers. If you have questions, ask them now.”

The five exchanged glances. Irene finally spoke.

“Hoejang-nim, what happens next?”

Ji-ho met her eyes for a moment.

“The company’s trainee system is being rebuilt. It will take no more than a month to establish the full program. Until then, continue your independent practice. Once a week, I’ll personally lead a special vocal session.”

He paused and folded his hands on the table.

“Dance and acting instructors will be appointed within that same month.”

The trainees’ expressions shifted almost imperceptibly.

Personal vocal instruction from Song Ji-ho—the producer behind some of the year’s largest records—was not an ordinary company benefit.

IU turned to him in surprise. LOEN already employed respected vocal coaches, more than capable of training pre-debut artists.

He noticed.

“You can attend too, when your schedule allows.”

Her eyes widened.

“Me?”

“You.”

IU stared at him. Korea’s top female soloist, back in class beside five trainees?

Ji-ho continued before she could object.

The Encyclopedia of the Perfect Singer did more than contain exercises. It analyzed an individual voice and recommended methods suited to its range, habits, and weaknesses. One month of targeted instruction would give each student a foundation she could continue independently. When they reached the next plateau, he could introduce the advanced regimen.

“Irene, Seulgi, and Joy—you have several years of training behind you. Your fundamentals are strong. Wendy and Yeri, your natural conditions are promising, but promise is not a substitute for discipline.”

His gaze moved across all five.

“Work harder from this point forward. If your development stays on schedule, the company intends to debut you next year. The detailed plan will be delivered through the trainee department.”

Hope flashed openly across the table. Even Irene’s practiced composure could not hide it.

Ji-ho stood.

“That’s all. Ji-eun, show them around the company.”

IU rose with a sweet, obedient smile.

“Of course, Hoejang-nim.”

The door closed behind him.

Tension escaped the conference room like air from a loosened knot. The trainees looked at IU with a mixture of awe and curiosity. She was not merely a senior artist; she was the nation’s little sister and one of the industry’s most successful soloists.

All five bowed.

“Annyeong haseyo, IU sunbaenim.”

“You don’t need to be frightened.” IU stood and smiled. “I’ll show you around. We’re under the same roof now.”

She led them through the recording floors, administrative offices, and practice wing. Her manner remained warm and unpretentious; by the time they reached the trainee rooms, the five girls had begun to breathe normally again.

“Where are you staying? Has the company arranged a dorm?”

“It has.” Seulgi could not keep the happiness from her face.

The apartment stood in an upscale residential area near LOEN: four bedrooms, two living areas, tasteful furnishings, and every appliance they could reasonably need.

Wendy’s eyes curved with her smile.

“It’s better than the dorms of plenty of debuted artists. Ji-ho—” She caught herself. “I mean, Hoejang-nim—really took care of us.”

“Coming here was the right decision,” Joy agreed.

IU blinked.

The bright girl sounded remarkably familiar with him.

“I haven’t properly learned everyone’s names yet.”

“Son Seung-wan, sunbaenim.”

“Park Soo-young.”

“Bae Joo-hyun.”

“Kang Seul-gi.”

“Kim Ye-rim.”

IU matched legal names to faces while keeping her private labels intact.

The breathtaking one was Joo-hyun. The lively, curvy one was Soo-young. Seung-wan seemed most comfortable talking about Ji-ho. Seul-gi carried herself like an athlete. Ye-rim was the cautious maknae.

It was nearly lunchtime.

“The cafeteria food is good. Come on—I’ll buy lunch.”

The trainees lit up.

“Is that really all right, sunbaenim?” Joy asked.

“Of course. I told you—we’re one family now.”

IU let the conversation breathe for a few steps before asking in a deliberately casual tone:

“I heard all of you were close to Ji-ho during his trainee days.”

Joy raised her hand at once.

“Wendy was closest to him.”

Wendy nodded without embarrassment.

“We entered around the same time and got along well. We practiced together often.”

IU looked at her more carefully.

She had assumed Irene would be the person to watch. Beauty that striking naturally drew suspicion—especially when the beauty in question had gone unusually quiet around Ji-ho. Yet Irene had revealed nothing. Wendy was different. Whenever she spoke of him, admiration shone openly in her eyes.

It might be innocent.

It might not.

Lunch would provide time to find out.

Cheongdam-dong · Galaxy Securities equity investment division

“Annyeong haseyo, Chairman Song.”

A sharply dressed man came forward and adjusted his glasses before offering both hands.

“I’m Kim Dae-hyeong, head of the Equity Investment Division at Galaxy Securities.”

“Director Kim. Thank you for seeing me.”

They shook hands and sat.

“You want to acquire shares in Daum Communications without the company realizing who is accumulating them?”

“Correct. Over the next six months, I want as many publicly traded shares and small private blocks as possible. I don’t want Daum’s management alerted.”

Kim Dae-hyeong considered the request.

“What acquisition budget are you contemplating?”

“Two hundred billion won.”

The director’s eyelid moved.

Two hundred billion.

Ji-ho had only recently spent three hundred billion acquiring LOEN. Now he spoke of another two hundred as if allocating a production budget. The rumors of an unseen financial consortium behind him suddenly felt much less absurd.

Kim Dae-hyeong’s manner became even more careful.

“The shares themselves can be bought,” the director said. “The secrecy cannot be guaranteed. At that volume, price movement, disclosure thresholds, counterparties, and market surveillance will expose the accumulation. We cannot complete it without Daum noticing.”

Ji-ho nodded.

He had considered dividing the order among several securities houses, but such a structure required political reach, trusted intermediaries, and a network deep enough to coordinate silence. He possessed money and momentum, not yet that kind of invisible infrastructure.

If one of Seoul’s largest securities firms could not promise discretion, there was no value in forcing the meeting further.

“Understood. Thank you for the candid answer.”

Minutes later, the car pulled away from Galaxy Securities.

Ji-ho leaned back and closed his eyes.

He had hoped to save one of his remaining wish spheres. Kakao was too important for thrift.

At this stage, Kakao’s valuation also hovered around one trillion won. After its eventual merger with Daum Communications, a two-hundred-billion-won position assembled at the right time could translate into more than ten percent of the combined company—enough to place him among Kakao’s three largest shareholders.

A limited wish may create favorable commercial conditions but cannot directly conjure an operating company without consideration.

Ji-ho formed the request with care.

SECOND COMMERCIAL WISH · SUBMITTED

Capital Use ₩200 billion to acquire equity in Kakao or Daum Communications.

Constraint Avoid alerting either company’s senior leadership during accumulation.

Outcome After the future merger, consolidate a final holding above 10% in the successor company.

Wish accepted.

Favorable conditions will be created over the next six months: multiple offshore fund accounts; purchases from retail holders, large individual investors, and minor shareholders; and temporary institutional indifference to anomalous accumulation. At merger confirmation, beneficial ownership will consolidate under the requester’s name.

Ji-ho opened his eyes. A faint smile touched his mouth.

In the end, the system remained the only broker capable of making the impossible look administrative.

His phone began to ring.

Count to seven. Stop. Look back. The scent of strawberries arrives before the kiss.

The caller ID read Jessica.

INCOMING CALL · JESSICA

“Hello.”

“Ji-ho… it’s me.”

“Sica. What’s wrong?”

“I wanted to invite you to dinner tonight. Are you free?”

“I already have plans tonight.”

“Oh.”

The disappointment was audible, though she recovered quickly.

“Then when do you have time?”

“I’m busy for the foreseeable future. Let’s talk later.”

He ended the call.

Jessica remained in the driver’s seat with the phone against her ear long after the screen went dark.

I’m busy. Let’s talk later.

The words circled her mind. His voice had been flat—not hostile, not impatient, simply distant. It bore almost no resemblance to the warm, attentive man who had listened to her proposal and offered advice as if her ambitions mattered.

She lowered the phone and stared at its black screen.

Had she rejected him too sharply that day? Or had he never cared as much as she assumed?

No. If he felt nothing, why give her serious advice? Why look at her with that unmistakable interest in his living room?

Yet the recent record was impossible to misread.

Messages answered two days late, if at all. Coffee postponed. Dinner dismissed with one indifferent sentence.

A thin current of panic moved beneath her pride.

She had believed that sending the first message, making the first call, and proposing dinner were already major concessions. She was Jessica. She was the woman. He was the shameless one. Surely, once she opened the door by an inch, he would rush through it.

His response had delivered a colder truth: what she considered bold initiative might mean almost nothing to a man whose life had become crowded with companies, songs, films, and women willing to step closer than she had.

Jessica rested her head against the seat and closed her eyes.

JESSICA · FRACTURED IMPRESSIONS

His courteous nod at their first meeting.

The patience with which he had listened across a restaurant table.

The thoughtful precision of his advice.

And now: a voice as remote as a stranger’s.

“Jung Soo-yeon, what exactly are you doing?”

She had approached him because she needed help, then preserved her pride as if he were the petitioner. She wanted to move closer but had waited for him to cross every remaining step. Knowing about Tiffany had made her feel as if she possessed leverage.

But leverage only mattered if the other person still wanted something from you.

She opened their KakaoTalk thread, typed a sentence, erased it, typed another, and erased that too.

Seven hundred billion won. Billboard. LOEN. Tiffany. Taeyeon.

Twenty-five years old, with five years remaining before thirty.

Seven years of training and six as an idol—thirteen years of relentless work, with no guarantee that the future she wanted would wait for her.

Time could no longer be wasted.

She put the phone down.

First, she needed a plan. Then, perhaps, a drink.

In the departing car, Ji-ho looked through the window and allowed the corner of his mouth to rise.

“This time, she’ll have to think seriously.”

Some people should not be accommodated too easily. Jessica’s pride was not a flaw he wished to destroy, but it was a wall. Until she understood that waiting was also a choice—and that choices had consequences—she would never decide what she truly wanted.

Irene’s apartment · evening a carefully set table and a question neither of them asks directly

The apartment was spacious, warm, and unmistakably hers. Soft amber light touched a plain tablecloth; bowls and chopsticks stood in exact alignment. Nothing was ostentatious. Everything had been considered.

Ji-ho sat opposite Irene and took another bite.

She had been unusually quiet all evening. Her chopsticks moved through the rice without purpose, and every few moments her eyes drifted toward him before retreating.

“What is it?” He looked up. “Is there something on my face?”

“Ani.” Her gaze dropped immediately. “Nothing.”

He let the silence rest rather than press her.

“Are you settling in at LOEN?”

“Not yet.” She searched for the right phrasing. “It’s too free.”

“Too free?”

“SM is a standardized production line. LOEN feels more like a workshop. There are fewer rules, so everyone has to decide for themselves how hard to work.”

Ji-ho smiled.

“Talent needs room to become individual.”

Privately, he considered YG the most successful of the major companies at cultivating artists with immediately recognizable identities. Its performers often sounded as if they possessed ideas and ownership. But complete freedom carried its own dangers. Neglect could masquerade as trust, and trainees without structure could wander into habits that destroyed them.

SM’s artists rarely embarrassed the company in public. Whatever happened behind closed doors, the system drilled courtesy, discipline, and presentation until they became instinct.

Ji-ho wanted both: SM’s architecture and enough freedom for an artist to remain a person.

When dinner ended, they talked until the heaviness in the room softened. Later, Irene emerged from the bedroom carrying her sleep clothes. Ji-ho approached with a glass of milk.

He took the folded clothes from her arms and offered the glass.

“Drink this. It should help you sleep.”

“Thank you.”

She tasted it cautiously. The flavor was clean and faintly sweet, without the heaviness she disliked in ordinary milk. Her eyes narrowed in approval, and she finished the glass.

Ji-ho watched with mixed satisfaction and guilt.

The height-restructuring milk was intended to help her reach the proportions she had always wanted before debut. Explaining that, however, remained considerably harder than giving it to her.

Irene handed the empty glass back.

“It was good.”

“I’m glad.”

She lingered instead of turning away.

There was still something she wanted to say.
