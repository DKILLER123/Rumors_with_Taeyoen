Chapter Two Hundred Fifty-Three

Grammys, Here I Come

LOEN Entertainment · chairman’s production studio one album assembled from the future, one old scandal reopened, and one meeting Jiyeon cannot stop imagining

Ji-ho knew nothing yet of what had unfolded in Jeonju.

Jessica’s company would not be built in a week—or even a season. Market research had to be credible. Incorporation, licensing, banking, intellectual-property registration, operating hires, and manufacturing review would each consume time.

The largest decision remained unresolved: acquire a small company with proven formulas, or assemble a professional team and develop the first products from the ground up?

Girls’ Generation’s world-tour schedule made the calendar even tighter. Jessica would not be free to take an executive role seriously until at least the middle of the following year.

For the moment, the delay gave Ji-ho something rare.

Uninterrupted time for his English-language album.

He retrieved a card from the system inventory.

The works originate in later real-world years; their appearance here is part of the novel’s alternate-history system premise.

Ji-ho read the titles twice.

He had expected songs such as “See You Again” or “Shape of You.” The alternatives were no less formidable.

“7 Years,” originally associated with Lukas Graham in the future he remembered, would become a worldwide chart leader and earn Grammy nominations for Record of the Year and Song of the Year. Its greatest strength was not vocal spectacle but structure: an entire life compressed into a few minutes.

“Stay with Me” would become one of Sam Smith’s defining records, collecting major Grammy recognition across Record of the Year, Song of the Year, and pop performance categories.

The remaining songs were not filler.

FUTURE IMPACT · SYSTEM REFERENCE
❧ ◇ ❧
A Global Singles Portfolio

“Someone You Loved” · “Let Me Down Slowly” · “Say You Won’t Let Go” · “Perfect” · “Blinding Lights”

Future chart records, streaming totals, certifications, and awards vary by territory and year. The card selects them for proven emotional and commercial reach rather than one identical metric.

“Someone You Loved” would grow from a stark piano ballad into one of the United Kingdom’s defining streaming-era songs. “Let Me Down Slowly” would escape its quiet beginning and travel globally through emotional restraint rather than vocal excess.

“Say You Won’t Let Go” and “Perfect” would become staples of weddings and proposals across multiple countries.

Then there was “Blinding Lights”: four weeks atop the Billboard Hot 100, the year-end No. 1 song of 2020, and a ninety-week chart run that would redefine longevity for a modern pop single.

For a first Western album, the card had supplied more than enough power.

The coming market would not be easy. Late 2014 would bring “Uptown Funk,” which would explode in 2015. That year would also belong to “See You Again” and Adele’s “Hello.” In 2016, “Closer” and “7 Years” would dominate enormous parts of the global conversation.

But if Ji-ho released this collection before those songs existed in the public mind, he would not enter the Western market as a hopeful Korean export.

He would enter carrying its future standards.

Of the seven, “7 Years” held him longest.

Its opening evoked childhood with almost cruel efficiency—the age when one believed a single summer could last forever. European pop-ballad writing, understated percussion, and hints of rock created a progression that felt larger with every verse.

Seven. Twenty. Thirty. Sixty.

Each age became a doorway into another version of the same life.

For Ji-ho, the twenty-year section struck closest. He had arrived in Korea at eighteen, entered the trainee system at nineteen, and by twenty had become one of the most discussed figures in Asian entertainment.

The generated lyric could not remain untouched. Its emotional architecture was right, but the details had to belong to him: Los Angeles nights, Koreatown bars, a one-way flight, empty practice rooms, and the stubborn American-born kid who refused to believe poverty or origin could decide the size of his life.

Ji-ho entered the studio and began rewriting.

Hours vanished into syllables.

“Grammys,” he murmured at last, looking at the unfinished page. “I’m coming.”

At six in the evening, Ji-ho finally emerged from the studio and released a long breath.

Rewriting lyrics looked effortless from the outside. In practice, changing one autobiographical detail could disturb rhyme, stress, melody, and the emotional logic of the entire verse.

His secretary approached and bowed.

“Hoejang-nim. The front desk received this file for you.”

“Thank you.”

Ji-ho carried the envelope to his office. Inside was the private investigator’s report on the T-ara controversy.

The first section concerned messages sent to Lee Ah-reum before a Music Bank recording on July 27, 2012.

Ji-ho sat straighter.

If the allegation was true, Areum’s silence—and the anonymous staff member’s refusal to appear—became easier to understand.

It did not explain Kim Kwang-soo.

CCM had CJ behind it. An affiliated director in a criminal network should not have been enough to make the company surrender T-ara’s reputation without resistance.

Ji-ho reviewed the final pages.

The Record · Kim Kwang-soo, the Turbo Years

During the Turbo era, Kim Kwang-soo ran his company with violence and with gang help. Member Kim Jung-nam accused him publicly: ashtrays thrown, chairs smashed. If Kim Jong-kook tried to leave, Kim Kwang-soo sent gang members to hunt him down, drag him back, and beat him — with the standing threat that “if you cry, we replace the rapper.” Mikey confirmed it: Kim Jong-kook dragged to the basement, the sounds of things breaking coming up through the floor.

When Kim Jong-kook finally went solo, the intimidation simply changed channels — obstruction, blacklisting, years of locked-up music releases and vanished broadcast slots.

And in 2013, with all of Korea turned against T-ara, the group still walked onto Music Bank stages as if by right. That was Kim Kwang-soo: gang connections and capital connections, spent freely to keep the lights on.

What mattered to Ji-ho was the unresolved pattern.

Kim Kwang-soo had survived decades in an industry where capital, broadcasters, managers, and hard-edged personal networks overlapped. Even after T-ara’s public collapse, the group continued securing major music-show stages and overseas schedules.

So why had CCM not released the threatening messages in 2012, when they might have shifted the entire public narrative?

Fear of Beomseobangpa?

A private relationship with the family?

Or a motive the investigator had not yet uncovered?

Ji-ho closed the file and rubbed the space between his brows.

The evidence was useful. The explanation was not complete.

He took out his phone and called Jiyeon.

✦ ✦ ✦
T-ara dormitory · early evening one ringtone and an immediate change of plans

Jiyeon lay on the practice-room floor, staring at the ceiling without seeing it.

Practice held no appeal. Her thoughts kept returning to Ji-ho.

Should she call?

Send a message first?

He was a chairman now. What if she interrupted something important?

Then memory supplied the way he had kissed her and the quiet certainty with which he had told her he liked her. Warmth climbed into her face.

That made her his girlfriend, didn’t it? One of them, perhaps—but still.

Her ringtone began playing “If You.”

Jiyeon snatched up the phone. The contact name on the screen read:

♥ Unfaithful Boyfriend ♥

She sat upright as if electrified, cleared her throat, and answered in the gentlest voice she could manufacture.

“Yeoboseyo?”

“Jiyeon-ah. Can you meet me tonight?”

Her smile escaped before she could restrain it.

“Tonight? Of course.”

“I’ll pick you up.”

“Pick me up? Mm…”

She looked down at her sweat-damp T-shirt and immediately gave him the dormitory address.

The moment the call ended, Jiyeon sprang to her feet with a delighted scream.

“Aaaah!”

Hyomin flinched beside her.

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon. What is wrong with you?”

“Eonni, I’m leaving.”

Jiyeon threw both arms around Hyomin and planted an exuberant kiss on her cheek.

“Tell Eunjung eonni I’m missing tonight’s practice.”

“Where are you going?”

“I’ll tell you later.”

She fled toward the dormitory like a bird released from a cage.

Inside, she went straight to the shower. Warm water washed away the practice-room sweat while she rehearsed possible greetings and rejected all of them.

Then came the wardrobe.

After trying several outfits, Jiyeon chose a soft pink dress. She turned before the mirror twice, judged it acceptable, and moved to her makeup table.

Her preparation became a careful ritual: natural brows, restrained eyeshadow, lashes separated rather than overloaded, a touch of blush, and a moist rose-beige lip color. She finished with the lightest veil of perfume.
