Chapter Fifty-Four

First Place!

And under the shades, behind the delivery, Ji-ho was twelve years away — in the other life, the one where he’d spent everything he had on the dream and watched reality grind it flat anyway; where surrender had curdled into a decade of drifting. All of it — the complicated, unhealed weight of it — poured straight down the microphone.

With the Hip-Hop Kid running at full effect, the emotion broke over the hall like a levee failing. The performance space had stopped being an evaluation. It was, for these few minutes, Ji-ho’s first concert.

Performance

His voice tears suddenly bright and full-force — a rasping, burn-the-boats register — the sound of a boy shouting his own name back into himself: If it’s too late to turn around, then I put it down and look forward. Those burning eyes. That white-hot dream. Shout it at the world — shine like the brightest star. The closing bars: all the questions of those years, all the sweat poured out uncounted, all the cold tears swallowed instead of shed — and over the top of it, rising, the resolve to begin again.

Verse to verse, the emotional architecture climbed exactly as built — the early struggle, the mid-song answer to every doubter, the final open sky of it. Pitch and weight and the handling of every phrase-tail carried the crowd up the staircase with him, and by the last chord the hall was a single held breath.

“Whew— thank you. Thank you, judges.”

He bowed deep.

Clap…

Clap-clap…

Scattered at first — and then the room detonated.

CLAP-CLAP-CLAP-CLAP-CLAP!

“SONG JI-HO! SONG JI-HO! SONG JI-HO!”

Resonance that complete has nowhere to go but out of people’s mouths — the hall boiled over, trainees on their feet, chanting.

The One’s famously narrow eyes had achieved maximum aperture. That’s my disciple? That’s MY disciple. I struck GOLD. He was applauding hard enough to hurt.

Irene stood staring at the boy on the stage, and the ledger settled itself in her chest: lost. Completely, unconditionally lost — swindled by him AGAIN. And yet. The figure up there was so blindingly bright — and that song had written out her own three years, every drop of the bitterness and the grind and the refusal, like he’d been in the room for all of it. The talent was blinding. And then the other memory surfaced, uninvited and suddenly vivid: his arms around her, his back to the motorcycle, the heat of him, the heartbeat under her cheek, the clean soap smell—

Her heart bolted like a startled deer. Her hand came up over it. Why is it so LOUD—

“Eonni! EONNI!” Seung-wan had her by both shoulders, shaking, shrieking. “He’s SO COOL!! AAAAH!! How does someone WRITE a song like that?!”

“SONG JI-HO! SONG JI-HO!”

The half-month coffee-runner plan had died without a funeral. Wendy, avowed mocker, was now a fangirl at full crimson boil.

At the judges’ table, Lee Soo-man applauded with the long view running behind his eyes. If the song is genuinely his — the boy’s value just changed categories. A Korean-American with native English and a straight line into the American market. The hero halo — the public’s goodwill toward him here is bulletproof, the casual-viewer appeal unbeatable. The face is flawless, the vocal is heavyweight, no stage fright — and look at the details: costume chosen precisely for the genre, crowd-work gestures, pacing. That is NOT a rookie’s stagecraft. Add composition at this level… I’m looking at a star in the raw.

The problem: EXO just launched. No new boy group for years. Will he wait? …No. A boy like this doesn’t wait. Then how does the company hold him? A solo?

He glanced sideways at The One, whose eyes had disappeared entirely into a grin.

“Soon-won-ah. You’ve taken in quite the disciple.”

“I only taught the singing. The writing — that’s entirely the boy’s own gift.”

“That song will chart.” Yoo Young-jin leaned toward the founder, voice low. “Soo-man hyung — this company passed on Park Jin-young and G-Dragon over their looks, and we’ve regretted it twice. This kid’s face has no argument against it anywhere.”

Lee Soo-man laughed out loud — because the face was, frankly, his favorite line item. What was an idol who didn’t look like one?

“First place for this month’s evaluation — I propose Ji-ho. Objections?”

He scanned the table. None. It wasn’t close.

He rose and walked to the stage, and the hall hushed around his footsteps. A warm hand landed on Ji-ho’s shoulder.

“This song — ‘A Boy,’ yes? You wrote it?”

“Ne, Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim. The copyright’s already registered.”

“Hah! GOOD.” The founder’s laugh rolled across the hall; then a beat of deliberation. “Your rank — we’ll set it at A. For now.”

For NOW? As in — pre-debut team pending?! The excitement nearly cracked the composure.

“Thank you, Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim!”

In an excellent mood — finding ore like this did that — Lee Soo-man turned to the hall and made it official.

“First place, month-end evaluation, April 2012 — I don’t imagine there will be any dispute. Ji-ho: first place, and promotion to A-rank!”

In the entire history of SM Entertainment: the first trainee — and likely the last — to take first place at month-end in his first month in the building.

CLAP-CLAP-CLAP-CLAP!

The applause came down like weather. Skill was the world’s one universal currency: sing well, dance well, look good — all negotiable. But the ability to write at this level — that was the asset no one could argue with. And every trainee in the hall who had ever looked down on the foreign kid was now performing the same rapid internal calculation: befriend him NOW, before the price of admission goes up forever.

And in one corner, the trainee who had once blocked a hallway and demanded a deeper bow stood with a face the color of paper. Ryu Tae-jun — two years of training, still B-rank — had just watched the new kid take first place with the exact specialty Tae-jun had built his whole identity on. Rap. Two years of his life had been converted, in four minutes, into a punchline. The envy came first. Then the colder thought, arriving with sweat: he’s A-rank now. Is he going to come for me? …Maybe I should apologize first. Maybe TODAY.

Center stage, Ji-ho stood in the roar and let it wash over him, grinning without restraint. Nobody alive could refuse this feeling — every eye in the building, every voice, all of it pointed at him. Vanity was human nature, and tonight he declined to apologize for it.

Entertainment industry — Ji-ho is officially on his way.
