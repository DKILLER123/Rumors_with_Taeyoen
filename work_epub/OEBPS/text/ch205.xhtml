Chapter Two Hundred Five

Sunny, You Could Grow Five Centimeters

June 5, 2013 · the smallest hours The villa — a dark hallway, a study, a hidden cabinet one long hair in a bathtub, one box of evidence — and a conversation that cuts to the bone

In the stillness, Sunny’s eyes opened in the dark.

The hair. The long hair in the bathtub drain would not stop replaying.

The shared bathroom — she’d used it FIRST tonight. And Taeyeon had never set foot in this house before today. So the long feminine hair already in that tub belonged to… whom, exactly? Had a woman lived here — BEFORE Taeyeon?

And once suspicion opened its eye, the details lined up and saluted. This afternoon: Taeyeon takes his call, announces free time — and then somehow doesn’t leave for nearly two hours, because he “had things to finish.” The diffuser downstairs, aggressively fragrant — who saturates a house in pomegranate blossom on a random Tuesday, unless something needs covering? If he’d pre-cleaned the house, then the woman’s things hadn’t left. They were HIDDEN. Somewhere inside these walls.

Ji-ho. You bastard. Taeyeon is my sister. I am not watching her be played.

She lay perfectly still until Yuri’s breathing beside her went long and even. Then, with glacial patience, she folded back the blanket, set bare feet on cool floorboard, and made no sound at all. A shadow slid out of the guest room and dissolved into the hallway dark.

The height secret didn’t matter anymore. Evidence did. Catch him — expose him to Taeyeon’s face. That was the whole mission now.

She pressed her lips thin and began to work through the sleeping house by feel. The unfamiliar floor plan nearly cost her a vase at the hallway corner; she caught it two-handed, heart drumming against her ribs.

And almost in the same moment, one floor down, on the sofa — his eyes opened.

The air was too quiet. And the quiet had a texture to it — the specific texture of someone trying very hard not to exist. Not imagination. Certainty.

He rose without a light, bare feet silent on the boards, a night cat parsing the darkness for signal.

Roughly three minutes later: the faintest rustle from the upstairs study.

His chest tightened. He took the stairs fast and soundless.

✦   ✦   ✦

Sunny crouched at the recessed cabinet beside the bookcase, fingertips on a box that had been deliberately buried deep.

She eased it open. Inside: several sets of women’s sleepwear, soft-woven, expensive — and toiletries.

Sized visibly NOT for Taeyeon. Sized, if anything… close to her own frame.

Her breath snagged.

There it is. Ji-ho, you absolute son of a—

“Nuna.”

The voice came flat and level from behind her.

Her whole body seized. She turned, slowly.

He flipped the light switch. Under the sudden lamp his face was unreadable, mild.

“Did no one ever teach you that going through other people’s belongings is illegal?”

She rose to her feet. The first flush of panic burned off fast — and what condensed in its place was colder.

She lifted her chin, and the mockery came up in her eyes.

“And YOU, Ji-ho? Did no one ever teach you that men who row two boats get the hatchet?”

Their eyes locked. The air pulled taut as wire.

He let the silence sit a moment. Then his face cooled.

“That box isn’t yours to touch. And I owe you no explanations. You’ve crossed the line twice tonight. Tomorrow morning, first thing — you leave.”

She stepped FORWARD instead, face tipped up at him, unblinking.

“Correct. You owe ME nothing. And TAEYEON? Do you owe HER an explanation, or not?”

“Ji-ho. When I found that hair in the bathroom, I said NOTHING — that was me giving you face. Now the evidence is sitting in a box in your study. Tomorrow, YOU tell Taeyeon yourself. Clearly. All of it.”

The bathroom. The HAIR. — One strand. One single strand, out of an entire counter-surveilled, department-store-camouflaged operation. The classic hole in the hundred-layer net.

She moved to walk past him. His hand closed on her wrist.

“Nuna. Turning my house inside out in the dark — was that REALLY about a few strands of hair and a hunch about hidden women?”

She glared up at him. “What is THAT supposed to mean?”

“Wasn’t it about the height secret?”

Silence.

Because — yes. Of course it was. Or it had been. She was DONE with the 155-centimeter life. Done with the upward camera angle, done with the kindergarten jokes, done with being the punchline that smiled first. She wanted the secret — whatever it was, whatever it cost. She’d cased this house all afternoon and found nothing. She’d planned tonight’s search before the soju was even open. She knew exactly how low the act was. And she’d watched Taeyeon come back TALLER — taller, glowing, suddenly ranked first on every beauty poll that mattered: most beautiful singer on the peninsula, top-five face in the world per that annual list, the Forbes celebrity board, the Gallup idol-preference survey — the name Taeyeon climbing every chart in real time. The wanting had nearly eaten her whole. But after tonight — after Sica, after the shouting — the appetite had died. What was left was the hair in the drain, and the box in the cabinet, and a sister being deceived. And THAT, she would not swallow.

She wrenched at her wrist. “Yah. Let go. SHIBAL — I said let GO.”

He released her. She stepped back and looked at him with open contempt.

“Remember what I said. Tomorrow, you explain it to Taeyeon. Or I will personally show her exactly what kind of man you are.”

“Hahahaha.”

The laugh came out of him low and genuinely amused.

“What are you laughing at?”

The amusement dropped off his face between one breath and the next. “Why would I take orders from you? This is between me and Taeyeon.”

“You can ignore me.” Her smile went thin and cold. “And I can also walk down that hallway right now, wake Taeyeon up, and let this whole house see you clearly.”

“Go ahead.” He even gestured toward the door — after you. “Wake her. Bring her here.”

The sheer nerve of it made her laugh through her anger. WHERE does this confidence come from?

She spun on her heel — genuinely going for the door, genuinely going to wake the house—

“Oh — and one more thing. Tiffany is also my woman. Call her in too. Saves a second trip.”

Her body stopped as if it had hit glass.

She turned back around, very slowly, and her voice came out scraped dry.

“Mwo?? You— what did you just say?”

“Exactly what you heard.” He advanced a step. “Fany — is also mine. Go on. Call them BOTH in.”

He kept coming; she kept retreating.

“And then Girls’ Generation comes apart at the seam. The tour four days from now — gone. The company, the staff, months of nine women’s preparation — vapor. All of it, tonight, on your signal.”

Her back met the wall. Her head was white noise.

“No— impossible. FANY? Never— you’re lying to me. Tell me you’re lying—”

“Call them in and cross-examine. Isn’t that precisely what you wanted?”

“You—!!” Her eyes rimmed red as she stared up at him. “You’re THREATENING me. You bastard— you must have deceived Fany somehow, you—” her hand came up and swung, “—you’re GARBAGE!”

He caught her wrist without effort.

“What I am — isn’t yours to adjudicate. That belongs to Taeyeon. And to Fany.”

“Ji-ho, are you even HUMAN? Taeyeon and Fany are — they’re each other’s closest— you’re playing BOTH of them and you’re not afraid of hell?!”

He gave a cold half-laugh — and then, quietly:

“Sunny. Your father. Your uncle Lee Soo-man. Do they each have exactly one woman?”

“Of course they do!”

“Hm.” He took her chin in two fingers and tipped her face up, made her hold his eyes. “Look at me and say it again. You genuinely believe that?”

Her gaze flickered — and slid. Her father, and her uncle: good to their families, yes. Publicly impeccable. And outside the walls of the house— …she had heard things. Everyone had heard things. No one had ever said things.

“I half-suspected you were actually that naïve.” He released her chin, and his tone went dry and edged. “So they may, and I may not. Is the deficiency in my conduct — or in my RANK?”

“Sunny. You know the truth already. You’re not innocent — you’re CALIBRATED. You run the standard-issue sliding scale: for men at the top of the pyramid, the moral dial turns itself down. Family patriarchs, resource holders — as long as the foundation doesn’t shake, it’s all filed under ‘private matters, no harm done.’ Have I said one false word yet?”

Cold climbed her legs from the floorboards. She reached for a rebuttal and found the shelf empty.

“You’re— talking nonsense—”

“Then answer one question.” Another step; his voice stayed level, which made it worse. “The same conduct, in your uncle’s stratum: you people go quiet, look away, tidy it out of sight. In ME — suddenly you’re holding the sword of heaven. Why? Is it that my fortune and my position haven’t yet bought my way into the circle your family’s name protects?”

“Or is it something colder — that in your unconscious ledger, Taeyeon and Fany, your SISTERS, are finally outsiders: satellites who might attach themselves to a stronger body, or get hurt by one — and whose feelings can be weighed and traded when the group’s stability and the resource-holder’s authority demand it?”

“I don’t—”

“You do.” He cut her off — and the strange thing was that his voice carried something close to pity. “Look at the exact center of your anger tonight. You’re screaming at me for playing with hearts, aching for a sisterhood betrayed. It’s moving, nuna. Truly. But examine it. Is what enrages you REALLY the death of some pure idea of love?”

“No. What enrages you is the machine — the old patriarchal order fused to modern capital, the exact structure you hate and depend on in the same breath. Taeyeon and Fany are top-tier idols. And inside this structure, even THEY reflexively orbit toward a stronger resource core. And I — I merely used the machine. Did the thing powerful men have done for a thousand years while society filed it under ‘understandable.’”

She leaned against the wall because the wall was available. His words had gone through the moral outrage like a blade through wet paper — and behind it stood the actual architecture: her father’s unexplained absences; the company’s hushed “entertainment” folklore; the industry’s thousand silent handshakes. When THOSE things surfaced, had she — had anyone — ever once raised a flag? No: silence, management, understanding. Men are like that, once they’ve made it. Show me the rich man who isn’t.

“You’re— building excuses for being SHAMELESS—” she managed, but the pressure had gone out of it.

“No. I’m stating the rules of the game as they exist.” His voice settled, even and unhurried. “I’m not litigating right and wrong. I’m telling you who WROTE the rules — a thousand years of clan law, the rank-order everyone pretends is culture, capital’s grip on every resource that matters, and a gender structure tilted so far it’s load-bearing. Under those rules, morality FLOATS. It reprices itself against money and power in real time.”

“Since antiquity: where the money and power pool, the beautiful women arrive. Tell me I’m wrong. You’ve lived in this industry your whole adult life, nuna. The idol trade itself — under the singing, under the choreography — WHAT is the actual product?”

She pressed her lips shut. No rebuttal came, because none existed.

He watched the fog and the hurt condense in her eyes, and delivered the last of it, quietly.

“You can wake them right now. Lay everything on the table. And the outcomes are enumerable: they hurt — and then, the way this society has trained ten million women before them, they weigh the stability, the feelings, the practical stakes — and they accommodate. Or: they break. And Girls’ Generation loses its keel, and the nine of you lose the one resource platform that holds the rest up.”

“Your anger is real, Sunny. But its target is mis-assigned. Don’t aim it at one piece of garbage named Ji-ho.”

He looked at her steadily.

“Aim it at the thing that lets your father’s conduct pass unremarked. That routes women as accomplished as Taeyeon and Fany into dependency orbits anyway. That has you pinned against this wall at three in the morning, torn exactly in half. The structure wearing the name ‘tradition’ — inequality, running in the peninsula’s bones.”

A pause, and a self-mocking curl at the corner of his mouth.

“Me? I’m just the mirror that doesn’t bother lying about it. The ugly one you can finally see the room in.”

Dead silence. Only her breathing, uneven, in the lamp-lit study. Every moral charge she’d carried up those stairs had struck a wall poured from history, culture and cold current reality — and what stood in front of her no longer looked like a best friend’s trash boyfriend. It looked like the entire world she profited from and suffocated inside, wearing one man’s unforgivably calm face.

And the machine was real; she knew its schematics by heart. Modern money and power concentrated harder than any dynasty’s: the men who held it held the jobs, the ladders, the megaphones, the narratives. Which produced two permanent artifacts. First: the willing trade — resources for proximity, filed by everyone involved under “consenting adults,” never under “morality.” Second: the managed reputation — PR, press, connections, the halo purchasable at scale, the flaws expunged from record. The old culture had always priced a man by rank and achievement and discounted his private conduct to zero. The modern peninsula had simply automated it. Success-first, the ancestral operating system — “cultivate the self, order the family, govern the state” had always been a ladder pointed at GOVERNANCE, not at goodness. Add the seniority pyramid with its licensed cruelties. Add an economy where married women vanished into unpaid service regardless of what they’d been before — where a woman could out-earn a small company and still be expected to fold laundry for her in-laws the day after the wedding. Where the professional fortresses — law, medicine, prosecution — stayed male, so the girls flooded the one meritocracy with open doors: nine female applicants for every male trainee. Where the pressure ran so hot that schoolgirls scheduled surgeries around exam season, and parents banked funds for their daughters’ faces the way other countries banked for college. A country that had, in consequence, quietly become one of the most accepting places on earth of the successful man with many women — accepting BY the women, first and most of all.

She straightened off the wall, finally, and rebuilt her composure piece by piece.

“You’re swapping concepts and you know it.” Her voice came back low and level. “But — fine. I’ll grant it. What you’re describing exists. And it’s everywhere.”

“If Taeyeon and Fany accept it — then I have nothing to say. I’d even owe you one compliment: you have ability.”

“Because look at you, by the numbers, RIGHT NOW: one singer. In fame, in wealth, in position — TODAY — you rank below both of them. How you got Fany to walk in willingly, I genuinely cannot imagine.”

“But hiding it from Taeyeon — that’s not power, that’s a thick face and an empty hand. Textbook trash-man conduct. Tell me I’m wrong.”

“You’re not wrong.” He nodded, accepting the whole charge without discount. “I don’t dispute a word of it.”

“When I met Taeyeon nuna — I was a trainee with nothing. So: yes. For now, I hide it. From her, and about Fany. That’s the truth of my position.”

“But I don’t consider myself in her debt.”

He crossed the space between them, bent to her ear, and dropped his voice to almost nothing.

“I DO have a method for growing taller.”

Her breath stopped.

“Taeyeon nuna’s five centimeters — that was mine. My method.”

“And that method goes to MY women. Only mine.”

“Fany nuna will be getting taller too, by and by. Try not to look too shocked when it happens.”

He straightened up and regarded her — the shock, and beneath the shock, the thing she’d never once managed to hide from anyone: the wanting.

He smiled.

“With your foundations, nuna — five centimeters, give or take.”

“And I confess I’m honestly curious what a person THIS committed to the height question will choose to do — now that she’s finished calling me garbage.”

He turned and walked out. At the doorway he paused, not looking back.

“Oh — and Taeyeon nuna: not your concern anymore. I’ll tell her myself. I’ll choose the time.”

“But not now.”
