The ballad was finished at forty-one minutes past four in the morning, and nobody in the building was awake to see it.

“Stay with Me” came in at three minutes and twenty-nine seconds, which is twenty-two seconds shorter than the version the choir had sung into a church in Gangbuk-gu in July, and the reason for the difference is that the man who cut it had stopped believing in the second verse. The gospel line had gone. The children had stayed. There was a frame near the end — four seconds, a woman’s hand coming off a piano lid — and the editor had asked, twice, in writing, whether it was going to stay, and the answer had been the same both times, in the same three words: it stays, it is the point.

That was the record. The morning had other things in it.

Girls’ Generation had landed at Gimpo the previous afternoon from Jakarta, on a charter that had been moved forward by six hours without anybody telling the department that had written their schedules, which meant that nine women had arrived at an airport in the rain to a car that came for seven of them; that the two who were left had got a taxi; and that by the time the first of them had reached the dormitory there was already a photograph of the whole thing on a portal with five thousand comments under it, most of them about a bag.

And since Thursday afternoon, on the thirty-first floor, in the room with the glass wall and the table that seats fourteen, a man from Los Angeles had been asking questions in the flat patient way of somebody who is paid by the answer.

There is a way to shoot a room of forty people being a crowd, and it is to tell them they are going to have to applaud on a cue, and then to give them the cue, and then to make them do it until it stops being a cue.

They did it nine times. The first six were the applause of a room that has been told to be enthusiastic, which is a sound like rain on a plastic sheet. The seventh was better because a man at the back fell off his stool and the room laughed and then applauded the laugh. And then, because he had been in that room before, and knew what a man at the end of a long table does when he is given an answer he does not want — the two fingers on the paper, the breath, the small turning of the head towards a window he has looked out of ten thousand times — he let the silence do the rest of it, and the rest of it came.

“That is the most arrogant thing I have heard in this building in eleven months,” said Lee Soo-man, “and the reason I cannot argue with it is that it is not arrogance, it is arithmetic. You have been in nine hundred and forty days of rooms. Mine, other people’s, on a bus, on a floor, behind a wall with a vent in it, and there is not one person in this country under the age of fifty who has watched as much being made by as many fools as you have, and none of them have got anything out of it, because they were watching a person and you were watching a method.” He tapped the schedule once. “And now you are going to tell me that you have put yourself in the video as well.”

“I have not put myself in the video.”

“There is a shot list on page four with a man in it, in a bar, at two in the morning, at the end of the third segment, and the only reason it is not you in it is that you have got a crew of eleven and a lawyer in a corridor, and I have been reading this company for thirty years and I know what a shot list that size costs, and it does not cost what you have paid, which is nothing, which means you have been doing it on a weekend, in the same clothes, yourself.” Lee Soo-man picked up the pen on his desk and looked at it as though it had said something rude to him. “You are the Chairman of the biggest distributor in this country, and you are shooting music videos for free in a bar in Hongdae with a light from a refrigerator, and my girls are in it, and they are in it because a man from Los Angeles is going to see a reel on Monday and think: this one is going to be impossible to sign, and I would like to be in the room when he is not.”

“Is that a compliment?” said Song Ji-ho.

“It is an observation,” said Lee Soo-man, and signed the document, on all three pages, with a stroke that pulled the paper forward on the desk, and pushed it across, and did not look at it again, and then said, in a voice that had gone back to being a chairman’s, without any of the arithmetic left in it: “Clause three is not nothing. You are going to write my syllabus, and you are going to write it for the trainees and not for me, and if it is any good I am going to take it away from you and put it in a room where it will be used on nine hundred children a year, and if it is bad you are going to correct it at your own expense, and both of those outcomes are worse for you than the fee.”

“Yes, seonsaengnim.”

“And,” said Lee Soo-man, “there is one more thing, and I want it said back to me in the words, because I have been unable to make anybody in this industry say it to me in the words for four years.” He took the glasses off again, and put them down, and the room understood, at that moment, all at once, that the fourth floor of a building in Seongsu-dong had been waiting for the sentence too. “Why did you bring a music video to my desk on a Sunday at four o’clock in the afternoon, when you have got thirty-one floors and a legal team with nothing to do? And you may not tell me it was courtesy.”

Song Ji-ho stood up from a chair he had been in for four minutes, with three pages in one hand, and put his other hand flat on the table, and said:

“Because if I had sent it, somebody here would have lost it, and then I would have owed you for the girls, and I do not want to owe you for the girls. I want you to have said yes in a room with two witnesses in it, and I want the four names on a page with your signature on it, and I want—” and here the man who had been told by a dozen reporters that he feels nothing, in a corridor, on a step, in a building he owns, stopped, and started again, and finished the sentence in a voice nobody on the fourth floor had ever heard him use in public: “I want you to know that they were not a trade this time. There is no deal in it. It is a video, and the reason I came is that I have been in your rooms since I was nineteen, and I have never once asked you for something I was going to pay for with a piece of paper about children.”

The silence after that had the quality of a room in which several people have decided not to move. The woman from the management desk put her file down. The general counsel looked, for the first time, directly at the Chairman of LOEN Entertainment. And Lee Soo-man, who had made three men cry in that chair and had been on the other end of a sentence like that one in 1995, with a woman who had not been paid for two years, looked up at him and said:

“Get out.”

“Yes, seonsaengnim.”

“And take your secretary. She has been standing in a doorway for fifty minutes and there is a woman on my desk at eleven tomorrow who wants to hire people who sit down.”

Chae Soo-bin, in a doorway, with a folder that she had been holding in front of her like a licence, went the colour of the wall, and the general counsel looked at the ceiling, and Lee Soo-man had already begun to read the schedule again, which is how it was known in that building that the meeting had been had.

They got the four of them out of a room in the basement of the SM building at half past eleven that night, and the four segments that were left went into the machine in a room on the eighth floor of a tower on Yeongdong-daero at three in the morning, and the whole of the thing was two minutes and forty-eight seconds long by the time the sun came up on the Monday, and it had eight women in it and one man, and the man was in it for nine seconds, and he was not looking at the camera for any of them.

Seo Ju-hyun was not asked. There is a clause from the spring about her schooling, and every manager on the shoot knew it, and not one of them said it out loud, and the absence is the reason the video has eight names in it and not nine, and the reason a girl in a group of nine is going to be the one person in the country who watches it on the third of October and is not surprised at all.

The man from Los Angeles watched it in a room on the thirty-first floor at four on a Monday afternoon — he had been told by his own office four times that he was going home on Sunday, and had not gone — on a screen that had been turned off and on again twice, and the nine seconds of himself did not make him look up.

“Who cut it?” he said, at the end.

“I did,” said Song Ji-ho.

“No,” said the man from Los Angeles, in the flat patient voice of a person who is paid by the answer and had just got one he did not expect. “Who cut it with you?”

“A girl on the eighth floor with a red pen,” said Song Ji-ho, “who has been doing it for four days and is going to be here for four more, and who is not on a list of anybody’s anything, and if that is the thing you are trying to work out about me, it is the only thing that is not on the sheet.”

And on the third floor of a building in Seongsu-dong with a bicycle chained to a railing, at twenty past four on a Monday afternoon, a woman came home from the market to find her elder daughter sitting on the floor under a sheet of paper pinned above a desk, and the sheet had a line added to it over the weekend in a smaller hand, and the mother stood at the door of her own sitting room and read it out loud, because she was a person who had stopped being able to help herself:

He filmed it himself, Mum. Himself. And the rabbit is still on top of the fridge.

Which is not how it had happened, and not where the video was going to be put, and not the last time in four years that a child in a corridor was going to be more accurate about Song Ji-ho than the people he paid.

The album came out on the third of October, and in the eighteen days before it, the thing the country was arguing about was not the record, and not the man from Los Angeles, and not the number of centimetres a nineteen-year-old had grown on a schedule: it was a music video in which eight women had been looked at, carefully, one at a time, by somebody who had learned to do it in a corridor; and at the end of it, in a low room with a ceiling that had been built for a hundred people, a girl stood up on a stool in front of forty strangers and asked them to be quiet, and they were, and that part had not been planned, and it stayed in.
