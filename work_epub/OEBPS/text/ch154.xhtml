Chapter One Hundred Fifty-Four

The Film Project

Late December 2012 → January 2013 Seoul — the car, the apartment, LOEN HQ the settlement, the ignition, and a script

“Right — Ji-ho, your quarterly settlement went out. Did you see it?”

He surfaced from the system interface and smiled at a visibly buzzing Dong-hwan.

“Saw it the second the bank text landed, hyung.”

“TWENTY-FOUR BILLION won!” The whisper failed completely as a whisper. “In three months— wah. Unreal.”

QUARTERLY SETTLEMENT · Q4 2012
Gucci + L’Oréal signing tranches (50% on signature)₩7.0B
Album sales, digital downloads & streaming royalties₩11.1B
Samsung, Binggrae & other endorsements₩5.9B
Total₩24,000,000,000

The number startled even him. The Ace Endorser title wasn’t prestige — it was a money press. And the quiet arithmetic underneath: on SM’s old trainee split, this quarter would have paid him barely one billion; in a nine-member team, a hundred-odd million each. He sent one more silent thank-you to the version of himself that had walked.

“Dong-hwan hyung. Everyone’s been running on fumes for months. I’m putting up four hundred million won as the team’s year-end bonus — distribute it for me?”

“FOUR HUNDRED— Ji-ho, that’s too much—”

“It isn’t.” A wave of the hand. “The makeup nuna gets up at three a.m. The stylists have never missed once. The assistants haul equipment through every airport in Asia with me. Nine people — that’s forty-some million each. About a year’s salary. Call it my heart, in transferable form.”

Dong-hwan’s eyes went warm. In all his years in this industry, artists who reached the summit and thought FIRST of the people carrying the bags — he could count them on one hand with fingers left over.

“I’ll distribute it properly. Oh — and Ji-ho. I know a seriously capable planning manager. If you want, I’d like to introduce him.”

“Please.”

“His name is Lee Hyun-woo. University degree in cultural arts management — artist planning, project development, brand partnerships, the whole core curriculum. Past two years he’s been living in the indie scene: positioning underground musicians, building small tours and online rollouts. Turned one busker’s low-budget street video into a mainstream platform invitation.”

“Ability’s not in question. The temper is… direct.”

“A planner needs ability first, hyung. Bring him by when there’s time.”

“Will do — he’s out of the country right now. When he’s back, you’ll meet.”

The van pulled up at his building.

“I’ll book the movers this week.”

“Thanks. Get some sleep, hyung — I’ll be leaning on you again soon enough.”

“Lean away. It’s my honor.”

Forty million won of personal year-end bonus does wonders for morale, he thought, watching the van pull off. Through fire and water, indeed.

Inside, the first order of business: the laptop, and the bank.

₩24,000,000,000. He stared at the digit string while the LOEN plan sharpened another full stop. Keep one billion as living money. Feed twenty-three billion to the machine. At 20× inside a year: 460 billion — more than enough for the acquisition.

He initiated the Wealth Robot.

A thread of light slid into the laptop, and the screen began to drive itself.

DREAM LIFE — WEALTH ROBOT · IGNITION

⟨ DING ⟩ Wealth Robot online. Enter investment amount.

INPUT₩23,000,000,000

SETUPAccount created from player profile · bank card bound · funds transferring

STRATEGYYen depreciation detected; policy trajectory indicates a major Japanese equity rally

PROJECTION20× target achievable in ~6 months

Reminder: no deposits, withdrawals, or borrowed funds once running. The machine is sealed until it finishes.

Six months? Better than planned. He couldn’t recall the exact date the policy dominoes would force SK’s hand on LOEN — but cash in hand early meant holding the initiative. That was the entire game.

The bank text chimed: ₩23B to a fund account named “Dream Life.” Balance: one billion. He wired Dong-hwan the four hundred million for the team. Balance: six hundred million. Done.

Twenty minutes of ab work and squats, a hot shower, and he stretched out on the bed with his phone: Girls’ Generation — Japan. f(x) — Jeju. Joo-hyun — home in Daegu.

Sleep, then.

Creak.

The door across the hall. His mind flicked awake: Bae Soo-bin back? They’d barely crossed paths since that one dinner — and he was moving out in weeks. One neighborly goodbye was owed, at least.

He got up and opened his door — and found her already standing in front of it, hand half-raised, caught mid-decision. Flustered didn’t cover it; her hands couldn’t find anywhere to be.

“I— um. I saw your light on, so…”

“…I mean — congratulations. On the album taking this year’s number one.”

“Thank you.” He smiled. “Come in for tea?”

“Tea? …Okay.”

He set her on the sofa and went for the kettle; before long, lavender steam softened the whole room. He set two cups down and took the seat opposite.

“Time moves fast. I’m moving out next month.”

Something in her chest pinched — she’d known it was coming; famous people don’t keep old apartments — but hearing it still landed. She rallied fast, and the smile she produced was real.

“Mm. As famous as you are now, a better place is only natural. I’m genuinely glad for you. It’s only going up from here.”

“Work keeps multiplying — I need somewhere that fits the schedule. And you? How’ve you been?”

Her eyes lit — the real kind.

“I’ve improved a LOT lately. The company’s planning my debut through stage plays now.”

Her head dipped, shy about it.

“I’ve been working really hard. I want to earn a real career in this.”

“That’s fantastic. Stage work is the hardest test there is — it’ll sharpen everything. You’ll pass it.”

“Mm. I’ll give it everything. And— if I ever become a real actress… maybe there’ll even be a chance to act WITH you someday.”

She watched him after she said it, nervous about the answer. He knew the arc she couldn’t: years of honest, unglamorous work, the breakthrough arriving late and sideways. But the craft was real and the face was real — a future collaboration wasn’t flattery at all.

“Of course there will be. I look forward to watching you burn up a stage. And if that day comes — working together would be a genuinely good time.”

Warmth moved through her like the tea. They talked futures until the room went easy and light.

January 2013 Hannam — the new apartment · LOEN HQ a quiet week, and a printed script

The Hannam residence’s mornings ran quiet and expensive. A week in, he was satisfied: European lines, a hillside elevation that bought privacy and a full sweep of the Seoul grid below. The “Eyes, Nose, Lips” promotion cycle had closed. And he was clear-eyed about the moment: after a run of consecutive miracles, a Korean-American act shining THIS hard in the domestic industry needed to bank some quiet — or invite the wrong kind of attention.

The company, meanwhile, had watched his acting-practice tapes with open surprise — the expressiveness comfortably outran what IU was showing on her current drama set. Shin Min-hyuk himself proposed the plan: films in the first half, music comeback in the second. Which suited him exactly. The main quest still hung in the interface: male lead in a film or drama — reward: the Master Director’s Craft. Command of a set, narrative rhythm, the language of the lens — a skill package that would widen every road he ever walked.

The question was the vehicle. He walked his memory down early 2013: New World — wrapped, February release. That Winter, the Wind Blows — already rolling. I Can Hear Your Voice — script half-written at best. …Wait. He stopped, went to the study, opened the laptop. The system’s script — The Girl in the Sun — reconstructed itself under his hands: a pure-love film. A man saves a cat; the cat returns as a girl; they fall in love. But a cat’s years are short, and the goodbye arrives exactly when the love is fullest — and a cat has nine lives, so the story bends into reincarnation instead of grief. Familiar bones, yes — but the emotional grain was fine, the budget small: 4 billion to make, 2 to market. And above all, it was an ACTOR’S piece.

The next day he walked into LOEN headquarters carrying three bound, printed scripts.

✦   ✦   ✦

The air in the Artist Development office went subtly strange as he handed one copy each to Lee Ji-yeon, Shin Min-hyuk, and Dong-hwan.

“This is—?” Lee Ji-yeon took the volume; the handwritten title on the cover was clean and firm: The Girl in the Sun.

“A film script. I wrote it.”

Three people exchanged one look — hold on, he writes SCRIPTS now? — and then three heads went down. The office fell to paper-sounds. Twenty minutes.

“Ji-ho-ssi. You truly wrote this?” Lee Ji-yeon’s expression had gone complicated.

“I did. I’ll revise any scene live, right now, if you want proof.”

“The story’s… complete. Genuinely complete.” Shin Min-hyuk had his glasses off, rubbing the bridge of his nose. “And the emotional line is handled with real finesse.”

“The nine-lives conceit keeps it out of tragedy,” Lee Ji-yeon added — skepticism fully converted to work-voice. “The subject isn’t novel, but the angle and the emotional density are.”

“The ending’s the clever part.” Dong-hwan closed the back cover and exhaled. “Not a cheap next-life reunion — the reincarnation is implied through detail and left open. The restraint is exactly right.”

“I’ve costed it roughly.” His timing was surgical. “Four billion production, two billion P&A — six total. For the pure-romance segment, break-even lands around twelve billion box office; past fifteen, healthy profit.”

Lee Ji-yeon and Shin Min-hyuk looked at each other: the young man had brought the script AND the market analysis.

“What structure do you want?” Shin Min-hyuk went straight to terms.

“I come in as lead actor and screenwriter, with equity. The company finances production; I take fifteen percent of net. And—” a measured pause — “the female lead. I want final say.”

Silence again. Min-hyuk’s fingers drummed the desk — his thinking tell.

“The script outruns anything we expected, and the writer holding casting authority is standard enough. But Ji-ho — first film, and you’re taking writer AND lead at once. The risk is real.”

“I know. Which is why I picked a small pure-love picture. If it fails, the loss is contained. But if it works—”

He didn’t finish the sentence. Nobody needed him to: if it worked, the word “singer” would stop being a boundary.

“We’ll need a board discussion.” Lee Ji-yeon closed the script. “But personally — I’m backing this. The emotional writing in here… it lands.”

Dong-hwan watched quietly from the side, freshly astonished. The artist he carried bags for kept doing things nobody had a category for.
