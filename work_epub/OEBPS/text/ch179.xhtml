Chapter One Hundred Seventy-Nine

Recording-Booth Benefits

The next day A CUBE Entertainment · recording floor six girls, one producer, one door that should have been knocked on

Next morning, Seoul, clear skies. In plain pale-gray casuals, ball cap and mask, he arrived at A CUBE Entertainment.

The building wasn’t palatial, but on ground this expensive it didn’t need to be; the address itself did the talking. And 2013 Cube was, by any honest reckoning, at full blaze. Newer fans who’d arrive years later would file Cube under “small company” — inaccurately. It held BEAST, digital-chart killers; 4Minute, all edge and identity; Kim Hyuna, the top-tier solo force herself; and the phenomenon-grade project duo Trouble Maker, she and Jang Hyun-seung, detonating charts with every return. Industry consensus ranked Cube the likeliest “fourth power” to stand alongside SM, YG and JYP.

A CUBE, the subsidiary spun up in 2011, existed to run one asset: Apink, the fresh-faced, natural-concept girl group debuted that same year — currently sitting at the exact hinge of their career, needing the one song that would detonate them into the mainstream.

The song he’d brought — “NoNoNo” — was precisely that accelerator. And the “LUV” that would follow it, with its untouchable seventeen consecutive music-show wins, would carry them all the way to the summit, a single girl group out-earning entire companies. That later years of management drift would let the first-generation “pure light” dim after its peak — that was an industry law nobody had repealed. But even so: Apink would stand among the longest-burning girl groups of their era.

The receptionist had clearly been briefed; recognition, a flutter of composure, and he was escorted up to the recording floor.

In the lounge outside the booth, six girls in careful casual wear stood up in unison the moment the door opened, and bowed.

“Annyeonghaseyo!”

He pulled off cap and mask and returned the bow with a warm smile. “Annyeonghaseyo, Apink. Second time we’ve met — no need for the nerves. Relax. Today I’m here to help you record, not to grade you.”

His glance moved down the line: Park Cho-rong, the leader; Son Na-eun, the visual; Jeong Eun-ji and Kim Nam-joo, the vocal engines; Yoon Bo-mi, the lead dancer; Oh Ha-young, the maknae. Six distinct colors. The line, collectively, exhaled.

“Ji-ho-ssi, thank you so much for writing us such a wonderful song!” The leader went first, voice gentle. “We’ll work hard to sing it well!”

“We’ve practiced it SO many times — we love it!” Eun-ji, all open sunshine.

“Jinjja, it’s such a good song. As expected of Ji-ho-ssi.”

“Ne — the whole comeback’s been nerve-racking, but practicing this song honestly calmed us down.”

He understood the heat under the politeness. This comeback carried their whole trajectory — and a certified hit-maker walking in with a song this obviously alive gave the room its first solid floor in months.

“You know the melody and the lyrics — that’s the foundation done.” He waved them into seats and pulled up a chair of his own. “It’s a bright, buoyant song, and it sits exactly on your team’s identity. Aegyo with a spine, girlhood without syrup, and a hook that won’t leave people’s heads. I have full confidence in this record.”

Then the work began. He turned to Eun-ji first.

“Eun-ji-ssi. As main vocal, your voice carries force and narrative — you take the core high sections and the emotional detonation points. On the opening group line — the ‘don’t be sad, no no no’ — I want your color leading from inside the blend.”

The directness snapped the room to attention. He turned to the leader.

“Cho-rong-ssi — your tone is soft with that sweet, milky grain. Verse one, verse two, the third chorus: those lean on exactly that warmth.”

Both women nodded along, fully engaged now. He swept the rest.

“The bridge sections belong to the rest of you, and they’re not filler — they’re the atmosphere and the lift. Bo-mi-ssi’s energy, Nam-joo-ssi’s steel, Na-eun-ssi’s cool clarity, Ha-young-ssi’s sweetness — fold each color into the harmony stack. That’s what gives the song its picture.”

Eyes brightened down the line; vague ideas snapped into shapes.

He distributed the line splits next. How the original timeline had allocated them, he had no idea — he simply cut it by present professional judgment and each voice’s character. Cube had pushed no one forward; the split came out fair. Vocals a little heavier, dance line a little lighter, kill part shared by all — and a team whose internal weather was genuinely good signed off without a murmur.

“Good. Everyone hold your parts — booth time. Who’s first? Eun-ji-ssi?”

“OK!”

One deep breath and she was through the double door, headphones on, glass between them, an OK-sign at the console.

SESSION LOG · “NoNoNo” — LEAD VOCALS & STACKS
01Jeong Eun-ji — core highs, emotional peaks. Note: “Looser. You’re twenty-one, not thirty-one.” Retake: immediate lift.
02Kim Nam-joo — power verified; asked for fine tremor at the bright edge. “Luster needs something under it.” Found on take three.
03Park Cho-rong — steady, warm, exactly on brief. No notes beyond “keep this.”
04Yoon Bo-mi — harmony seated back half a step, clearing space for the lead line.
05Oh Ha-young — tail notes sweetened, stopped short of cloying.
06Son Na-eun — pitch clean; line brought down for blend.

Method: every correction explained, several demonstrated bar-by-bar. Six voices ground pass by pass into one field.

The work was granular and exacting — breath turns, tail-note decay, consonant weight, emotional staging — but never harsh. Each note came with a why, and often a one-line demonstration that ended debates before they started. When Nam-joo’s high register rang out with that lung-powered stability, he nodded privately — Apink’s “iron lungs,” fully as advertised — then asked for the finer thing anyway, and got it.

✦   ✦   ✦

Mid-session break. Staff brought throat-soothing drinks and snacks; the girls melted into chairs, tired and bright-eyed at once.

“Ji-ho-ssi is seriously incredible…” the maknae stage-whispered to Na-eun. “Every single detail — he just CATCHES it.”

“Mm. And he knows what voice each of us should be using. Like he’s met us before.”

Na-eun’s reply was soft, her gaze on the figure beyond the glass partition talking mix ratios with the engineer.

Eun-ji drained half a bottle in one pull. “The genius-producer thing is not marketing. Half those little techniques I’m keeping forever.”

Cho-rong, being the leader, was running longer arithmetic. Watching his working profile, what she mostly felt was gratitude with weight behind it: a top-tier artist-producer had handed a cornered team a song that might bend their whole trajectory — and then shown up in person to grind takes. For an Apink standing at the cliff’s edge, that kindness had a size to it.

Na-eun watched her leader stare into the middle distance, and a small, mischievous circuit closed. She nudged Eun-ji, flicked her eyes meaningfully.

“So like I was saying — every song he’s touched since debut has hit. If only he’d keep writing for us~”

Eun-ji received the pass and sighed theatrically. “If only. But how? The man’s peak-season. Where’s the time—”

“—for a little nugget group like us,” Na-eun agreed, mournful. Then, with tremendous leisure: “Although. There IS a way.”

The rest of the room was already leaning in.

“What way?”

“Look around. This many beauties in one room.” A slow pan of the lounge. “We appoint a representative. She secures him. After that — could he refuse us songs?”

“And who’s the representative?”

“I nominate our leader. Gentle, domestic, takes care of everyone—”

“—and he JUST said the milky voice was full of character—”

The duet was in full harmony now; the rest of the room caught up and piled on with enthusiasm.

“Right?! Eonni, go capture him, and we eat forever!”

“Na-eun-ah, you’re a genius.”

Cho-rong went scarlet and dispensed justice — fists of love, one per conspirator. It bears noting that Park Cho-rong holds a third-degree black belt in aikido, and that the fists of love landed with documentary realism.

“Ah— APPA—!”

“You DESERVE it! Stirring things up! Both of you need re-educating!”

“Who’s stirring~” Na-eun, unrepentant, counted on her fingers from under the leader’s grip. “Look. Handsome. Writes hits. Gentle in the booth all day. Also writes screenplays. Also acts. As a boyfriend, where exactly is the flaw—”

“You little— you’re STILL going!” Cho-rong pinned her and went for the ribs, tickling with intent. “Say it again! Say it AGAIN!”

“HAHAHA— eonni— I was wrong—”

“And YOU! Jeong Eun-ji!”

“AH— I was wrong too—!”

The recording lounge dissolved into full riot: six girls in one laughing, shrieking tangle.

Which was, of course, the exact moment the door opened.

He walked in carrying a chart sheet. “About the harmony balance on the final—”

The sentence stopped dead.

The room was a tableau of girlhood at maximum entropy. Cho-rong, mid-laugh, pinning Na-eun — the top two buttons of her pale-blue chiffon blouse had surrendered at some point in the fray, and the blouse’s untucked hem had ridden up, baring an expanse of fair skin and a delicate line of collarbone, lace edging making a brief, unscheduled appearance. Na-eun, “suppressed” beneath her, was no better off: the white tee rucked up past her waist, a flat stomach and the low-slung line of denim shorts fully on the record, hair everywhere, cheeks flushed with laughing. Eun-ji had toppled backward across an armchair, wide collar swung far off-axis, one round shoulder and a shadowed line catching the light. Bo-mi and Ha-young were a single laughing knot with rumpled everything, Ha-young’s skirt hem several degrees north of regulation, and even Nam-joo — nominally the referee — had her collar askew.

The air held perfume, clean sweat, riotous youth, and an aggregate quantity of fair skin the visual cortex processed long before policy could intervene.

Time hiccuped.

His pupils flickered once; he wrenched his gaze off-axis with the reflex speed of a man who trains this specific muscle.

“Sorry!”

Fast retreat; the heavy soundproof door thumped shut on the whole scene.

Outside, back to the cool wall, he exhaled once and pressed two fingers to his brow. One glance — but the frame had burned in at broadcast resolution. Thin summer clothes, dazzling skin, and that entirely unposed, unfiltered aliveness — the image had zero intention of leaving on its own.

Knock. From now on: always, always knock.

Inside, the silence held for a full three-count.

“AAAH!!!”

Na-eun’s shriek broke the seal. She catapulted upright, hauled the tee down with both fists, dragged the denim waistband back to legal, face approaching arterial red. Cho-rong looked down at her own open collar and bare midriff and turned the color of a peony in one frame, spinning away to reassemble. Eun-ji righted herself and her neckline in a single motion, cheeks blazing. Bo-mi and Ha-young flew apart and audited hems. Nam-joo, first to regain government function — ears crimson — hissed:

“Quick! Everyone — FIX!”

A whirlwind of rustling. Buttons buttoned, wrinkles pressed flat by hand, hair hooked behind ears, heart rates negotiated downward. Dorm habits — they’d simply forgotten a guest existed on the premises today.

At last, dignity provisionally restored — the blush refusing full retreat, every glance ricocheting off every other glance — the leader drew several breaths and called through the door in her sweetest, mildest, most catastrophically embarrassed voice:

“Ji-ho-ssi. It — it’s fine now.”

“…Right.”

The door opened. He came in with his expression restored to factory settings.

“Just now — my apologies. I should have knocked first.” The bow of the head was sincere, and it drained half the embarrassment out of the room on contact.

“N-no! We’re the ones who— we get sloppy when it’s just us.”

“Let’s — let’s get back to work! The last harmony section, you said?”

He carried the retreat gratefully, was at the console in three strides, pulling up the session. “Right. The emotional layering on the final stack can go one level finer—”

The girls exhaled, collectively and quietly, and threw themselves at the work with twelve-tenths concentration — professionalism deployed as a scrubbing agent. The remaining hours ran clean and hard; a session this size wouldn’t close in a day, and at six he called it.

“Great work today. Every one of you delivered.”

He hung the monitor headphones on the stand and turned, gaze making the round and settling on Cho-rong — at, to be precise about it, eye height. She had very good eyes.

“This song is going to be something. I’m looking forward to your stage.”

“Gamsahamnida!”

Six voices, one bow.

✦   ✦   ✦

Down the corridor, early-summer evening air slipping through a window, he walked to the parking garage alone — and the frame from the lounge chose that moment to replay itself, dazzling white and inconveniently vivid. He shook his head and laughed at himself, once.

Supervising girl-group vocals, it turned out, required professional skill, a certain extra ration of composure — and an unbreakable knocking protocol.

He’d barely settled into the driver’s seat when the phone lit: Sulli. The name alone tugged the corner of his mouth upward. He answered.

“Hey, Sulli.”

“Oppa, are you done with work?” The voice arrived soft and glutinous, backed by a faint mew and something mellow on speakers.

“Mm, just walked out of the studio. What’s up? Our little princess isn’t busy today?”

Little princess. First deployment of the title. On her end, the smile went audibly sweeter. “Mmm~ not really. Oppa. Tonight — are you free?”

“I am. Missed me? Dinner?”

“I did miss you—” The admission came out with zero hedging and an audible blush, and then the pivot: “But it’s not only dinner. Ha-ra eonni’s heart is broken, so I’m at her place keeping her company. She bought soju and anju, and my job is to drink a TINY bit and talk. Except—”

A calculated pause, hope threaded through it.

“Two people drinking is a little bleak, you know? And eonni said so too, so she’s called a few friends. So — if Oppa’s finished, and doesn’t mind — you could come sit with us?”

Then, smaller, suddenly shy:

“Of course, if you’re tired and want to go home, that’s totally fine! I just — thought I’d ask.”
