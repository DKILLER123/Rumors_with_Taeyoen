Chapter Twelve

First Meeting: Taeyeon

Kim Mi-yeon had delivered the word yesterday: evaluation, today, 10 a.m.

So for once Ji-ho stole half a morning of mercy — no dawn alarm, just honest, glorious sleep, banking every ounce of rest so he could walk into the most important hour of his new life at full power.

Nine o’clock sharp, he stepped into the building, made for the practice room on autopilot, and worked methodically through his ritual: joints loosened, voice warmed, body stretched long and ready.

Provisional-to-official evaluations normally ran in batches — a matter of pure arithmetic. As the industry’s reigning giant, SM cast its recruiting net across the whole planet: the famous Saturday open auditions, global audition tours, and half a dozen other channels covering some twenty regions — the States, China, Thailand and beyond — pulling in over three hundred thousand applications a year. The Saturday auditions alone drew two or three hundred hopefuls a week; across all channels, five to six hundred entered first-round screening weekly. From that ocean, the second-round interviews kept only a trickle. The third and final round rarely saw ten faces. Those survivors got two weeks of training and a final assessment — and in the end, fewer than ten people a year signed as official trainees.

And then there were the exceptions — the rare talents granted a special early evaluation, one week ahead of everyone. Like Ji-ho, today. Like, in a future not yet written, a girl named Ning Yizhuo.

“Ji-ho!” A familiar voice from the doorway; a beckoning hand. “Come out here a moment.”

Mid-stretch, he snapped upright, straightened his clothes at speed, and jogged over. Behind him, the practice room erupted into whispers before the door even shut.

“Yah — did you hear? The American kid’s getting a special evaluation?”

“I heard too. They say his singing is insane. Haven’t you noticed he gets a private vocal lesson every single day? On top of everything?”

“So… think he passes?”

“Isn’t he kind of old, though? I’d say it’s fifty-fifty at best.”

“He passes. Trust me. He’s the last one out of the building every night. The man is not normal.”

“Wait, seriously? He’s THAT crazy about it?”

A round of low, impressed murmuring…

Out in the corridor, Kim Mi-yeon gave him her final pre-battle briefing.

“Don’t be nervous. Sing the way you always sing, and that’s enough.”

She’d spent the whole week drilling him personally, and she knew exactly what she was sending in: humble, freakishly retentive, improving at a rate that bordered on unfair — with a God-given instrument underneath it all. Barring disaster, he passed.

Nervous? Ji-ho had spent years singing for rooms full of drunk strangers in two languages; stage fright had died somewhere back in Los Angeles. But the gratitude was real — she had taught him like it mattered.

“Seonsaeng-nim, I’m not nervous at all. Honestly? I’m excited. Thank you for this week — for all of it. I mean that.”

“Thank me for what? If you pass, I get a bonus.” Her eyes crinkled; the joke came out deadpan. “If you fail, you’re paying me that bonus back yourself.”

A blink — then he laughed, bright and open.

“I don’t have that kind of money. Looks like passing is my only option.”

“Good. That’s exactly the attitude.” She clapped his shoulder, smiling. “Go on in.”

At the evaluation room he knocked, waited for the answer, and pushed the door open with unhurried calm. At center floor, spine straight, he bowed deep, voice ringing clear:

“Good morning, judges. My name is Ji-ho — Korean-American, from Los Angeles. I’m eighteen years old, and singing is my strongest skill.”

He rose, squared his shoulders — and his sweep of the judges’ table stalled for half a heartbeat. Eight evaluators today. And one of them had no business being here at all.

Rounded, almond-shaped eyes, clear and quick and deep enough to hide a night sky in. An oval face with a soft heart-shaped cast; full cheeks lending it something naturally, disarmingly young. Features fine as porcelain-work. Clean lines from shoulder to neck; long, deep-brown hair falling straight and soft. A slender frame in an ivory knit cardigan over a black pleated A-line skirt — gentle and crisp at once, coolness and sweetness folded improbably into a single presence.

Taeyeon. The reigning top-tier artist of the entire industry — sitting at a trainee evaluation.

What is SHE doing here?

Across the table, Taeyeon was studying him right back, openly curious. So this is the kid. The one whose singing supposedly beats mine — from when I first joined the company, anyway. Can’t judge the voice yet… but the face is textbook SM. Handsome. Annoyingly so.

“Ji-ho-ssi. Ryu Seok-min — recruitment management.” A man in his mid-thirties, black-framed glasses, comfortably rounding at the middle. “I’ve read your file. Thirteen failed auditions. What kept you going through all of that?”

“Hello, Ryu seonsaeng-nim.” Ji-ho’s gaze settled respectfully on the questioner. “I started singing in bars back in the States at sixteen — underage, technically, but nobody checked the kid behind the microphone. Somewhere in those rooms, I fell in love with it for real.”

“And I stopped being able to accept that a little bar stage was the ceiling. I want the stadium. Tens of thousands of people, all of them singing back. I crossed an ocean for that — and until it happens, quitting simply isn’t on the list.”

“And if you fail here at SM today? You’d stay on the Peninsula? Apply elsewhere?” Ryu Seok-min pressed.

“Of course!” No hesitation. Steel in the eyes, no theatrics needed.

A slow nod — and in the column marked career commitment, Ryu Seok-min wrote an 8.

Next, a briskly capable woman whose expression had clearly never met a joke it liked, eyes like search lamps.

“Yoon Su-jin, artist management. Ji-ho-ssi — suppose you discover misconduct close to you. Say a good friend is secretly meeting with other companies in breach of contract, and hiding it. What do you do?”

The question was a blade laid flat against the throat, and everyone in the room knew it. Cover for the friend — you’re damaging the company. Report the friend — you’re the person who sells out friends, and who trusts that person ever again? Every road out of the question seemed to run through a swamp.
