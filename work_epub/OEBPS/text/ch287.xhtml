Chapter Two Hundred Eighty-Seven

A Job That Lays Out Every Condition for Love

Thursday · 07:11 → 00:41 the east wing of a house on a hill · a kitchen with nobody in it · a music room at the LOEN building · and, much later, a floor one working day from the wage side of the desk: warm milk, a camel tie, five students, and every condition for falling in love already laid out by the job description

When Chae Soo-bin woke, the room was already light.

A single thin band of it had found the gap in the curtains and laid itself across the foot of the bed, straight and deliberate, like a boundary drawn by somebody patient.

She looked at the line of light for a few seconds. It was a little too bright. And then, the way a tide comes back in over dry sand, the room told her where she was.

Song Ji-ho’s house.

Song Ji-ho’s east room.

The night returned to her in pieces, each one landing more heavily than the last. The lock at twenty past three. The running order going out from under the sheet on her phone. A chairman in a towel with an empty glass, saying there were guest rooms in a voice that suggested he had already stopped discussing it, and her own head nodding before any of the responsible parts of her had been consulted.

Then a corridor, a door she had never opened in a year of opening every other door in this house for a living, and the light going out at four.

On the bench at the foot of the bed, where last night there had been nothing, there was now a small, exact arrangement: towels, folded; a toothbrush, still boxed; pajamas in silk, creaseless, sized correctly. Somebody had come in very quietly and left a hotel.

A house that keeps these ready at all times. Honestly. A playboy of the first order.

She sat up. The silk slid off one shoulder and she pulled it back without looking at it, because there is a limit to what a person can audit before seven in the morning.

Her phone said 07:11. The Friday running order had gone out of this room at six fifty, from under a sheet she had not slept under, and the last line of it, the small-font line the Chairman had never asked for and was never going to be told about, sat there now in the sent log, saying its one sentence about warm milk and ten thirty at night.

He gets up at eight, these days. There’s time.

She was wrong about that, though she would not know it for another two hours, and being wrong about it was the nicest mistake she made all day.

The bathroom was the size of a small apartment, and the shower was the separate, glassed, permanently-clean kind she had spent her whole adult life telling herself she did not specifically want. She undressed without ceremony and stood under the water and let it do what hot water does to a person who has slept four hours in a stranger’s silk.

Afterwards, in the mirror: color high, skin fine-grained and even, not one flaw anywhere, and eyes that were entirely, suspiciously bright.

Ani. Ani. What are you even thinking?

She rapped her knuckle once against her own skull, put on her own clothes from yesterday, and went out quietly, down the stairs, into the first floor of somebody else’s life.

The kitchen was empty. The living room was empty. The whole first floor was holding its breath.

Not one member of staff. Hmph. Who told you to have this many women: you can’t afford a witness in the kitchen before eight.

The day people came at eight; she knew that much from the roster she had never been shown and had reconstructed anyway, because knowing things nobody tells her is half of her salary. The house between seven and eight belonged to nobody, and for the moment, it belonged to her.

The refrigerator had eggs, bread, milk, and one small box of strawberries, which is the refrigerator of a man who eats like a rumor and is fed like a schedule.

She rolled her sleeves and began.

Eggs fried. Toast toasted. Strawberries rinsed, tipped, halved, and arranged on a saucer as though the saucer were going to be photographed. Milk warmed, not boiled; warmed, because a woman who has written a memo about warm milk at ten thirty is not going to pour the man iced anything at half past seven.

She worked slowly, and carefully, and quietly, the way a person moves when the task is not actually important and has decided, inconveniently, to feel like it is.

Two places on the table. His facing the window. Hers where she could see the door.

Eight o’clock came. Eight o’clock went. At twenty past eight she stood in the middle of his kitchen with her hands on her hips and had a short, silent negotiation with the eggs, and the eggs lost, because there are limits to what can be asked of a fried egg held at eighty degrees for an hour, and at nine she did the honest thing and started again.

Nineteen hours awake and a whole video locked in a day. The man has earned a morning. The morning has eggs. Both of these facts can be true at once, Chae Soo-bin, and neither of them is about you.

At twenty to ten there were footsteps on the stairs.

He came down in house clothes with his hair in open rebellion and his eyes at two-thirds, and when he saw her standing there with a pan in her hand he smiled before he was properly awake, which is how she learned that the smile does not consult him first.

“Morning, Soo-bin-ah.”

It really was an unfair smile. And the eyes when they curved: she had seen that exact curve before, on a face that lived two girl-groups away, and she caught herself doing the arithmetic in real time.

Smiling eyes like Tiffany-ssi’s. Is that a couple thing? Do couples grow into each other’s faces? Aigoo, and if that’s how it works, I do not want to know what this face plans to look like by winter.

She pushed the milk down the table to his side. “Good morning. Breakfast is ready. It’s the second breakfast; the first one lost its nerve at eight twenty.”

“Thank you.” He pulled the chair out. “Sit down and eat with me.”

“Would you pass me the jam?”

She handed it over without thinking, smoothed her skirt under her, and sat, carefully, the way one sits in a chair that has just become a dining table for two.

The breakfast was quiet.

Not the awkward quiet of two people negotiating a room, and not the busy quiet of a phone between the plates. It was the other kind. The domestic kind. The kind that assumes a hundred prior breakfasts and schedules a hundred more, and Chae Soo-bin sat inside it and understood, with the clarity of a person standing on train tracks, exactly how much trouble she was in.

He ate fast, the way he does everything. Last toast, glass up, and the line of his throat moved once, and she relocated her attention to the strawberry situation with the speed of a professional.

“I’ll go and change.”

He went up. She gathered the plates, stood at the sink with the water running, and the thought arrived at her elbow uninvited, the way the best and worst ones do.

What color shirt today? Light champagne, probably. He’s been living in that shirt all month.

She dried her hands, walked back into the living room with no job to do there, and the stairs delivered him on cue.

Light champagne. Sleeves rolled to the elbow. Collar open two buttons, and in one hand, uncinched, a tie in camel with a fine stripe in it.

She never did find the moment at which she decided to cross the room.

He watched her come, and said nothing, and when she stopped in front of him he tipped his head down a half-inch, which is not a gesture a chairman makes; it is a gesture a man makes, in a hallway, to a woman who is about to fix something at his throat.

Gentle, expensive, clean. The palette of a man who has decided to be approachable before noon, worn over the slept-in hair of a man who was editing film at three in the morning. The two facts do not fight each other; they tell you what kind of day he intends to have, and how little of it he intends to spend proving anything.
One knot, tied by an employee, at a height of twenty-two centimeters from her own collarbone, in under forty seconds, without either party saying a word about it. The house noticed. The house keeps everything to itself.

Song Ji-ho · the kitchen at nine fifty-eight, second breakfast

“Let me.”

She took the tie out of his hand and laid it around the back of his neck, and her fingers came to his collar, and then past the collar, and the skin at the side of his neck was warm, and her hands were not, and the difference between those two temperatures was the entire remaining content of her mind.

Too close. Close enough to count his lashes. Close enough to hear him breathe, which he was doing calmly, at the pace of a man being serviced by weather.

Sharp. Steady. Meticulous. First-rate. Reliable. Warm-mannered. Humble, somehow, on top of everything. Confident. Well-raised to a degree you can actually see.

And, which is not nothing: twenty, and handsome. I am a visual creature. The whole species is. I contain the whole species.

And there, at a range of twenty centimeters, with her knuckles resting lightly against a champagne collar, the honesty arrived, the kind she would never say out loud in a hundred years of employment: if this man were hers — if he were hers — he could lose the company, the building, the accounts, all of it, tomorrow, and she would not move.

He could even, and here her hands went on tying while something in her went on being appalled, stray — once — and if he came back corrected, honestly corrected, she would find somewhere in herself to forgive it.

A pity, then —

She sighed, finished the knot, slid it home, set the collar straight over it, and looked up.

He was looking at her.

It was a very still look. Still, and deep enough that she felt the floor of the conversation move.

Her heart dropped a beat, and she cleared her throat like a woman being lowered by crane out of a building.

“Ahem. Aha. There. Done.”

He said nothing. He went on looking at her for two seconds, or three, or a length of time not measured in seconds at all; and then his chin dropped, and he considered the knot at his throat the way he considered a finished cut.

“Good hands. Been practicing on somebody?”

Her mouth pressed itself flat. “I’ll get your shoes.”

The black calf sat on the bottom shelf of the cabinet in the hall, and she crouched, and brought them out, and set them at his feet, toes out.

He stepped in; she watched him do it; she watched him settle the cuffs over the instep, and the hallway did something to the light, and all at once she was eight years old again in a doorway she had not thought about in fifteen years.

Her mother used to stand exactly here — not here, there, in the doorway of a rented flat with a linoleum floor — every morning of her childhood, watching her father into his shoes. Flicking the dust off his shoulder when there was dust, and sometimes when there wasn’t. Righting a tie that did not need righting.

And little Chae Soo-bin would sit with her rice bowl and watch the two of them do this small, stupid, ceremonial thing, and be happy in a way she had no vocabulary for, because her parents loved each other, and it showed in the hallway before it showed anywhere else.

She was standing in a rich man’s hallway now, doing the same small things, item for item, gesture for gesture.

Only she was not his wife.

She was his secretary.

A secretary does a partner’s work all day long: the tea remembered, the temper accounted for, the tie, the shoes, the door. The only thing the job never issues is the name.

At the door he went first and she followed at half a step’s remove, which was not a rule anyone had written down and was nonetheless the law.

The car was waiting at the bottom of the drive. The driver opened the door; Song Ji-ho got in; Chae Soo-bin walked around to the other side and folded herself in beside him, and the whole distance between them in the back seat was twenty centimeters, which she could not stop knowing, because knowing distances is also half of her salary.

She sat like a woman at an interview: spine off the seatback, knees together, hands stacked on her bag. Which, she supposed, was not even wrong.

The city ran backwards past the glass. The sun came in low and side-on and laid itself along his face, and he read his phone with a small crease at the brow, the second title film’s notes probably; the man has two of them now, like a duelist — and Chae Soo-bin looked at the profile for a long time, and then made herself look at the window instead, and the window was no use at all, because her own reflection was standing in it, looking back, at twenty centimeters’ remove.

She knew it was wrong. She wanted that on the record, somewhere, even if the only court in session was the inside of a car. She knew about the other women. She knew what a heart does at this range, the way a person who works in a bank knows what money does to hands. And none of it changed the terms, because the terms were these:

Seven million won a month. Enough to live on generously, and to save like a woman with a plan, which she had, and which had a number on it and everything.

Every morning at the office she made his tea, and she made it right, because she had put the whole of his preference on a card in her phone the size of a hymn. Warm water on the tired days. The schedule in her head before it was in his. The temper, the pressure, the two a.m. silences; the way he went grey around the eyes in a conference room and came back to color in a corridor.

Him fearsome across a table. Him in a chair with his eyes shut. Him blazing in front of a lens. Him at home, soft-collared and sleep-tousled, a man with nowhere to be and no witnesses to be it in front of.

His women saw the parts. The parts were magnificent. But the whole of the man, the whole ledger, debit and credit, hallway and conference room, only one person in the world saw that, and she was sitting twenty centimeters from it, on salary.

And a person, her mother had said once, in the doorway of a rented flat, about a different man entirely and for different reasons, always ends up loving whatever it is they keep alive.

I know the illusion. I know it by name. “I’m special. I understand him best.” Every secretary who ever fell for her boss wrote that sentence first. Knowing the trick does not get you off the stage. It only means you can see the wires while you fly.

Too close. The border between the job and the rest of it had been worn out of the fabric so long ago that there was no longer a seam to point to. That is the entire problem with this profession, she thought. It lays out every condition for falling in love — the proximity, the intimacy, the being-needed at hours nobody else is awake — and then it calls itself employment and pays you on the twenty-fifth.

The car set them down at the lobby, and the lobby did what the lobby did.

They came through the doors as a pair, him first, her half a step behind, and the early shift stopped what it was doing to the last badge. Bows. Good mornings. Eyes that crossed Chae Soo-bin on the way to the chairman and kept going, quickly, the way eyes cross a piece of furniture that might be evidence.

She knew the arithmetic being done behind every fourth pair of them, and she had known it since her first day standing beside him, when the story had gone through this building faster than the fire alarm ever had. Pretty. Young. An actress who never got a role worth the name. Therefore, obviously and structurally, the chairman’s private arrangement. The company had decided it the way companies decide things: without malice, without evidence, and forever.

She cared exactly none, or told the number to herself as none twice a week, and followed him into the elevator, and pressed the floor.

The doors were closing when she saw it: a fleck of dust, small, gray, insolent, riding the shoulder of a four-hundred-million-won suit.

Her hand was already moving. She brushed it off, once, lightly, two fingers, a gesture thirty years older than she was, before any part of her had agreed to anything.

He turned his head and looked at her.

She took the hand back and studied the floor numbers with the focus of a woman auditing them for irregularities.

“—Dust.”

“Thank you.”

The doors opened. He walked out. She followed at half a step’s remove, down a corridor that rose to meet them: “Hoejang-nim, good morning.” “Hoejang-nim, good morning.” “Chae Biseo-nim, good morning.” Bow after bow after bow, all the way to the corner, a tide of respect that a person could drink all day and still be thirsty tomorrow.

…I should not enjoy that this much. Note it in the file. The file is getting long.

Bae Joo-hyun, Kang Seul-gi, Son Seung-wan, Park Soo-young, Kim Ye-rim. The five of them sat the way trainees sit when the door is about to open: spines straight, hands folded, charged.

He decided on severity, the way he decided everything: immediately, and in advance, and with every intention of being talked out of it later by somebody with better judgment than himself.

Vocal lesson · the music room, LOEN building · Thursday, four o’clock · attendance: five

Vowel migration one voice, top to bottom; no seams where the registers change

1Migrate the vowels (a, e, i, o, u) up and down until the color does not break at the pass. Unified timbre, no cliffs.

2Hum to find the mask. The hum is a searchlight, not an exercise: follow it into the head until the high notes arrive already lit, and cheap ones cost nothing at the throat.

3Climb the loudness ladder (small voice, middle voice, full voice) and never skip a rung. The voice that jumps the ladder in practice falls off it in performance.

4Breathe from the floor of the body. The belly launches; the throat only aims. A note pushed from the throat is a note on credit.

5Long tones and staccato, daily, in that order: the long tone teaches the breath to stand at attention; the staccato teaches the cords to snap and release.

6Condition the instrument’s housing. Lungs, posture, legs, stamina: a singer is an athlete whose stadium is inside her.

7Scales and vibrato, every day, without heroics. Flexibility is rented, not owned, and the rent is due in full each morning.

Source: the Encyclopedia of the Perfect Singer, which does more than hold exercises; it reads a voice the way an accountant reads a ledger, and prescribes for the range, the habits, and the specific weaknesses of the individual. The book is not stocked in any shop in the country. The five students are not to worry about where the teacher got it.
