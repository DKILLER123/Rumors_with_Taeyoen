Continuing IU’s Villa · Living Room two girls, one ceiling, zero knives

“Get a knife?” Ji-eun startled. “Isn’t that a little extreme?”

“Calm?”

Ji-eun thought about the first time she’d caught him. The perfume — Kim Tae-yeon’s — on his collar. The pillow she’d beaten him with until the stuffing came out.

And now?

Knowing full well the scope of his collection — and still here. Still his.

The Bad Apple Effect

People are strange about this. If a man has been a known good person for years, and one day does a small bad thing — the image collapses. Ah, so he was always like this underneath. The mask slips. The interpretation inverts retroactively.

But if a known bad person does one good thing — the interpretation softens. Ah, he’s not so bad after all. The prodigal returns. The audience is generous to converts.

The brain weights danger, betrayal, and negativity more heavily than the positive. A hundred good deeds cannot offset one that lands wrong. People expect “good” to hold — and the moment it breaks, they read the entire record as performance.

A bad man does good: “He’s turning around.” A good man does bad: “True colors at last.”

Which is why, this morning, Lee Ji-eun and Choi Jin-ri did not reach for the knife.

If Song Ji-ho had hidden it — had maintained the fiction of monogamy, had been caught in this bed by surprise — they would have taken him apart. But Song Ji-ho was a declared philanderer. I like you. I also like others. I will not lie about it. And so, this morning, the discovery landed not as a betrayal but as an increment.

Oh. So Ha-ra-unnie joined. All right.

Jin-ri studied Ji-eun’s face for a while, and a faint disappointment showed.

Ji-eun’s fight is a little weak, isn’t it? She can take this?

Has oppa already trained her that thoroughly?

She couldn’t even bristle?

“Why are you looking at me like that?” Ji-eun shifted under the gaze.

“Nothing.” Jin-ri retracted, picked up her water, sipped. “Just thinking… you’ve changed a lot.”

Ji-eun rubbed her nose.

She knew. The old Ji-eun — the one who detonated over small infractions — was already gone. Not that she never felt anger. But she’d learned when to deploy it and when to bank it.

When I fought Kim Tae-yeon, he warned me. He doesn’t like troublesome women. If I blow up now, and genuinely anger him — then what?

Anger is a private currency. It’s spent on him when we’re alone, to make him coax me. It is not spent in front of an audience to humiliate him — that’s the stupidest woman’s move in the handbook.

But the banking… the banking made it heavier. She could feel it sitting in her chest, accruing.

She glanced at the bathroom. Park Ji-yeon was in there — water running, the sound of a toothbrush at work.

Of the four best friends: Peach fell first. Then me. Now Ha-ra-unnie.

Next — Ji-yeon?

Can I just watch them all fall, one by one?

She looked at Jin-ri. Jin-ri was already looking at her. Eyes met, bounced apart.

“So…” Ji-eun started.

“Mm?” Jin-ri blinked.

“You’re really just going to watch?”

“Watch what?” Head tilted, innocent.

“Playing dumb.” Ji-eun dropped her voice. “Ha-ra-unnie is in his arms right now.”

Jin-ri smiled. Leaned closer. Also dropped her voice.

“And what would you like to do about it?”

Ji-eun deflated.

What would I like? I’d like him to eat some consequence. I’d just rather someone else served it.

Jin-ri read her like a billboard.

“Honestly? I think oppa’s being a bit much.”

“Right?!” Ji-eun lit up.

“But what can we do?” A sigh, well-placed. “He knows exactly what we’re thinking. If we make noise, he coaxes. We go soft. Next time, same thing.”

Ji-eun fell silent.

True. In this arrangement, facing Song Ji-ho, they were all the weaker party.

He loses Lee Ji-eun — and he still has Kim Tae-yeon, Tiffany, Choi Jin-ri, Goo Ha-ra, Jung Soo-yeon, Park Ji-yeon.

She loses him — and has what, exactly?

“So,” Jin-ri said, eyes carrying the unmistakable freight of someone about to volunteer someone else. “I think someone should stand up. Show him we’re not pushovers.”

“Yes! Yes! Exactly!” Ji-eun nodded hard.

“You do it.”

Ji-eun froze.

“Me?” She pointed at her own nose. “Why me?”

“Because you found out first. Obviously.” Jin-ri, as if explaining arithmetic. “And you’re Lee Ji-eun — LOEN’s cash cow. Oppa has to handle you carefully.”

“Peach-ah, my new album budget hasn’t been approved yet!”

“You and oppa went through that ocean survival thing together! That kind of bond — he won’t actually be angry. Go up, give him a piece of your mind. Let him know we have tempers too.”

“You go!”

“I’m just oppa’s little sister. I wouldn’t know about these things.”

“Sister? WHAT sister! Is there a sister who sleeps in his bed?”

“In bed, I’m the girlfriend. But right now — I’m not in bed, am I?”

“I still think you’re the right one. Ji-eun-ah, you’re good at this — you’re the one who fights.”

Stalemate. Two pairs of eyes, locked.

Then, simultaneously: both looked away.

The toilet flushed. The bathroom door opened.

Park Ji-yeon emerged, rubbing her eyes, saw the two of them huddled together.

“What are you two whispering about?”

“Nothing.” In unison.

Ji-yeon looked from face to face with suspicion, then dropped onto the sofa next to Ji-eun and leaned her head on Ji-eun’s shoulder.

“Where’s Ha-ra-unnie?”

Ji-eun and Jin-ri exchanged one compressed look. What do we do?

Three of the four have already fallen. Ji-yeon absolutely cannot be next. Send her up to wake them — that way Ji-yeon will definitely not fall for Song Ji-ho.

No! Oppa’s that good — if Ji-yeon sees all three of us already with him, it’ll be psychological priming. Absolutely not!

Then what?

Get Ji-yeon out of here first.

Think about it — Ha-ra-unnie was the one who always opposed me and oppa. Now she’s become his woman too. Hah. I’d love to see how she faces me now.

…Peach, you’re mean.

“What are you two doing!” Park Ji-yeon planted herself between them. “I asked you a question and you’re making eyes at each other — tell me! What are you hiding?”

“Ji-yeon-ah.” Ji-eun took her hand. “Ha-ra-unnie had an early schedule, she left at dawn. Do you have one today?”

“I do. The company organized a charity bazaar — we all have to be there.”

“Charity bazaar? Then you’d better go get ready.” Ji-eun pulled her up. “Come on. Morning schedule for me too.”

“Let’s go together.”

Later That Morning The Master Bedroom the aftermath

Goo Ha-ra stretched on the bed, catlike and luxurious.

The movement was too generous — the duvet slid away, exposing a wide stretch of pale skin. At her collarbone, a mark like a strawberry sat, vivid against the white. Below it: a waist so narrow it bordered on the architectural — twenty-one and a half inches, nothing surplus, the line of it clean as a drawn bow. Her face, small as a palm, was framed by dark straight hair fanned across the pillow. Legs, long and proportioned, tucked slightly at the knee. The whole of her arranged on white sheets like a doll someone had left out.

She was warm. More thoroughly warm than she’d been in a long time.

Her hand, drifting across the sheet, touched something cold and damp.

Goo Ha-ra’s eyes snapped open.

She looked down at herself. The whole of last night came back in a single, high-pressure flood.

She and Song Ji-ho—

How did I fall for that line of his? Aish.

What now? Are Peach and Ji-eun awake?

AISH. I fell asleep! Of all the— This is all Song Ji-ho’s fault!

Is he a wild bull? Does he take something? I thought I was going to fall apart.

The bathroom water stopped. The door opened.

Song Ji-ho stepped out, towel in hand, naked.

The physique hit her full force — this was not something she’d been in any state to catalog at the time, and in the morning light it was significantly more vivid. Shoulders wide, waist narrow, abdominal muscles individually addressed, the V-line descending past the border of the towel, every inch carrying the specific, unhurried authority of someone who knew exactly what he looked like.

Goo Ha-ra stared for several full seconds before her brain caught up with her eyes.

Then her hands clamped over her face and she shrieked.

“AH!!!”

“Why aren’t you wearing clothes?!”

He looked at her — at her own, equally unclothed arrangement — and his tone was level.

“Neither are you.”

!!

The realization landed. She dove into the duvet and wrapped herself into a tight ball.

“AAAAHHH!”

“Don’t look!”

He found his own clothes in Ji-eun’s closet — where he’d left them — and dressed, unhurried.

“Done. Get up, take a shower. I’m taking you home.”

Home. Grandmother.

She surfaced, just her eyes above the duvet line. He was already dressed, fixing his cuffs.

“Forget it.” Her expression was layered, complicated. “Let’s just pretend this didn’t happen.”

His hands paused. He looked at her. The corner of his mouth moved.

“Oh? Enjoyed it and now you won’t pay the bill?”

She went scarlet from collarbone to hairline.

“WHO— who ENJOYED—!!”

“Did you not? Because I recall this morning you—”

“STOP TALKING!”

She clapped both hands over her ears. She was red from the roots of her hair to somewhere past the duvet’s edge.

The face. The body. The stamina. …Have I been wasting my life?

He crossed to the bed, bent, and gently pulled her hands away from her ears. He looked at her with an expression that was no longer playful.

“Goo Ha-ra. Listen. This isn’t your call anymore. It’s mine.”

“I’m not letting you go. So you can’t go.”

“Half an hour. I’ll be outside.”

His fingers, releasing hers, grazed the sensitive skin at the side of her neck.

“Mm—”

A small shiver went through her whole body. Goosebumps rose in a fine wave from her collar to her ears, which went red past the lobes.

He straightened and walked out. The door closed.

She sat in the duvet, looking at that door, her heart still hammering.

Then · What Made Her

She was nine when her mother left. Walked out of the house in Gwangju and kept walking — no contact, no money, no note, for over twenty years.

The divorce was formalized in 2006. Mother relinquished custody. After that: silence.

Father worked construction sites, far from home, rarely seen. Calls came, occasionally, but presence — in the daily, ongoing, there-when-you-wake-up sense — never.

Her brother, Goo Ho-in, two years older, was doing his military service.

It was her grandmother who raised them both. fed them, kept them in school, held the household together on the kind of income that doesn’t leave margins.

So: grandmother was the center. The whole weight of the word family lived in one person.

And the scar from nine years old ran so deep she’d structured her entire emotional life around the fear of being left. In every relationship, she was the one who gave more, clung harder, braced for the exit — because the first person who was supposed to stay had walked out the door and never come back.

And Song Ji-ho had just said: I’m not letting you go. So you can’t go.

Domineering. That was the word for it.

But the strange thing was — she didn’t feel offended. She felt safe.

As if someone had taken the decision out of her hands. As if she no longer had to be the one bracing for the door.

She sat with the duvet for a while. Then she got up, went to the bathroom, and stood under the hot water, still turning the sentence over.

Clean, she opened the closet. Her own clothes were… somewhere she could not locate. Ji-eun’s, then. She pulled on a plain white T-shirt and jeans.

In the mirror: hair damp, flush not fully gone, the strawberry mark at her collarbone still vivid. She tried concealer. It didn’t take.

…Whatever.

She breathed. Walked to the door. Her hand closed on the handle and stopped.

Peach and Ji-eun are up, aren’t they? What will they think of me? Will they—

She couldn’t finish the thought.

She checked the time. Exactly half an hour.

He said half an hour. He decides. So: half an hour.

She turned the handle and went out.

One person in the living room. Song Ji-ho on the sofa, phone in hand. He looked up when he heard her.

They knew, and they left. Quietly. Without a scene.

The realization should have made it worse. Instead, Goo Ha-ra felt something inside her go very still.

She looked at him.

“If the women around you can take a scene like this and walk away from it without a word — do you actually believe they love you?”

He set his phone down.

“Love?” A small, dry sound. “Human genetics put a three-year shelf life on romantic passion, at maximum.”

“What people actually pursue, for the whole of their lives, is a better life and better enjoyment. They just dress the instinct up in nicer vocabulary and call it love.”

“Whether you want someone handsome, someone with a good body, someone capable, someone with money, someone with power, or whether you’re lonely and need company, or have physical needs — at the root, every one of those is the same instinct: a better life.”

He stood and moved toward her.

“I don’t know if they love me. I know that I can give them the lives they want. And they can’t bring themselves to leave. That’s enough.”

System · Passive Skill — In Effect

Silver Tongue

The Player’s tone softens automatically; the gaze turns warm and radiates sincerity. Even lies are instinctively believed by lovers. Rapidly soothes a lover’s negative emotions — wounded feelings, anger, jealousy, insecurity.

Every word landed on the softest part of her — the part that was nine years old and standing in a doorway, watching someone leave.

He’s right. I debuted, I went on variety shows, I earned money, I kept going — for what? For a better life. For grandmother to stop worrying.

And this person — standing here — can give me that. All of it.

And I’m already—

She was traditional, in the particular, unspoken way of someone whose grandmother had raised her. The body had already decided. The body, in her private moral grammar, carried weight. She was already his — and that fact, once installed, changed the rest of the arithmetic.

Maybe… trying wouldn’t be so bad.

Slowly, her arms went around his waist. She didn’t say anything. She pressed her face into his chest.

“Come on,” he said. “Let’s go see your grandmother.”

That Morning En Route · Seoul → Gwangju one driver, one chairman, one girl who didn’t expect any of this

Ryu Dong-hwan had the gifts boxed and stacked in the trunk, ordered by size, secured against corners.

He stood by the car and watched Song Ji-ho come out, arm around Goo Ha-ra, who leaned into him, docile as a cat, face still carrying the complicated pink of someone who had not yet fully processed the morning.

…Chairman.

If he walked in tomorrow with Lee Boo-jin on his arm, I would not blink.

They got in. The car pulled out.

Gwangju.

Goo Ha-ra leaned against his shoulder and watched the landscape reel past the glass. Her interior was complicated.

Her childhood had been unlucky — that much was true. But “unlucky” was the sanitized version. The accurate version: her mother left when she was nine. Not “left” as in moved out — left as in vanished, cut contact, contributed nothing for over twenty years. Her father worked construction sites in other cities. Her brother was in the army.

Her grandmother had raised them. Every meal, every school uniform, every scraped knee, every morning she didn’t want to get up — her grandmother was the one who stood there.

So: that person was the axis. The person she couldn’t disappoint.

And Song Ji-ho had said he’d go. To Gwangju. To the door. To meet the axis.

Something in her, somewhere below the ribs, went soft.

“Sleep if you want,” he said. “I’ll wake you when we arrive.”

“Mm.”

She closed her eyes. The highway ran smooth under the wheels, and she was asleep within minutes — a deep, untroubled sleep, the kind that comes from no longer being the only person responsible for holding yourself together.

Song Ji-ho, with Goo Ha-ra against his shoulder, was also running his own interior arithmetic.

Choi Jin-ri and Goo Ha-ra. The same childhood wound — parental absence, the wrong people, the long stretch of years where nobody caught them. He’d already rewritten Jin-ri’s trajectory in this life. And now, through a piece of drunken theater he hadn’t scripted, Goo Ha-ra’s had entered his orbit.

Love is premature. But as a man — in this second life — her road is mine to see to. That much is settled.

The car wound through Gwangju’s old district, turning, turning, and drew up in front of a weathered residential building. The kind of concrete that has been rained on for thirty years and has opinions about it.

Goo Ha-ra opened her eyes. The familiar building. The tightness arrived in her chest.

“This is it?” He asked.

“Mm.” She nodded, lip between her teeth. “Grandmother’s on the third floor—”

“Let’s go.”
