Chapter One Hundred

My Taeyeon

Back at the hotel, Ji-ho turned the day after tomorrow over and over in his head.

Can’t be discovered. Can’t be boring.

The brief constrained him — and then it shaped him. The answer surfaced clean: camping. Away from every crowd, under an entire sky of stars, a world with a population of two.

He prepared like it was a comeback stage: a mid-mountain campsite booked for its famous privacy — expensive, and worth it; tent and bedding checked; fresh ingredients bought; and one new ukulele.

✦   ✦   ✦

Dawn, barely. The streetlights still wore halos of mist.

His car ghosted to the mouth of the lane. The window slid down on a masked face and smiling eyes.

Inside two minutes, a small figure came quick-stepping out of the complex’s side gate — ball cap and mask hiding nearly everything except two enormous, glittering, thoroughly overexcited eyes. She pulled the door open and slid in, bringing a gust of cool morning air and the sweet warmth of her underneath it.

“Go go go,” she urged in a stage whisper, yanking the mask down to breathe. “I think oppa’s light came on when I was sneaking out. Nearly died.”

“The guilty conscience of a thief.” He pulled away, grinning.

“YAH! And whose fault is that?” She glared and pinched his arm. “If someone photographs me climbing into a man’s car at dawn, even an IDIOT could figure out whose car. And then we’re both unexplainable. Seonsaeng-nim murders me, and the fans murder YOU.”

“Didn’t you invite me to Jeonju?”

“Excuse me?” The mask came all the way off so the glare could operate at full power.

“I said—” instant, shameless retreat, “—the most dangerous moment is always the safest moment. Also, nobody knows my car. ALSO, did I not pick the single safest hour of the day?” One hand on the wheel, the other crossing over to fold around hers, entirely at home. “Sleep a bit if you’re tired. It’s a while out of the city.”

She WAS tired — awake since four, thirty minutes at the closet, final verdict: the grayest hoodie and jeans she owned. But excitement was currently beating sleep by knockout. She shook her head and scratched a little tease into his palm.

“Not tired. Where ARE we going? You’ve been all mysterious.”

“Didn’t we agree it’s my show?” He checked the mirror, and let one card show. “Let’s just say… somewhere you get to not be ‘Girls’ Generation’s Taeyeon’ for a day.”

She blinked — and warmth flooded in behind it. She stopped asking. Instead she turned to exploring the cabin like a cat in new territory, running a finger along the console, opening and closing the storage bin.

“Didn’t get a proper look the other night. So THIS is the new car? It’s more… ordinary than I imagined.”

“With what money?” he said. “Unless nuna wants to keep me. I hear that’s called soft rice where I’m from.”

She knew the phrase by now. She fished out her phone and thrust it at him.

“Passcode 890309. Transfer it yourself.”

All talk of soft rice aside, actually spending Taeyeon’s money was never going to happen. He ignored the phone and pressed a button instead; the window cracked open a sliver of cool dawn air.

“Music?”

He thumbed a track. The intro dropped and her whole face lit.

“That’s OUR song!”

“Mm. ‘Mr. Taxi.’” A grin. “Correct energy for the road.”

He accelerated onto the empty boulevard. She started humming along, tiny at first, then unfurling into full voice — and at the hook he came in underneath her, low and easy, the two lines locking together with suspicious precision.

The track ended. Two seconds of silence.

She turned, eyes glittering dangerously.

“Not bad AT ALL. Have you been secretly practicing?”

“Practice? Please.” Maximum nonchalance. “Heard it enough times, that’s all. SOMEONE is fairly self-obsessed, and a boyfriend should know his girlfriend’s discography.”

“Self-obsessed? WHERE am I self-obsessed?”

“…You made me fall for you automatically?”

She barked out a laugh with zero idol polish whatsoever — full ajumma — and finally, completely relaxed, slid down into the seat and watched the world reel past: the city thinning, fields and hills rising, the mist burning slowly off as the sun climbed.

It was beautiful.

✦   ✦   ✦

By the navigation, they should have arrived an hour ago.

Instead: a three-way fork, the car idling, and Ji-ho frowning between his phone and a paper map.

“So…” she said, biting down hard on a smile, “we’re lost, aren’t we?”

“Not lost. We elected the more scenic route.”

“Ohh—” she said, vowel stretched to maximum insolence, leaning over his phone, “then a question for our tour guide: why does the more scenic route END?”

He choked. He looked over; she was radiant with schadenfreude, eyes crescented.

“Fine. I confess. Found this campsite in an online guide — it said this way was prettier, only slightly longer. I must’ve taken a wrong fork.”

“A campsite even a Jeonju native has never heard of.” She confiscated the paper map and studied it like a document under audit. “Must be new. Let me see.”

A few minutes later her finger landed on a hairline of a road.

“This one. See the little green tree symbols? That birch stand we passed looked exactly like this.”

“You’re sure?”

“Not remotely. Never been here either.” She shrugged, breezy. “But sitting at this fork won’t find the road. Might as well try? Besides—” she looked out at the hills rolling away in green, “—the scenery IS good.”

So they took the narrow dirt track, the car swaying gently over the ruts. She put her window down and reached out to catch leaves off the wind.

“When I was little,” she said suddenly, “Dad used to take me and oppa and Ha-yeon on trips. No nice car — just an old sedan, and we got lost constantly. Mom would grumble from the back seat, and Dad would announce, every single time: ‘adventure is the whole point of a road trip.’” She turned and smiled at him. “So it’s fine. Getting lost is part of the date.”

Something turned over in his chest.

“Thank you.”

“For what?”

“Not complaining.”

She tipped her head.

“Do I LOOK like a complainer?”

“You’re a superstar,” he said gravely. “I assumed it’d be ‘YAH! You can’t even find a ROAD? My time is EXTREMELY valuable—’”

The impression was unflattering and accurate enough to earn him a pinch on the thigh.

“I do NOT talk like that!”

“Yah — are you INSANE? I’m driving!”

“I barely used any strength!”

“Then why so jumpy?”

“Maybe check WHERE your hand landed?”

She looked. Her hand was clamped on his thigh at an elevation best described as diplomatically sensitive. She went scarlet, let go — and then burst out laughing first, a laugh with a distinctly unsavory edge to it.

Mid-laughter, the car swung around a bend, and the world opened up: a flat meadow shelved above a valley. The campsite. Found.

✦   ✦   ✦

He started unloading the trunk. She hovered, itching to help, and was immediately defeated by the enormous tent bag.

“Too heavy. You take the sleeping bags.”

“I have TRAINED core strength!” She seized the tent bag, technically lifted it, and staggered one full step.

He rescued it, fighting the grin and losing.

“Yes, yes. Our Taeyeon, strongest in the land. Although—” a glance around, voice dropping confidentially, “—throw out your back now, and certain plans for tonight get canceled.”

She flushed to the ears in one frame, grabbed a sleeping bag, and threw it at him.

“Who— WHO agreed to any plans?!”

And fled, shyly, to a safe radius.

The moment she’d heard “camping,” she’d known exactly what he was scheming. …In fairness: she’d been eyeing those abs for months herself.

From behind a tree, she made one quiet phone call.

Outgoing · Eomma

“Eomma. I’m at Yeon-hee’s — probably staying over tonight.”

“Mm. Okay.”

“Eomma — saranghae.”

Her mother hung up with a soft, fond smile. This child. Still exactly like middle school. Aigoo.

“What’d Taeyeon say?” Kim Ji-woong was on vegetable duty at the sink, not looking up from the cucumber in his hand.

“She’s staying at Yeon-hee’s tonight.”

“MWO?” His hand spasmed. The cucumber snapped clean in half.

“Aigoo, you can’t even wash vegetables — out, out, OUT.”

Exiled from the kitchen, he took out his phone, hovered over his sister’s number for a long, long moment — and put it away.

“Ji-ho,” he growled at nobody. “The day Taeyeon introduces you, I will personally demonstrate the LOVE of an older brother-in-law.”

✦   ✦   ✦

At the campsite, tent assembly commenced — with Taeyeon seizing the lead on the strength of extensive theoretical credentials: she had watched a great many camping variety shows, and appeared on several outdoor ones.

Between theory and practice, however, yawned a canyon.

“That pole goes through here—” he began.

“I KNOW, I know!” She knelt on the tent fabric, brow furrowed at a flexible pole, jamming it with total confidence. “This is how they do it on TV… huh? Why won’t it go in?”

He crouched beside her and watched her wage war on the pole. Sunlight lay along her earnest profile; a fine dew of sweat stood on the tip of her nose. He watched for a good long while before saying, gently:

“It’s backwards.”

She froze. Rotated her head slowly toward him. Then launched.

“You KNEW! You were just WATCHING me embarrass myself!”

He dodged, laughing; they went down in a tangle on the tent fabric; somebody’s elbow took out the poles; and the half-built tent collapsed on top of them with a soft whump.

Sudden dark. Sudden quiet. The fabric shut out most of the light and made a small private room of the space between them, close enough to hear each other breathe.

She laughed first — muffled, shaking.

Then he did.

They found each other’s hands in the dark and touched foreheads.

“We’re so bad at this.”

“Mm.” His voice went soft. “But happy.”

The tent did, eventually, rise — under his management, with Taeyeon reassigned to tool delivery and “morale support,” which in practice meant sitting on the picnic mat eating mandarins and cheering. When the beige dome finally stood, she applauded like a dam had been completed.

“It’s our first home,” she said suddenly.

He was driving the last stake, and his hand paused on it. He looked back: she sat hugging her knees in the tent’s doorway, gazing into that small space with soft eyes, sun tracing her outline, wind lifting the fine hairs at her ear.

“Mm,” he said quietly. “The first.”

✦   ✦   ✦

At dusk he lit the charcoal.

She volunteered for skewer duty and produced a series of crooked, structurally questionable assemblies she herself couldn’t defend — pivoting instead to the claim that each skewer was color-balanced.

“It’s ART.” She held up a masterpiece of alternating pepper, onion, and beef, deadpan.

“Yes, yes, Artist Kim.” He accepted it gravely and laid it on the grill.

The charcoal ticked and popped; fat hissed; the smell unfurled across the campsite. She squatted by the grill and guarded it with the eyes of a small animal awaiting feeding. When the first skewer came off, he blew on it and handed it over.

“Careful, it’s hot—”

Too late. She’d bitten. She sucked air, eyes watering, refusing on principle to spit it out, huffing steam around the mouthful.

“Dummy.” Half worry, half laughter; he cracked a water bottle into her hands.

She gulped, fanned her tongue, and glared at the skewer with love.

“It’s too GOOD, that’s the problem… since when do you grill like this?”

Since deliberately. He’d practiced — and the romance of a mountainside dusk was quietly upgrading six-point food to eight.

“Since I decided to take you camping.” He turned the skewers. “Couldn’t have you eating burnt food on our first date.”

Her chewing slowed.

She looked at his firelit profile — intent on the grill, handsome, yes — but the handsome wasn’t the point tonight. The point was the practicing. The care. A full, pressing warmth spread through her chest, and it wasn’t just being moved; it was the deep-mattress safety of being treasured — the same feeling as watching him plant himself between her and the two most powerful men at SM.

“Ji-ho.”

“Mm?”

“Why do you like me?”

The question arrived out of the dark without warning. His hand paused on the skewer. He didn’t answer right away — he moved the cooked food to the rack’s edge and sat down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, both of them watching the coals breathe.

“If I say ‘no reason,’ is that a cop-out?”

“Massive cop-out.”

He laughed. Thought. Firelight moved in his eyes.

“At first — fan reasons, obviously. You on stage, lit up like you owned the entire world.”

His voice stayed level, like a man reading back a memory.

“Then one day I was heading to the production room and passed a practice room. You were pressing a pain-relief patch onto your lower back — and then drilling the same move into the mirror, over and over. Tiffany nuna and Seohyun nuna kept telling you to rest. You shook your head and said, ‘It’s fine. I can keep going.’”

She remembered that day. Mid-promotion rehearsals; the old injury flaring; the ache that arrived on schedule whenever the choreography ran long.

“And I thought: this person carries the entire weight of ‘perfect’ alone. As an idol. As the leader. The fans get flawless Taeyeon — and the ordinary human being gritting her teeth underneath, the one who hurts and tires, gets hidden away.” He turned and looked at her directly. “I wanted to meet the hidden one. I wanted to be the person who tells her, on the bad days: you don’t have to be perfect here.”

Her vision swam. She looked down at her own knotted hands, nails pressing crescents into her palms.

A long moment. Then, hoarse:

“That ordinary person is… a lot of trouble. Oversensitive. Cries easily. Unreasonable sometimes. Plus a mountain of garbage that comes with this job—”

“I know.” He took her hand, unbent the clenched fingers one by one, and laced his through. “I know all of it. I’m still here.”

The tears finally fell — onto their joined hands. She didn’t wipe them. Out here, beyond the cameras and the crowds, she was allowed to be only Taeyeon: allowed to be fragile, allowed to cry.

“And you?” he asked softly, thumb tracing the curve of her hand. “Why’d you accept me? Half the internet said it for weeks — I’m just a trainee.”

She sniffed and tipped her head back. Dusk had sealed the sky; the first star burned through the indigo.

“Because you’re very handsome.” A long, deliberate pause.

“…That’s IT?”

She stroked an imaginary beard with one hand, wrapped her fair fingers over his with the other, and smiled at him, eyes brimming with lamplight.

“Handsome is the entry requirement.”

“At first, it was your singing. Listening to you, I kept seeing myself — the me who’d just entered the company. A younger Taeyeon, basically. It made me want to look after you.”

“Then it was the talent. Music is the most important thing in my life, and your music kept showing me MORE of you.”

“But what decided it was the courage. The plans. The way you carry things.”

The moment you stood in front of Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim and the sajang-nim and put yourself between me and everything — that’s when I fell all the way, Ji-ho.

The charcoal spat a single spark into the dark; it climbed and died. Their hands stayed woven together, warm and solid.

✦   ✦   ✦

After dinner he brought out the ukulele. She cocooned herself in a blanket against his shoulder and tilted her face up at a sky the city had never once shown her — stars in reckless surplus, the Milky Way a hazed bridge across the whole dark.

“Requests?” He tuned by ear.

“‘A Boy.’” Then a small headshake. “No. Let’s sing together. ‘Some.’”

The strings hummed under his stilled hand. A beat.

“…Okay.”

Performance

The intro drifts out — simpler than any studio version, and warmer for it. Two voices braid over four nylon strings and rise into the valley wind: the almost, sung by two people who are no longer almost anything. The last chord hangs in the night air a long time before it lets go.

“Nuna.” He set the ukulele down, voice tighter than usual. “About this song… the company has a new plan.”

He laid it out: the debut project, IU, all of it — hiding neither the company’s logic nor his own tug-of-war. He spoke slowly, weighing every word, but the words had to be said, and he said them all the way to the end.

She listened in silence, fingers pulling absently at the blanket’s fringe.

When he finished, she gave a small “mm.”

“I understand,” she said — and the voice came out impossibly level. “IU suits it. The song will be a hit. For you, it’s the best possible start.”

A rational statement. Flawless.

But her knuckles had gone white around the fringe, and her lips had pressed into one thin stubborn line.

“Taeyeon—”

“I SAID I understand.” Faster now. “Really. I’m an artist, I know how these decisions work. And we— it’s not like we could sing it together publicly anyway.” She attempted a smile; her mouth declined to cooperate. “So. It’s good. Really.”

“I’m sorry.” A thousand words, reduced to the only two left.

“Don’t say sorry.” She shook her head — and the tears finally broke loose. “Don’t… that makes it worse…” She swiped at them and they kept coming. “I’m fine… it’s just… it’s MY song… in my heart it was already—”

The sentence drowned. She shot to her feet, blanket sliding to the grass, and walked fast — almost ran — to the tent. Her back looked very thin. Her shoulders were shaking.

He followed immediately. Inside, a single camp lantern burned low and amber; she sat with her back to him, arms around her knees, folded down as small as she could make herself.

He tried gathering her in from behind. She pulled away — hard, with her whole body.

He let the stalemate breathe. Then he settled behind her, drew one long breath, and — unaccompanied, into that tiny fabric room — began, very quietly, to sing.

Performance

A song she has never heard, because it does not exist yet: an apology in the shape of a ballad — a man begging her not to hear “sorry” as leaving, singing that the word undoes him too.

Her shoulders tremble once.

Verse by verse, the song stops being about parting at all and becomes about treasuring — an inventory of everything he refuses to lose. He sings of her eyes — and kisses them, each damp lid, softly. Of her nose — and his lips find the high bridge of it. Of her lips — peach-sweet, cool, soft. Of her hands, which once touched his face so gently — and he lifts her hand and lays it against his cheek.

Her fingers curl at the contact — then slowly open, and trace the line of his jaw.

The last line says: I can still feel you. The song ends. The tent holds nothing but breath, and far away, the small silver noise of insects.

Another song no one’s ever heard. An apology in a melody. Ji-ho — you really are the worst.

She turned around, rims of her eyes red, saying nothing — just looking at him.

Then she leaned in and kissed him.

It began featherlight, salted faintly with tears — and deepened, degree by degree, into something blazing, pouring across the gap every single thing that hadn’t survived being spoken.

He turned with her, laying her down gently against the soft sleeping mat, and the small amber room closed around them. The lantern got knocked askew at some point; its light slanted up the tent wall and trembled there like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the mountains kept their silence and the stars wheeled on schedule.

And in a wilderness beyond every label and every watching eye, she was finally, only, his Taeyeon — and he was only her Ji-ho.

Near dawn she slept, worn through and content, her head pillowed on his arm.

He lay looking up at the tent ceiling, listening to the even tide of her breathing, and felt — for the first time in either of his lives — entirely whole.
