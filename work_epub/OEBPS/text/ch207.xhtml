Chapter Two Hundred Seven

The Promotion Machine

June 5, 2013 The salon → the Shilla Hotel → an evening press screening a suit that silences a room, a red carpet on fire — and the first reviews land

The car slid into the salon’s block; Sulli found a space and parked, the morning’s fizz still sitting in her eyes.

Inside, the head stylist — Director Kim — came forward beaming. “Ji-ho-ssi, you’re in TOP condition today. With a base like this, we barely have to work. Oh — Sulli-ssi too? That red dress is perfect on you.”

“Thank you, eonni~”

Sulli, sweetness itself, installed herself in the one seat with a direct sightline to him, propped her chin in her hand, and settled in to watch — unblinking — as if styling were a spectator sport and she had front-row season tickets.

The salon was one of the industry’s top rooms; famous faces cycled through daily, and nobody blinked at anything anymore.

The process ran its course — cleanse, skin prep, base, brows — his eyes closed or chin tilted on command, entirely relaxed. She watched the whole time, gaze tracing lashes, nose line, jawline, the small roll of his throat when he swallowed. By the time the hair came together — loose, tousled, deliberately careless — her heartbeat had abandoned its time signature.

The face had always been unfair. Professionally polished, it stopped being a face and became a jurisdiction.

Then wardrobe. Dong-hwan produced the Gucci: a deep-gray bespoke suit with a quiet expensive sheen, silk white shirt beneath, one collar button open, no tie — formality and disobedience in exact balance.

When he stepped out of the changing room, the salon hit a mute button.

The cut drew the wide shoulders and narrow waist in two strokes; the deep gray cooled his skin to porcelain; the styled hair surrendered the full forehead and the brow bone above those eyes. He wasn’t posing. He adjusted one cuff and looked up — and the compound effect of a boy’s freshness, a man’s gravity, top-shelf tailoring, and the invisible machinery of Big Star and Absolute Focus landed on the room like weather.

“Wah—” A young assistant mid-perm clapped a hand over her own mouth, eyes still shining over it.

“Completely daebak… he’s a walking pictorial today,” another stylist murmured.

“That build, that face — ‘the male star women most want to date,’ number one, no recount needed.”

Director Kim folded her arms and admired her own work, then glanced sideways, grinning. “Sulli-ssi. Am I right or am I right?”

Sulli did not hear the question.

She had left the building. It wasn’t that she’d never seen him in formalwear — but today’s version had unsheathed something the lazy everyday surface usually kept holstered: the edge, the command. The light followed him like it was unionized and he was the shop steward. When he tipped his head to murmur something to Dong-hwan, the clean line of his neck and the curve of his throat made her forget to breathe on schedule. Her heart had been picked up by something soft with claws in it. Her face burned. Her eyes were doing something openly criminal: memorizing him, centimeter by centimeter, for later.

“Sulli?” He was in front of her, one hand waving gently through her sightline, amusement warm in the voice. “Gone somewhere?”

She surfaced with a start, met his laughing eyes — which held, in perfect miniature, her own dazed face.

“I— I was NOT!” She stood in a flurry, orbited him once with theatrical scrutiny, and delivered her verdict chin-first. “Passable. Barely handsomer than usual. By — a fraction.”

The eyes — lit up like signage, helplessly adoring — filed a full dissent.

He didn’t prosecute. He flicked the tip of her nose. “Let’s go, Driver Choi. The venue awaits.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Afternoon. The Shilla Hotel ballroom — the production press conference for The Girl in the Sun.

The stage backed onto the film’s key-art wall; the front rows bristled with lenses; behind them, invited fans and industry seats. The air had a low charge in it.

When he and Sulli hit the red-carpet entrance, the shutters fused into a single sustained roar, flashes strobing the hall white.

Deep-gray Gucci, spine straight, a faint composed smile — every motion arriving pre-lit. And beside him: Sulli, changed into a pale-yellow strapless mini dress, skirt flaring soft, skin winning its argument with the lighting rig, black hair loose, smile like a small sun that had opted into show business. The height difference alone was a poster. The combined face-value was a public safety event.

“Ji-ho-ssi! Over here!”

“Sulli-ssi! Here!”

“Center, please, both of you!”

“Ji-ho-ssi — attending with Sulli-ssi today, how do you feel?”

“Sulli-ssi! You play lovers this time — how was the experience?”

“There’s a kiss scene in the film, we hear — are you two dating?”

The questions came braided into the shutter-noise. He held the smile, nodded to the familiar bylines, answered nothing on the move — one hand hovering, gentleman-correct, at the small of Sulli’s back as he steered her up onto the stage to join Kwak Jae-yong, Lee Kwang-soo, Lee Min-ki, Kim Hae-sook, Sung Dong-il, Chae Soo-bin and the rest of the line.

Kwang-soo lit up on sight. “Ji-ho-ya. You’re too bright today. Standing next to you is a workplace hazard.”

“Kwang-soo hyung, your serious face has its own… distinguished quality.”

A step down the line, Chae Soo-bin was looking at him and briefly somewhere else at the same time.

He’s — brighter than he was on set. How is that possible?

“Soo-bin. Long time.”

“Mm. Long time, Ji-ho-ssi.” She surfaced and returned the smile properly.

Sulli made her bows down the senior line, then took station just off his shoulder — where her gaze kept drifting, unbidden, to the straight back and the profile that outshone a stage full of professionals.

✦   ✦   ✦

The conference ran its rails: the director on his return to pure love; the cast trading set stories. When his turn came he talked script origins and the interior of Seo Yeon-woo — sincere, funny in the right ratios — the mic carrying that low-grain voice out across a hall that kept breaking into suppressed cheers.

Sulli sat with her chin propped, listening with her whole face, pride and worship entirely unencrypted.

Asked for her leading-lady reflections, she took the mic with a smile like opening weather. “Getting to work with seniors like these, inside a script this good, was the most valuable schooling of my life. And especially — Ji-ho Oppa. On set he guided me through everything, scene by scene, with so much patience. Truly — thank you.”

She turned to him on the last words, eyes bending into crescents, and the feeling in them ran right up to the rim.

He met the look and returned it — soft, steadying.

The room’s fan section audibly died of it. The lenses feasted. Shutter-thunder, round two.

A pure-love film sells on its leads: they gave the cameras what the cameras came for — shoulder to shoulder, matched glances, clean smiles — professional, unhurried, extremely printable.

At exit, the crowd surged; he tucked her behind his arm and broke a path through the lenses, and she followed in his wake, close enough to feel the warmth off his sleeve, wrapped in the particular safety of being escorted through chaos by the person the chaos is aimed at.

Sweet. All the way down.

In the car, sealed away from the noise, she exhaled hugely and then spun on him, eyes enormous.

“Oppa! Today you were— you were TOO handsome!” Vocabulary failed; volume compensated. “On stage I blanked mid-cue TWICE because of you!”

He loosened his collar and arched an eyebrow, drawling. “Oh? Only today?”

“YAH!” She lunged over and thumped his shoulder, laughing despite herself. “ALWAYS! Today ESPECIALLY! There — satisfied, narcissist Oppa?!”

He caught the offending hand, laughing, and looked at her — flushed, lit up, vivid with the day — and found the whole picture excellent.

“Mm. Satisfied.” He let go and ruffled her hair. “Rest a while. There’s still the press screening tonight.”

“Not tired!”

And underneath the bounce, a small voice keeping its own ledger: I think I like him even more than yesterday. Enough that hiding it in public is becoming a professional skill.

✦   ✦   ✦

Evening. A premium cinema across town: the media preview — trade press, name critics, distributor representatives.

Guests signed the logo wall, collected the press kit and gift bag; the loop of trailer and behind-cuts ran on the big screen while the room filled. Brief remarks from the distributor; then Kwak Jae-yong and his leads took the stage for introductions and a few short reflections; then the lights died completely.

The encrypted print rolled. Staff walked the aisles softly against camcorders. For two hours the room belonged to light and sound alone — the fantastical meeting, the sugared daily life, the countdown hiding inside the love story, the ending that folded hope into reincarnation. Down front, critics’ silhouettes sat unmoving. Somewhere in the middle rows, a laugh; later, at the empty-apartment shot after the cat’s life gutters out, the unmistakable small sounds of people wiping their eyes in the dark.

Sulli’s native, unstudied cat-ness fit the role like weather fits a season; and his Seo Yeon-woo ran the full ladder — wonder, love, refusal, grief, and that last thin stubborn hope — rung by rung, without one false step. Over it all: Tiffany’s theme floating in like oxygen, the duet with Taeyeon running underneath the reunion like a tide, and Kwak’s frames composed like postcards from a kinder planet.

The lights came up. One beat of silence.

Then applause — from the corners first, then everywhere, sustained, unforced, long.

The Q&A ran friendly: intent, performances, the music. Asked about box-office hopes, Kwak grinned into the mic. “Come in wanting to believe in love. I don’t think this film will let you down.”

He added, simply: “It’s a film about companionship, love, and keeping hope after loss. We’d like it to warm the summer.”

Photos; brief scheduled one-on-ones; a slow, warm dispersal — critics clustering in twos and threes by the doors, still talking; several coming over unprompted to shake the director’s hand, and his.

By late evening, the embargo-safe first impressions were live.

The July 2 date was strategy, not accident: land ahead of the blockbuster crush, let the word of mouth compound, own the early summer. On tonight’s evidence — story, performances, frames, and a two-song soundtrack people were already demanding — the film had every tool it needed.

SM’s machine spun up in parallel — the two OSTs had genuinely startled the company, and pushing them meant pushing Taeyeon and Tiffany. CJ, reading the preview data, began adding screens. And one by one, the members of Girls’ Generation kept their promise: the film’s poster, reposted across their accounts, rippling outward through the night.

Late. Sulli pulled the car to a stop at the villa; the engine ticked into silence.

He unclipped his belt, leaned across, and kissed her once. “Drive carefully going home.”

Good cologne. Warm mouth. And suddenly — she did not want to go home at all.

His hand had just found the door handle when slim fingers closed around his wrist.

She was looking at him with her eyes full of lamplight and something underneath it — the pale-yellow dress soft in the dim car, the whole evening still warm on her skin.

“Oppa. Tonight — I don’t want to go back.”

He looked at her for two full seconds. Then he smiled.

“Pull the car into the garage.”
