Chapter Twenty-Six

Misfortune Hunts the Suffering

“Ehh, hyung, hyung — the video, then! The video! When we’ve had our fun before, didn’t we always share to the group chat? Sulli — fine, I know you’d never post her. But at least let your brother watch it after. Just to feast the eyes! You’re only playing with her anyway — it’s not like you’re going to marry her, right?”

“Who marries a woman with depression? She’s the sick one, not me.”

The sneer came with something venomous underneath. Of course there would be a video — a video was leverage, insurance, a leash for afterward. But no one else would ever see it. That was the whole point of a leash.

“No video sharing this time.” A fold of bills changed hands. “Go drink.”

“Thank you, hyung — thank you, hyung.”

The vial passed the other way, snatched with an eagerness that told its own story.

“Good brother.” An arm hooked over Yun Seong-sik’s shoulder. “Speaking of the group — why’s it gone so quiet lately?”

“Don’t ask. Dry season. No good targets around.”

The two of them left the storeroom shoulder to shoulder, chatting like men leaving a bowling alley.

Behind the shelving, Ji-ho’s hand was white around his phone. He saved the video, checked it twice, and only then rose stiffly from the crouch, face dark as slate.

A man could be a rake — heaven knew Ji-ho had been one. But there was a floor under that word, and these two lived miles beneath it. Drugs. Cameras. Group chats. Not men. Vermin with a taste for grooming.

And he’d seen this exact machinery before — from the far side of history. Choi Jae’s playbook was, move for move, the same one that would horrify the world years from now: the Nth Room, the Jung Joon-young chats, the Seungri scandal. Drug the woman. Film the crime. Build a group chat and pass the horror around like trading cards.

His thoughts went to the last timeline — to the Sulli the world watched unravel. The sudden change in her, the “living unfiltered” era the tabloids feasted on, and then the ending nobody was willing to have seen coming. He’d bet everything he owned that it started here — with this man, or one exactly like him, slipping through the crack in a lonely girl’s armor. Win her starving heart with counterfeit warmth, wear her as a trophy, and discard her when the shine came off — one more stone stacked onto an illness that was already winning. Until it won.

And now he was standing at the exact fork where that road began.

Ji-ho was no crusader — he’d have been the first to say it. Fourteen years of bars had shown him spiked drinks and worse, and he had lived by the oldest rule of nightlife: other people’s karma is other people’s karma. You don’t touch it.

But.

Seven years old when her father walked out. Shipped off to earn at eleven. He knew that arithmetic — his own started at eight, in a Koreatown apartment that emptied out one parent at a time. He knew exactly what three in the morning felt like when the whole world had contracted to one small person who nobody was coming back for — that airless, drowning helplessness with no adult on the other end of it.

An unhappy childhood takes a lifetime to heal. And if he stood aside and watched a girl with his own childhood walk the same road to the same cliff — knowing, this time, where the road went—

He couldn’t. It was that simple. He asked himself the question once, honestly, and couldn’t.

One long breath, dragging himself level. Then he was moving — out of the storeroom at speed, eyes raking the bar for Choi Jae.

Instead his gaze snagged on a figure ahead. …Sulli?

She had swallowed her tranquilizers in the restroom, waited out the worst of the shaking, and was now trudging back toward the booth on heavy legs — when a tall silhouette planted itself in her path. She sighed on reflex, dredged up the professional smile, and recited the litany:

“I’m sorry — no photos tonight, and I can’t really sign anything either.”

A small bow, a sidestep—

“Do I look like a fan to you?” A hand closed on her wrist. “Come with me.”

“Yah—!” The color dropped out of her face; she wrenched against his grip, voice a furious whisper. “Who ARE you? Let go of me!”

“Quiet. Sulli. Unless you’d like the two of us trending together by sunrise?”

That stopped her — a flinch, a beat of wavering — and then, half dragged and half deciding, she let him pull her into the backstage break room.

The instant the door shut she tore her arm free and rounded on him, eyes blazing wet.

“Yah! WHO are you? What do you want?”

He didn’t answer. He opened the video on his phone and held it out between them, voice low.

“See for yourself.”

She took the phone, suspicion in every line of her — then saw the face frozen on the thumbnail. …Oppa? Her finger trembled on the play button.

“Enough talk. The vial. Give. Tonight she’s MINE—”

“Her dad divorced out when she was seven — gone, vanished. Her mother shipped her to SM to earn and never looked back. That girl is a stray dog on a cold street—”

“Watch how I train her after tonight—”

“Who marries a woman with depression? She’s the sick one, not me—”

“I’ll record tonight. Give you a little show, brother—”

Word after word, each one a blade going in to the hilt.

Her face drained to paper. Both hands flew to her mouth. The tears came all at once, a dam simply ceasing to exist — and she staggered backward until her legs quit and she sat down hard on the floor, lips moving around the same broken syllables.

“No. No, it’s not — it can’t—”

Abandoned by her father before she could spell the word. Turned into an income stream by her mother. Wrung out by the company that owned her hours. And the one person — the ONE person — who had seemed to genuinely care… had been running a con the entire time. A costume of kindness, tailored to her exact starvation, worn by a man who described her to his friends as a stray dog he was training. Who was going to drug her tonight? Who planned to film it?

How could he? How could anyone? How—

Her fists beat against her own chest, tears streaming — and no sound would come. Her throat had sealed around a boulder. It was grief with the air cut off, despair in a vacuum, and she was suffocating inside it in total silence.

Ji-ho watched her come apart, and his own eighth year rose in him like water finding its level — the door that closed and never reopened, the second door a few years later, the apprenticeship in surviving alone. People always bleed for the wounds they recognize. He crouched down slowly and set a hand on her back, patting gently, uselessly, the way he’d once wished someone would.

Field Notes

Rope frays where it’s thinnest; misfortune hunts the ones already suffering. The old proverb describes a real and merciless arithmetic: hardship compounds.

The “unlucky” are usually the long-deprived — of resources, of affection, of safety. Every new blow drains reserves they never had, and a lifetime without positive feedback teaches the deepest lesson of learned helplessness: nothing I do matters. From there come the passive choices, the desperate gambles, the spiral that feeds itself.

A girl raised without either parent’s love grows a pleaser’s reflexes over a sensitive core — doubts herself in every silence, blames herself for every storm, errs because she fears erring, and sinks by degrees into the very illness that misfortune was building all along. And then, weakened, she draws the predators too.

But a short, brutal pain beat a long, fatal one. Her illness hadn’t rooted deep yet — not in 2012. Cut the parasite off now, get her real treatment, and Sulli could still walk back out of the dark, whole.
