Chapter Eighty-Four

The Debut Project

The Day of the Busan Trip SM Entertainment — Top-Floor Strategy Room ten people, one future on the table

A dozen figures around the long table, the air formal and taut. Lee Soo-man at the head; CEO Kim Young-min at his left hand; music director Yoo Young-jin at his right. Below them: the heads of Production Teams One, Two, and Three — Jo Woo-chul, Kim Wook, Kwon Yoon-jung — plus the leads of Marketing and Planning.

No pleasantries.

“I’ve called you here to discuss the development path of one particular trainee.”

The projector lit: the trainee file, and the essential data on two songs — including the library system’s anomalous statistics on the second one.

“Ji-ho. American. Nineteen. Roughly three months with the company. First monthly evaluation: overall first place on the strength of an original song, promoted to A-rank. The second song — ‘Some’ — written and demoed independently. I trust some of you have heard it. Or at least heard OF it.”

Jo Woo-chul nodded. Kim Young-min and Yoo Young-jin studied the screen — particularly the library’s near-unprecedented “recommendation intent” figures and a feedback record with effectively zero negative marks.

“This trainee—” a pause, the gaze sweeping the table, “—has brought me a proposal. He does not want a group. He wants to debut directly as a solo artist.”

Silence — then a low ripple of muttering. The faces around the table ran the gamut, most of them landing somewhere between startled and skeptical.

Kim Young-min spoke first, brows drawn.

“Solo? Chongkwan-nim, that demands real caution. The company’s center of gravity right now is TTS and EXO — resources are already stretched to the seams. A brand-new solo launch, a MALE solo at that, means promotion, production, and a management team — none of it cheap. And he’s a foreigner.”

Marketing followed immediately.

“The CEO is right. Market acceptance is the wall. Domestic listeners judge soloists far more harshly than idol groups. A foreign-born male soloist air-dropped in with no group fandom underneath him — where does the fanbase COME from? How do we manufacture the conversation? The risk profile is severe.”

Team Two’s Kim Wook added his weight.

“His practice logs show elite vocals, granted — but the dancing sits at upper-C-rank at best. Stage presence can’t be judged off two monthly evaluations. Variety instincts: completely unknown. A solo artist stands on personal magnetism and all-around skills. Three months is… three months.”

Team Three’s Kwon Yoon-jung:

“And is the writing SUSTAINABLE? Two good songs can be lightning in a bottle. If the output dries up, the solo route hits a wall instantly. Slot him into the next boy group instead — core vocal, resident writer — and the strengths get used while the risk gets shared. Far sounder.”

The objections were concentrated and entirely on target: resources, market, readiness, risk, passport. Real obstacles, laid on the table in plain view.

Yoo Young-jin had been silent throughout, reading the arrangement breakdown of “Some” for the third time. At last he looked up.

“The song is genuinely excellent. The boy’s taste and instincts exceed plenty of working professionals. But — Soo-man hyung — solo, NOW? Isn’t that rushing it? Let him do OSTs first, a few featurings, build recognition… THEN talk solo?”

Every eye went to the head of the table. The opposition was reasoned; even the veteran music director favored the conservative road.

Lee Soo-man heard all of it out — and nodded, slowly, without a trace of displeasure.

“Every concern raised is real. I’ve weighed each one myself. You’re right: by convention, a three-month foreign trainee debuting solo sounds like a fantasy. Five years ago — three years ago — I wouldn’t have convened this meeting.”

Then he leaned forward, one finger tapping the table.

“But the times are moving. The market is moving. And SM cannot afford to be the one thing in this industry that stands still. Ask yourselves — what IS this company’s core competence?”

He answered his own question.

“Systematized star-making. Superior production. And a record of shipping artists who DEFINE trends instead of following them. H.O.T. to BoA. TVXQ to Girls’ Generation. Now EXO. We have never stopped experimenting — that IS the brand.”

The finger moved to the projection.

“Ji-ho is not an ordinary trainee. What he owns is the asset this industry will value more with every passing year: top-grade writing, married to a voice distinctive enough to carry it. That is not something an assembly line produces in volume. That is SCARCITY.”

He turned to the CEO.

“Kim sajang — you say resources are tight. Then invert the frame. A soloist who writes his own material at commercial grade slashes our production costs and our dependence on outside writers from day one. And we launch light: a digital single first, costs contained, escalation only on proven market response. Compare that to the capital bonfire of building another full group — the EARLY risk is actually lower.”

To Marketing:

“Acceptance? Precisely BECAUSE he has no group history, he has no fixed image to fight. We build from zero: the pure musician. The prodigy writer. And his American background is not a liability — it’s a bridge. Native English, Los Angeles-raised, fluent in both markets. This company has been hunting for credible roads into the global mainstream for years; EXO’s dual-track launch shows what a market bridge is worth. A writer-artist rooted in the SM system who can walk into Western media without an interpreter? That may be a better move than any of us has priced in.”

To the team heads:

“Readiness? Three months is short — but certain gifts don’t answer to calendars, and his improvement curve is the steepest in the building. We build him the densest targeted program we’ve ever run. Stage experience accrues from small live venues. Variety sense gets ground in on carefully chosen programs — DURING activity, not before it. Remember: BoA debuted at thirteen.”

A beat.

“And as for public acceptance — you’re all forgetting something. He saved Sulli. The public already adores this boy. The narrative writes itself with one gentle push.”
