Chapter One Hundred Seventy-Four

Sulli-yah, I Have a Girlfriend

May 2013 · the final day of shooting Gapado Island, off Jeju the last scene, the wrap, a bonfire — and a confession in the dark

Dusk on Gapado was beautiful enough to hurt.

The falling sun poured molten gold across an endless sea, staining it a gorgeous burnt orange, and the waves broke soft against the rocks like an elegy sung for a story about to end. This was where the school-years timeline had been shot — every corner of the island held a memory: the tree-lined path flooded with late light, the clear stream, the soft green mountains stacked along the horizon. Every frame came out looking like a poem. There was no better place for youth, or first love.

Neither lead needed help playing young. Twenty years old apiece, maintained to an idol’s standard, buttoned into high-school uniforms — they turned instantly into the most luminous boy and girl on any campus, and the camera believed every second of it.

But under the loveliness, the story had turned toward its built-in sorrow. As the deadline set by the Samshin grandmother closed in, the world’s memory of Go-i was being erased, hand over invisible hand — first distant coworkers, then the adoptive parents who loved her — until, on the final day, it came for the man whose fate was knotted deepest into hers.

On this island that held their first and last memories, The Girl in the Sun arrived at its final scene.

“The Girl in the Sun! Finale — take one!”

“Action!”

The lens opened on the sea, wide and glittering.

This was the water where Seo Yeon-woo had once saved a small cat — Go-i’s original body — and, later, where two teenagers had shared a first green kiss. Now Go-i sat alone facing it, her back a thin, solitary line against all that blue, as if she might dissolve into it at any moment.

“Nyaa~”

A pitch-perfect imitation of a cat, from behind her.

She turned, slowly. Seo Yeon-woo stood there wearing the gentle smile of a man who finally knew everything — the Samshin grandmother had given him the whole truth.

“The goldfish — Little Rascal. That was you, wasn’t it? You ate him.”

She held his gaze a long moment, everything moving at once behind her eyes — guilt, longing, love, and one clean line of relief. He knows. At last, he knows.

Then she laughed, and the sunset lit the laugh so bright it broke the heart outright.

“I’m sorry.”

Shimmering water, extravagant light, a beautiful girl and a beautiful boy — the camera crept close, harvesting every millimeter of expression, and every frame came out poster-perfect. The lovelier it looked, the heavier the goodbye weighed.

The scene moved. He crossed to her and put out his hand.

“Let’s go ride the bike.”

“The memories — they’ll disappear soon, you know.”

“It’s fine. Come with me.”

The shadow in her eyes thinned a little; the smile crept back. “If you’re going to live as a human, I suppose — you have to do some useless things.”

His hand closed around hers. “That’s right. That’s exactly what a person IS. We know the most beautiful things get harvested by time, like the green barley on this island — and we plant anyway, every season, and love with everything we have anyway.” He looked into her eyes. “What matters isn’t how long the world remembers us. It’s how deeply, right now, we remember each other.”

Two hands, gripped tight.

Then the film’s most romantic and most merciless sequence: the bicycle down the winding coast road, her arms around him from behind, the sea wind tearing at hair and hems — two people setting the coming forever-goodbye down by the roadside for one hour and laughing like the world’s most ordinary, luckiest couple. The camera chased them: the flying skirt, the locked arms, the leaning silhouettes.

Off to the side, the Samshin grandmother watched the sky, pity standing in her old eyes, murmuring to no one.

“Let today’s sun run long. Give them — just a little more time.”

The bicycle rolled to a stop where everything had started. Hand in hand, they stood side by side before the vast water that had witnessed their meeting, their love, and now their parting.

“Oppa. You saved me here.”

“And here is where we — fell in love.”

“And now, finally — we’ve come back.”

She turned to face him, rose to her toes, closed her eyes — the last kiss, offered.

He tilted his head away.

Her eyes opened on raw hurt. “Do you… already hate me? Even this one last wish—?”

“No.” His hand came up and smoothed her hair, grief standing plainly in his eyes. “It just feels like — the cycle is about to close.”

“The cycle?”

“We kissed here. Fell in love here. Then we were pulled apart. Then — reunion, running away, marriage. If we kiss here again… doesn’t that mean the loop truly ends?” He was looking at her like a man carving a face into his soul. “My memory is almost full of you now.”

Full — and then erased.

She didn’t answer. She let go of his hand, turned back to the sea, gave him only her thin silhouette, and when her voice came again it had gone quiet and level.

“Oppa. Was I — a good wife, back then?”

He stepped up beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and corrected her gently. “‘Back then’? No. ‘From now on’ — always.”

“You really are an idiot.” She laughed, and the tears fell without sound. She wiped them with the back of her hand, turned her face to him, and said it with her whole heart. “But it’s okay. I still have — eight lives left.”

“I’ll find you again. And fall in love with you again, Oppa.”

He had told her once that cats live nine lives.

“Until then — let’s both live well, somewhere. Each of us.”

“And then someday — we meet again. And love each other again.”

She turned, rose onto her toes a second time, and kissed him.

This time he didn’t turn away. His eyes closed, and he answered the kiss with everything in it — farewell and vow, love and defiance, all of it poured into one point of contact. The two of them fit together without a seam, and the sun’s last light stretched their joined silhouette long and thin across the water, as if to fold it into the horizon for keeps.

“CUT!!!”

Kwak Jae-yong’s voice cracked through the megaphone, thick with feeling. “PERFECT! I hereby declare — The Girl in the Sun — officially WRAPPED!!!”

“WOOOAAAAHHH!!!”

The dam broke. Cheers, applause, whistles — the whole coastline went up at once. Two and a half months, from late-winter chill to early-summer warmth, across half the country: the film that carried all their labor and feeling had closed its final bracket.

They separated slowly, both breathing unevenly, eyes rimmed red, neither fully back from where the scene had taken them.

She looked at him from inches away, and the two and a half months replayed themselves at speed. In front of the cameras they had embraced, kissed, squabbled, split ordinary days between them — all that sanctioned closeness, all those permitted touches, all those long, scripted looks. Today it ended, officially and forever.

Did she ache at that? Of course.

The license to be close to him in broad daylight — expired today.

Regret, though? She shook her head, very slightly, and felt something braver rise instead. No. Because the scripted intimacy was over — which meant the unscripted story, HER story, was finally free to begin. She had decided: a real confession. These two months of blurred lines had ruined “little sister” for good.

“Everyone — two months of hard work! Thank you!” Kwak had the megaphone, wearing the smile of a man setting down a piano he’d carried uphill. “Back in Seoul — I’ve booked the Seoul Hotel, wrap party in three days! Eat, drink, be compensated!”

“The director is a KING!”

“Thank you, Director!”

“Ji-ho-ssi! Sulli-ssi! Wrap photo! Over here!”

“Ne!”

They pulled themselves together and waded into the celebration. The full-company photo froze the moment for good; then came the round of farewells — the local Gapado crew, the young supporting cast who’d stayed on the island for the school-years material. Lee Kwang-soo, Chae Soo-bin and the Seoul-side cast had wrapped their pieces earlier; the real party would happen back in the capital. And since the last ferry to Jeju had already gone, the whole crew would sleep on the island one more night.

That evening the islanders and the production’s support staff threw the long tables open under the sky — a seafood feast at the edge of the water, beer and soju criss-crossing, laughter unbroken. Sea wind, low stars, two and a half months of weight sliding off a hundred pairs of shoulders at once.

At peak volume, somebody started the inevitable chant: the writer-slash-leading-man owes us a song. He didn’t fight it. A guitar materialized; he settled by the bonfire and played.

bonfire, unplugged · “Eyes, Nose, Lips” · Ji-ho · 2012

Low voice over six strings, the surf keeping time underneath — the farewell in the song folding into the farewell in the air, until the two are indistinguishable.

The crew goes quiet without agreeing to. Even the director’s chin is moving with the phrases.

The last chord thins out against the waves. One beat of sea-noise. Then the applause rolls down the whole length of the table.

“Now Sulli! Make her sing! DUET!”

Several beers deep, cheeks glowing pink, she lit up at the suggestion and looked straight at him. He grinned, nodded, and his fingers found a new figure on the strings — the sweet, cat-and-mouse opening of a certain duet.

bonfire duet · “Some” · Ji-ho & IU · 2012 — tonight’s cover: the leading lady takes the lady’s part

Her voice comes in sweet and a little tipsy-soft on the opening line about a phone that never rings; his answer slides in on the counter-line about something that’s almost his, and their eyes catch across the fire.

The song is ABOUT the maybe-space between two people — and every person at the table hears exactly how little acting is happening.

Around the fire, the crew hums the hooks under their breath. Firelight, laughing faces, the whole beach gone warm and half-romantic.

The dinner wound down through its last toasts. People drifted off in twos and threes — to bed, or to a final quiet drink.

She drew a long breath of cold salt air, felt the alcohol thin and the courage thicken, and scanned the dark until she found him: alone on a rock apart from the crowd, watching the black water.

“Oppa. Walk with me? Just a little.”

He turned at her voice. Flushed cheeks, and eyes that were far too bright for the hour — expectation burning in them at a wavelength he knew on sight.

What’s coming, comes.

He ran the ledger one more time. The women knotted into his life sorted into two kinds. Sulli, Taeyeon, Joo-hyun — they had known him back when he had nothing, and built something deep against that plain early canvas; in their eyes he was still, somewhere, the good and trustworthy boy from the start. Then there were the ones like Ji-eun and Tiffany, who had long since diagnosed the philanderer and stayed tangled anyway — adult players in a game everyone at the table understood, where the worst case was a battlefield of jealousy, not a shattering. Sulli’s kind of pure, stubborn devotion was the far harder thing to answer.

So answer it. Tonight.

He picked up the flashlight beside him and stood. “Okay. Let’s walk.”

✦   ✦   ✦

They followed the same coast road the bicycle had taken that afternoon, walking side by side away from the thinning noise. The flashlight was the only light; the surf was the only soundtrack. She slipped a hand around his arm.

The night wind off the water ran colder than expected, wet, straight through her thin layers. She shivered; the last of the beer burned off; her head went clear and her heart went faster.

“Cold?”

“A little~” An honest nod, a slightly embarrassed smile.

He said nothing — just shrugged out of his jacket and settled it over her shoulders. It came wrapped in his body heat and that clean, fresh smell of his; the cold lost on contact, and her heartbeat skipped once, hard. She pulled the front closed and ducked her chin into it, smiling at the ground, sugar spreading through her chest.

When he started walking again, his hand found hers — naturally, warm and dry around her chilled fingers.

They left the sand for a raised grass path. The flashlight beam swept across barley fields gone deep gold in the dark, ripe heads hissing softly against each other in the wind. Sea wind. Barley waves. Bottomless dark, one cone of light, and beside her, holding her hand, the man whose breathing she knew by heart.

The strange, floating certainty arrived: right now, between sky and water, the world contained exactly two people.

They found a flat shelf of grass and sat, facing a sea that at night read as pure, fathomless ink.

“Oppa~”

“Mm?”

“Can we — turn off the flashlight?”

Darkness would lend her courage. Darkness felt private. Safe.

Click.

True dark swallowed everything. And with sight gone, every other sense came up loud: the surf, closer; salt and crushed grass and warm earth and barley; and beside her, his even breathing, and the settled, safe smell of him.

Then — no warning at all — a warm, tipsy, sweet-scented weight pushed him gently backward into the grass, and cool, soft, trembling lips found his in the dark.

Two and a half months of filming had taught their mouths each other’s shapes and rhythms; after one startled half-second, he answered on reflex. But this kiss ran on none of the set’s rules — no director, no blocking, no lens. It was hotter, more direct, entirely and unapologetically HERS. The dark, the open air, the beloved warmth in reach — the feelings she’d pressed flat for months burst the levee all at once, and she poured across him, urgent and thorough, as if to convert every borrowed on-camera intimacy into something owned, and stamp her name on it.

Only when his hands pressed her gently back did she surface, breathless, unwilling.

She didn’t retreat far. She settled flat against his chest like a cat that had located the world’s one heat source, drew a long, greedy breath of him, and felt her mouth curve, helpless and happy.

“Oppa. You know what?” Her voice against his sternum was light and utterly serious at once. “I’ve been obeying you. All this time.”

His hand moved slowly on her back. He said nothing.

“I got my driver’s license.” The inventory began, pride creeping in at the edges. “At dinners, I stopped just agreeing with whatever everyone orders — I pick a dish I actually want. When work asks for something unreasonable, I say no now. I don’t just swallow it and hurt anymore.”

A pause.

“And you know what I found out? People don’t like me MORE when I suffer quietly. And they don’t hate me for a fair no. Saying what I don’t want — it isn’t hard at all. It actually feels — amazing.”

“Also…” Quieter now. “The pills. I don’t lean on them anymore. My head’s so much lighter. I sleep through the night.”

His hand stopped, then patted twice, gently. “You’ve done so well, Sulli-yah. You’ve grown — and you’ve been braver than anyone knows.”

He could hear it under the words: not just changed behavior — a power supply coming back online, room by room.

The praise made her glow. Her arms tightened around him as if to seam herself to his ribs. Then she lifted her head. In the blackness neither could see the other’s face — but she knew he was listening with everything.

“Oppa. I have so many things I want to do now.” Her voice filled with lit windows. “I want to travel everywhere with my boyfriend. Decorate a little home that’s only ours. See the whole sky of stars from a grassland. Get a tattoo that means something, together. Go camping and whisper nonsense around a fire. Dive and see the corals and the fish. And someday — the very end of the world, and the auroras.”

She let the wishes hang in the dark a moment, glittering.

Then she pushed herself up on her hands, aimed her eyes at where his face had to be, and asked it — hope and terror in the same four words.

“Oppa — will you go out with me? And do all of it — together?”

The dark had traded her sight for hearing, and hearing was merciless: he could feel her breath trembling against his face; could picture, in perfect resolution, the fawn-wide eyes and the fear of the answer crouched behind the hope.

The silence held for several seconds. Sea wind. Distant surf.

Then his voice, gentle as ever:

“Sulli-yah. Of course I’m willing — to do every one of those things with you.”

Her heart went off like a flare — he said yes, he SAID YES — the joy detonating so hard she nearly shouted, the next words already leaping — “Really?! Then let’s—”

And the back half of his sentence came down like ice water on an open flame, freezing everything it touched:

“But I already have a girlfriend.”

“…Mwo?”

The blood left her face all at once.

Motion, breath, thought — everything stopped. Her ears rang. The calm sentence kept cutting, over and over, through eardrum and heart alike.

Has. A girlfriend.

Who? Since when? Why didn’t I know ANY of it? Then these two months — the gentleness, the closeness that went so far past brother-and-sister — what WERE they?

Shock, disbelief, the specific pain of feeling deceived, and a loss too large to hold — they came in from every direction at once, black water over her head. She lay rigid where she was, body gone cold and stiff against him, and the arms that had been the warmest place in the world one minute ago now felt like a bed of needles.

The darkness was no longer a romantic hiding place. It was a pit with no bottom, and no air.

Oppa. You pulled me out of hell with your own hands. Are you going to push me back in with them too?
