Chapter One Hundred Forty-Four

One Album, and the Throne of a Top Soloist

Release week · November 2012 Everywhere the songs traveled twelve tracks, crossing borders without visas
◍ China · Jiangsu SUZHOU 2:00 a.m. · an office with the lights still on

Lin Wei closed the last spreadsheet and pressed her thumbs into her aching temples. Three straight weeks of pitch war at the ad agency — and this afternoon the client had chosen the rival’s proposal anyway. She sagged back into her chair and opened her music app, letting the daily recommendations autoplay into the empty office.

An acoustic guitar figure opened alone — eight spare seconds of fingerpicking, all space and sorrow. Then a voice came in, warm and faintly rough, singing in Korean.

She couldn’t understand a single word. It didn’t matter. The grief moving under the melody closed around her heart like an invisible hand.

“If you, if you—” The chorus broke the surface in English, and those two words she caught cleanly.

And all at once last week came flooding back: the boyfriend’s perfectly calm face as he explained their personalities didn’t fit; her own stubborn jaw holding the tears in; the midnight she moved out of the shared apartment alone.

When the song ended, her face was wet.

She tapped the track info. “If You” — Ji-ho, SUNYE.

“…Korean-American?” The nationality line in the artist profile caught her slightly off guard.

Some impulse she didn’t bother examining made her buy the entire album. Forty-nine yuan — nothing, really. She looped all twelve tracks to the end, and before sleep, posted to her feed: “Stumbled onto an album tonight. It’s as if someone set my private thoughts to music.” — the SUNYE cover attached.

By eight the next morning there were thirty-odd comments waiting underneath.

Moments · under Lin Wei’s post

#1 Wei-jie listens to Ji-ho too?? “Eyes, Nose, Lips” has been on repeat for me all week!

#2 “A Boy” took me straight back to university…

#3 This album exploded in Korea — didn’t expect this many people here to be listening too.

Only then did Lin Wei realize she was not the only one the album had found.

◍ China SHANGHAI a high-school classroom · the ten-minute break

In Year Two, Class Three, a knot of girls bent over one phone.

“Look, look — Ji-ho’s ‘Spring Day’ MV!” The ponytailed one was practically vibrating. “He’s SO handsome!”

“The girl is gorgeous too!!”

On the little screen, Kim Ji-won glanced back from a bicycle, sunlight breaking across her smile, while the vocal underneath asked, gently, how much longing had to fall like snow before spring would finally arrive.

“I want to learn it and sing it to my crush—” The short-haired one had gone pink to the ears.

“Then buy the album first! We are NOT losing to the Korean fans!”

The committee deliberated at length and passed its resolution: digital albums all around, plus one pooled physical copy for the permanent archive.

“My mom would never let me chase idols,” the one in glasses whispered, “but Ji-ho’s family came from here originally, sort of. I can say I’m supporting a heritage artist!”

It became their shared secret.

◍ China · Guangdong GUANGZHOU Metro Line 2 · 7:30 p.m. rush

Li Hao stood wedged into the evening crush, earphones on shuffle, watching the ad boards strobe past the window.

The piano intro of “Eyes, Nose, Lips” began, and he glanced down at the translated lyric line scrolling across his screen — a verse about a face the fingers still remember: the eyes, the nose, the lips, down to the smallest fingertip, all of it still touchable in memory.

He went very still.

His ex used to rest her palm against his cheek exactly like that. It made her feel safe, she’d said. Two years since the breakup — he would have sworn he’d forgotten the details.

The chorus arrived, the voice climbing into that ruined, still-hoping register — asking whether, even by accident, even for a single second, a person might be seen once more—

Li Hao ducked his head and wiped his eyes quickly in the packed train.

At his stop, the first thing he did was search the name and buy the album. The second was to open the comment section and write: “Thank you for teaching me that remembering isn’t a crime. It’s proof that I truly loved someone.”

Twenty thousand likes in three days.

◍ Thailand BANGKOK Siam Square · early evening

The mall was at full flood when the giant LED wall cut to black — then a beam of gold split it open and SUNYE surfaced in the light, followed by the “If You” highlights: IU looking back through the rain, him standing wordless inside it. Thai subtitles ran beneath: Ji-ho’s first solo album SUNYE — out now.

A university student named Natasha stopped mid-stride. She spoke no Korean. The music didn’t require it of her. She filmed the screen and posted it: “What IS this? It’s so beautiful?”

Ten minutes later, a Korean exchange-student friend replied: “That’s Ji-ho — the hottest solo act in Korea right now. The whole album is worth it. Trust me.”

That night she found it on Spotify and looped it into the small hours. The next day she joined the freshly founded Thai fanbase — two hundred members and climbing — learning the Korean lyrics together, organizing listening parties online.

◍ Singapore MARINA BAY a rooftop bar, after the meetings

Business concluded, Director Choi from Seoul and his Singaporean partner Mr. Wang settled in over drinks.

“Anything new out of Korea these days?” An idle question, stirred along with the ice.

Director Choi smiled and produced his phone.

“Let me recommend one song. Our whole office has spent the week arguing about this young man.”

He played “At Gwanghwamun.” The piano spilled out across the terrace, and the voice that followed moved like a night sea-wind — warm, with one cold edge folded inside it. Mr. Wang listened with his eyes closed, one finger keeping slow time against the table. When it ended, he opened them.

“This singer… is not a typical K-pop idol.”

“No,” Choi agreed. “He’s a singer-songwriter — wrote and produced the whole album himself. And he’s American-raised. Korean-American, building his career in Seoul.”

“American?” A flicker of real surprise.

“Trained three months in Korea and debuted. A genius, frankly.” Choi pulled up the sales chart. “Look at Southeast Asia. On the Singapore chart, ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips’ has held the top three for five consecutive days.”

Mr. Wang turned it over for a moment.

“Our hotel’s anniversary gala is next month. Perhaps we invite him to perform. American upbringing, Korean training, international polish — the story sells itself.”

One week later, an invitation from Singapore arrived in the team inbox. The attached fee was extremely generous.

◍ Vietnam HANOI the Old Quarter · a small tailor’s shop

Nguyen Thi Thanh, twenty-four, worked her needle to whatever YouTube’s autoplay brought her — and today it brought the “Spring Day” MV. She understood none of the Korean. The beauty of Jeju needed no translation, and neither did the song’s tenderness toward time already gone. It felt like her memory of the childhood neighbors who had moved away long ago. Like her first love, who had left to work in Korea and never come back.

She set down the needle and thread, opened the comments, and typed in careful, effortful English: “I hear this song from Vietnam. I don’t understand the words, but I cried. Music is the language we all speak.”

Five thousand likes — and replies from everywhere. Heard it in Mexico; cried too. American listener here — it brought back my late grandmother. France reporting in: art has no borders.

For the first time in her life, Nguyen Thi Thanh felt connected to a world that far away.

The conference room went quiet.

“An American-born artist, debuted in Korea, singing Korean ballads — and pulling these numbers in the West…” The VP weighed it slowly. “There’s something universal in the music.”

“It’s emotional authenticity.” The A&R head shrugged. “I played the whole album. I don’t speak Korean. Didn’t need to — the raw emotion carries on its own. ‘Been Through,’ especially. That’s a man walking out of the dark, and you can hear every step.”

The director cued a video: Times Square, a fan flash mob — a few dozen kids of every complexion dancing to “A Boy,” a hand-lettered sign held overhead: SUNYE — The Light in All Languages.

“The fans self-organized. His company hasn’t spent a dollar over here. This is entirely organic.”

The VP made the call.

“Contact his team. Let’s talk endorsement.”

◍ United Kingdom LONDON an Underground passage · a busker’s pitch

James set up in his usual tunnel and tried the new piece — the guitar arrangement of “Eyes, Nose, Lips” he’d worked out the night before.

Three notes in, an Asian girl stopped dead and stared at him. As the melody unspooled, more and more commuters slowed their walk. When he finished, the girl came up and asked — Korean first, then English:

“You play Ji-ho’s songs?”

“I’ve only heard it three times — I was trying an arrangement.” He laughed, shaking his head. “The melody’s beautiful, isn’t it?”

“I’m an exchange student from Korea.” Her eyes had gone bright. “I did NOT expect to hear Ji-ho in the London Underground! Could you play it once more? I want to film it for SNS.”

He obliged. This time she filmed, humming the Korean melody softly under his guitar.

The video went up tagged #SongJiho and #LondonStreets. Ten thousand shares inside three hours — one of them carrying a verified reply:

Song Ji-ho · Official

@SongJiho · Twitter · reply

Honored to hear my music in the London Underground. Thank you, @JamesStreetMusic, for the beautiful rendition.

James woke the next morning to fifty thousand new subscribers.

◍ Canada TORONTO 1:00 a.m. · “The Insomniac’s Channel”

DJ Allison ran her late-night show the way she always did. Tonight’s theme: songs that remind you of someone.

A call came through — a young man’s voice.

“I’d like to request a song. For my first love, three years ago. Her name was Mia.”

“Which song?”

“It’s Korean — it’s called ‘If You.’ She probably won’t understand the words, but… the chorus asks whether it’s still not too late, whether two people could ever go back to what they were. I want her to know that question never expires.”

Allison searched the station library and found, to her delight, that broadcast rights for SUNYE had cleared that very week. “If You” flowed out into the small-hours airwaves.

“That was from SUNYE, by the Korean-American artist Ji-ho,” she said into the dark, between songs. “Sometimes music says the things we can’t.”

The show’s back-end data afterward: listener engagement up forty percent for the slot. Across social media, strangers traded their own “If You” stories. In the program log she wrote a single line: “Tonight I watched music cross language and borders as if they weren’t there.”

✦   ✦   ✦
1:00 a.m. · Seoul LOEN Entertainment — his studio the numbers, and a decision about money

One in the morning, and he was still combing through the global sales dashboards when the door opened on Ryu Dong-hwan and a tablet.

“Another record down. China digital sales just broke 700,000 — the biggest single market in the world for this album. Six Southeast Asian iTunes charts, still number one. And in the West — two songs inside the Billboard World Digital top 100.”

“It’s honestly a miracle. Ji-ho — you’re unbelievable.”

He laughed, and privately ran the audit. The quality was real; the Boost Card was quietly compounding it; and in the Chinese-speaking market, the story of a Korean-American kid from LA singing his heart out in Seoul traveled with its own tailwind. On the overseas boards, the album was outpacing Girls’ Generation and BIGBANG — the peninsula’s flagship teams. One album, and the throne of a top soloist was simply… his.

“Hyung — one more thing. Take on more endorsements for me. As many as you can vet. But the brands have to be clean ones.”

“Are you short on money?” A blink of genuine surprise.

“A little.” A nod. No elaboration.

The Wealth Robot still sat unstarted in the vault — deliberately. The plan had always been to wait for the year-end album settlement and then feed the machine every last won at once; no deposits allowed after ignition, so a bigger seed was everything. But wait too long, and the LOEN acquisition window would swing shut without him. So: between now and December, earn as if the deadline were real. Because it was.

“Fine. I’ll curate them properly.” Dong-hwan nodded, then clapped his shoulder. “Oh — this year’s MAMA. November 30th, the Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre.”

“You’re nominated for Best New Male Artist, Best Male Artist, Best Collaboration, and Song of the Year.”

“New Artist and Collaboration should be yours outright. The other two — nobody on this earth beats Psy this year.”

An award show. His stomach dropped half a floor. That meant IU, Taeyeon, Tiffany — and himself — all inside one venue. He hadn’t seen any of them since promotions began. And IU was still waiting on her answer. The situation had “hazardous” written across it in three languages.

“Right — I’ll drive you home. Music shows start Thursday. We’re going for first place.”

“No need, hyung. You’ve been up as long as I have — go rest. I’ll drive myself.”

“But—”

“Aigoo, no buts. You know my constitution better than anyone. I’m fine. Go home. Sleep.”

Dong-hwan studied the set of his jaw and finally surrendered, sighing as he handed over the keys.

“Then message me the moment you’re home. And drive slowly — you’ve slept eight hours TOTAL in two days.”

“Understood, mother.” He grinned and took the keys.

With the Idol Aura running its repairs every night, short sleep simply bounced off him. His body felt annoyingly excellent.

✦   ✦   ✦

Half past one, and Seoul was still wide awake — bars roaring, pocha tents glowing along the sidewalks. Coffee by day, alcohol by night: the peninsula’s eternal liturgy.

He took the Cheongdam Bridge with the Han flowing black and silent beneath, touched the car stereo, and let “As It Flows” murmur through the cabin.

The car rolled to a stop in a discreet lane near a certain apartment building. He took out his phone.

Joo-hyun 🌙 · 10 minutes ago
Are you there yet?
At the spot you said.
Wait for me.

Five minutes later a slim figure came out at a quick walk, wrapped in a long ivory puffer, hood pulled low — and failing entirely to disguise that particular cool-water presence.

He unlocked the doors, and Irene slipped into the back seat like a neat-footed cat, bringing in a breath of winter night and the faint scent of gardenia.

“Why so late?”

She pushed the hood back and her hair came down like water. Bare-faced, in the dim cabin, the face remained frankly unreasonable.

“Album data to close out. Overseas feedback to read.” He turned in his seat, studying her by what little light there was. “And you? I thought I told you to sleep first.”

She didn’t answer that. She reached out and laid her palm gently along his cheek.

“You’ve lost weight.” A small crease formed between her brows. “Isn’t Manager Ryu watching your meals at all?”

“I’ve been eating—” He caught her hand and pressed it flat against his face. “Just busy.”

The air went quiet for a few seconds. Only the stereo kept murmuring.

Then she leaned in, brought her other hand up to frame his face, and touched her forehead softly to his.

“Close your eyes.” Quietly. An order.

He obeyed.

Her breath crossed his lashes, the bridge of his nose — and stopped a single inch from his mouth. There, and not there.

“Irene—” Her name, hoarse. The name — not nuna.

“Hm?”

“I missed you.”

The last syllable was still in the air when her lips came down on his.

Soft at first — a question, a consolation. Then he answered it: hand closing at the nape of her neck, weight shifting, pressing her back against the door, deep and unhurried and starving all at once. The weeks of exhaustion and pressure and missing her all found the same exit at the same moment.

His grip was hard enough to ache, and she allowed all of it — opened to him, let him take what he needed. Her hands slid from his jaw to the back of his neck and locked there. The temperature in the small cabin climbed by quiet degrees.

He really was extraordinary, she thought, somewhere beneath the heat. A first album, and he had simply walked to the top of every chart. She believed him now — believed he could build the company before her debut, exactly as he’d promised. And with the dread about the future gone, something in her had finally settled. She still practiced hard, every day. She no longer practiced until one in the morning. A woman without fear makes different choices — and one of tonight’s choices was to be, for once, the one who moved first.

At last he drew back a little, forehead resting against hers, breath rough. In the low light her lips were kissed crimson and wet, her eyes fogged bright — beautiful enough to hurt.

“Right — I brought you a present.” He produced a paper bag.

She opened it while he angled his phone light for her.

A pad of letter stationery. And three sealed envelopes.

“These are—?”

“One I wrote in China, when I missed you.”

“One I wrote after I came back — when there was no time to see you, and I minded that more than I let on.”

“And one I wrote just now. On the way here.”

“The stationery is yours — for writing back to me.”

“Ji-ho—” Her voice had gone unsteady.

“Joo-hyun.” Her full name, said carefully, like something set down on velvet. “In a handwritten letter, we pawn a small piece of our life to time, pen-tip first. Everything in our world moves fast now. This is the slowness I’m keeping — deliberately, specifically — for you. Some words only take root when they land on paper. The page always runs out before the feeling does.”

Her tears came down with no warning at all.

Because Irene was, beneath all that winter-water composure, a hopelessly literary creature. She collected journals. She ironed clothes to clear her head. She photographed landscapes — the moon above everything. She drew; she watched films; she copied out sentences that struck her and kept them like pressed flowers. And the gift she had wanted most, her whole life, without ever telling a single person — was a handwritten letter. He was the busiest man she knew. He had written her three.

The tears would not stop. He wiped at them, alarmed.

“Why are you crying? Did I say something wrong?”

“No—” She shook her head, which only made it worse. “I love this gift.”

“And— seeing you this tired hurts. And seeing you win makes me so happy. Both at once. It’s very confusing—”

Something in his chest went soft and structural at the same time, and he pulled the whole of her into his arms. Her face found the hollow of his shoulder; warm tears soaked through the wool of his sweater.

“Don’t cry.” He smoothed her long hair down, patient, unhurried. “My body’s fine — I promise. The album worked. Once the music-show stages are done, I’ll give you more of my time. Properly.”

“Really?” She looked up, eyes rimmed red.

“Really.” He nodded, grave — and then smiled. “But before that—”

He kissed her again, and this one was all gentleness. She wound her arms around his neck and answered in kind — two young people in a small dark car, trading warmth and breath while the city went on without them.

A long while later they separated by inches. She stayed against his shoulder, fingers idly working the yarn of his sweater.

“I bought the album,” she said suddenly.

“Hm?”

“Ten copies.” There was unmistakable smugness in it.

He laughed out loud.

“Miss Irene. You couldn’t simply have asked me? I have an entire BOX.”

“That’s different.” Perfectly serious. “Support as a fan, and care as a girlfriend, are two separate line items.”

His heart folded completely. He kissed the crown of her head.

“Oh — right.” She remembered something. “‘As It Flows’ is getting such a good response. I have a fan club now, you know.”

“I know.” He smiled. “I told you. That face alone is a debut.”

“It really is that beautiful.”

She ducked her head, shy, and was quiet for a few seconds.

“You… in my heart, you’re the most handsome one. Also.”

The most beautiful girl of a generation, curled against his chest, saying molten things in that cool, clear voice. There are worse ways to close out a release week.

He tipped her chin up and caught her soft mouth again, and she answered without reservation.

He smells so good. Better than I do — and I’m the girl. Kissing him is… very comfortable. Objectively speaking.

A long time later they parted, and she raised one hand, fingertip tracing the line of his brow, the corner of his eye. He captured the hand and kissed it.

“What are you thinking about?”

“That if someday… something forced us apart.” Her voice was very small. “How much that would hurt.”

He took her hand properly, fingers interlacing, and closed them tight.

“It won’t happen.” Flat certainty. “We won’t be separated. Whatever comes — I’m not letting go.”

She looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled — the smile breaking open across the not-yet-dry face like weather clearing after rain.

“I believe you.”

✦   ✦   ✦

“You should go up.” Softly. “Sleep early tonight.”

She nodded — and did not let go. They held on a while longer in the quiet, and finally she straightened and began setting his collar to rights with small proprietary tugs, her voice recovering its usual cool-edged tenderness.

“Sleep properly when you get home. I learned samgyetang. On your rest day — I’ll make you a pot.”

“Okay.”

“No staying up over the dashboards.”

“Okay.”

“Message me every day. Even if it’s only to say goodnight.”

“Okay.”

She regarded this run of perfect obedience and lost the fight against her own smile.

“Why are you being so well-behaved?”

“Because you’re the one saying it.” Just as soft. Twice as warm.

She kissed his cheek one last time, pulled the hood up, and pushed the door open. Cold air poured in — and his hand shot out and caught hers.

“Joo-hyun.”

“Mm?”

His eyes were very bright in the dark. Bright as starlight.

“I love you.”

She went still. Her eyes reddened all over again — but she nodded, hard, and squeezed his hand in return.

“I love you too.”

The door shut. She crossed to the building at a quick walk, and he watched until the lobby swallowed her before he started the engine.

The stereo had looped its way around to “If You.” But the sad lines about parting sounded different now, somehow. When someone is waiting for you, every hardship acquires a meaning. When you are trying to walk toward someone, you have no choice but to become stronger.

He pulled out into streets just beginning to pale. His phone buzzed.

Joo-hyun 🌙
Tell me when you’re home safe.
Also — I know exactly how hard you were holding back just now.
Thank you.

He smiled at the screen.

Every one of them got the real thing. That was the only rule he had left — and the only one he intended to keep.
