Chapter Fifty-Three

One Song to Stun Them All!

Ji-ho caught the arched eyebrows from across the hall and grinned right back. Wait for it, Irene. Your turn to be surprised is coming.

Wendy had tested in the morning session — holding steady at B — and had absolutely no business being here now. She materialized at Joo-hyun’s side anyway.

“Eonni! Congratulations!”

“Your evaluation was this morning. What are you doing back here?”

“For Ji-ho, OBVIOUSLY.” Her eyes locked onto the target across the hall. “Eonni, I’ve got it all planned. There’s no WAY he makes A — so the second his evaluation ends, I’m going to be the very first person to laugh at him. And then: coffee-runner. Half a month. Every single day.”

The other performances rolled past him unwatched. Ji-ho had gone inward — breath, pulse, focus — tuning himself like an instrument for the slot at the end of the list.

✦   ✦   ✦

“Final trainee — Ji-ho.”

Every head in the hall turned at once. The trainee who saved Sulli — the whole building had been waiting a month to see what he actually had.

He drew the shades and the white cap from his bag and walked up unhurried. The bow hit ninety degrees.

“Hello, judges. I’m Ji-ho.”

He straightened.

“I’ll be performing an original composition. It’s called ‘A Boy.’”

An ORIGINAL? The One sat bolt upright, narrow eyes widening a millimeter. Since when does my disciple write? Across the hall, Joo-hyun’s and Seung-wan’s eyes had gone perfectly round. Original songs weren’t rare at evaluations — but nobody, NOBODY, had heard a word about Ji-ho composing.

He ignored the ripple entirely. Cap on. Shades on. Feet set. And as the beat dropped and his body found it, the transformation ran head to toe — the polite, mild trainee dissolving into something loose-jointed, streetwise, and faintly dangerous.

Performance

— the intro lands: restrained drums under hazy, layered electronics — and like a hand closing around every ear in the hall, the room goes silent — At the judges’ table, Yoo Young-jin’s posture changes completely. The casual slouch is gone. This kid is interesting.

Under the outfit’s full effect, Ji-ho’s rap delivery is running at genuine professional grade — and rap lives or dies on the pocket. The 4/4 drums stress beats one and three; he plants every lyric’s weight precisely on the downbeats, and each landing hits like a fist on a table.

The opening verses: a boy awake past midnight, fighting a body worn down to the wire, filling page after page with lyrics because the ideas will not stop coming. Flow is the soul of the form, and his refuses monotony — accelerating, dragging, cutting to silence. The fast passages land clean and unslurred; the slow lines stretch their tails deliberately; the sudden stops yank the whole room forward in their seats. Then the autobiography: landing here at eighteen and never once stopping. Confidence too big for his station, carried as a weapon. The knowledge that every climb has a descent waiting on the other side. He works the stage now — hand gestures cutting on the rhythm, short prowling walks, a hard stomp and head-snap on the heavy beats, hammering the pulse into the crowd. Half the trainee section is already moving without realizing it. Boy — don’t forget the one who fought for this. Those eyes that used to burn. Shout that voice at the world — shine like the brightest star.

Every line was molten sincerity from the bottom of a dreamer’s chest — trainee life’s vagabond grind and stubborn faith, rendered exact. And the hall it landed on was full of the only audience on earth that could feel it in the bone.

Who here wasn’t spending their youth? Who here wasn’t bleeding for it?

Irene — the “old” nineteen-year-old recruit who’d ground through three years of nights as the last one out of the building, who’d cried where no one could see and converted the pain into a legend’s résumé. Only she knew the full price of it.

EJAE — twelve years a trainee, contemporaries with Super Junior and Girls’ Generation, never once debuted under SM — and still walking the road, still refusing to put it down.

Seulgi — in the building since 2007, “little Taeyeon” by 2008 — now nursing ruined vocal cords and rebuilding herself as a dancer from the ground up. Two more years of unseen sweat stood between her and an August 2014 debut with Red Velvet, and a future as one of her generation’s finest dancers.

There is an old saying: one minute on the stage costs ten years below it. The trainee system had industrialized that arithmetic — and this song was its ledger, read aloud.

To a civilian ear, “A Boy” was a sharp hook over a clean beat. To every trainee in this hall, it was a documentary of their own youth.

Performance

His body has gone fully loose now — easy swings, offhand turns, power arriving in controlled bursts with that particular delinquent elegance. Somewhere in the second verse he has stopped performing for judges at all; the evaluation has ceased to exist. There is only the stage. The defiant verses: I’m Ji-ho — say what you want. Too old. Can’t dance. Say I got lucky — that saving her was a coincidence and coincidence is all I am. Then trade one day of your life for mine: at the company by eight, last one out every night, leaving at dawn with nothing but the loneliness getting heavier — a stranger in a strange land, grinding alone. Because I know exactly how bright that stage is.
