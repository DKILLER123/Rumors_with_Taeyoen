Chapter Two Hundred Sixty-Six

Sulli, I Think I Understand You Now

Gwangju Metropolitan City · old residential district halmeoni’s third-floor apartment

Ji-ho opened the trunk and gathered the gifts Dong-hwan had prepared: fruit, carefully selected health products, practical household goods, and several boxes of premium Korean beef.

Hara watched him balance the packages and felt her uncertainty deepen.

He had taken the visit seriously.

On the third floor, she stopped outside apartment 302 and drew a long breath.

“Remember. This is one visit.”

“One visit,” Ji-ho agreed.

Hara knocked.

“Coming.”

Quick footsteps approached. The door opened on an elderly woman with silver hair and a generous smile.

“Hara-ah! Why didn’t you tell me you were coming?”

Her grandmother noticed the tall man behind her and stopped.

She looked at Ji-ho, then at the packages, then back to her granddaughter.

Hara’s prepared explanation vanished.

“Halmeoni… this is Song Ji-ho.”

Ji-ho bowed.

“Annyeong haseyo, halmeonim. I’m someone Hara is getting to know seriously.”

The older woman’s eyes widened.

“The Song Ji-ho from television?”

“Ne.”

“Aigoo!”

Her surprise became immediate hospitality.

“Come inside. Hara, you should have warned me. I haven’t prepared anything.”

“Halmeoni, you don’t need to—”

“Nonsense. Someone important comes to the house for the first time and you expect me to do nothing?”

She drew Ji-ho inside and studied him with open approval.

“You’re even more handsome than on television. I said it the first time I saw you perform—whoever ends up beside this young man will be fortunate.”

She looked meaningfully at Hara.

“My granddaughter has excellent eyes.”

Hara’s face burned.

Ji-ho placed the gifts near the wall.

“I didn’t know what you preferred, so I brought a few practical things.”

“Why spend so much? Coming is enough.”

“Please accept them as respect, not obligation.”

Hara’s grandmother pulled him toward the sitting area and began asking questions. Ji-ho answered patiently—work, music, food, family, whether he slept enough, and whether television people truly ate as little as they claimed.

He complimented her health and asked about her cooking. Within minutes, she was laughing hard enough to cover her mouth.

Hara sat nearby and watched warmth return to a face that had carried too much worry for too many years.

Halmeoni had not looked this delighted in a long time.

“May I see Hara’s room?” Ji-ho asked eventually.

“Of course. Hara-ah, show him.”

Hara stood with renewed embarrassment and led him down the short hall.

✦ ✦ ✦

Her room was small and meticulously kept. KARA posters covered part of one wall. Books and framed photographs occupied the desk. A stuffed rabbit rested beside the pillow.

Ji-ho moved toward the photographs.

HARA · A CHILDHOOD IN FRAMES

A little girl with twin braids, smiling in a school uniform.

Hara and her older brother making faces at the camera.

Small arms around halmeoni’s neck, one cheek pressed against hers.

No photograph includes both parents.

He picked up the last one. A teenager, maybe fourteen or fifteen, wearing clothes that were several styles at once, standing in what was unmistakably a homemade photo studio — a sheet pinned to a wall, a desk lamp doing its best — smiling at the camera with the careful, self-conscious posture of someone who had practiced the smile in a mirror and was still not sure it worked.

“This is?”

Ha-ra leaned in, looked, and a small laugh escaped.

“That was when I was modeling for an online shop. Before debut. I was trying to earn some money for the house.”

He looked at her.

“Before debut, I auditioned for SM.” Her voice was level, the way people get when the story no longer hurts but the memory of the hurt is still filed somewhere precise. “Didn’t make it. Then I tried JYP — the judges didn’t even look at me. Finally DSP took me in.”

She smiled.

“So I always thought I was lucky. Rejected everywhere, but I still debuted.”

Song Ji-ho set the frame down.

“Do you know what I went through before debut?”

She shook her head.

“I was eight when my father disappeared. No note, no call.” He leaned against the desk, tone flat, as if recounting weather. “My mother kept me going for a few more years. Then she left too. From middle school on, I was alone.”

Ha-ra went still.

“I liked music. My voice was all right. So I started singing in bars — earning.” He paused. “Saved for two years. At eighteen, I crossed the Pacific alone. Came to the Peninsula to try out as a trainee.”

“But I was too old. A foreigner. Zero dance background. Singing entirely self-taught. I auditioned thirteen times. Failed thirteen times.”

“Thirteen?” Her eyes went wide.

“Thirteen.” A small smile. “But I didn’t stop. The fourteenth time, SM finally took me. And here we are.”

Goo Ha-ra looked at him, and something behind her ribs took a direct hit.

He has the same past. Abandoned. Alone. Door after door closing in his face. And he didn’t stop.

Same as me.

No — harder. I had Grandmother. I had my brother. He had no one.

“Song Ji-ho…” she said, very quietly.

He looked at her.

Her eyes were wet. Not crying — not yet — but the surface was trembling, holding.

He reached out and cupped her face in both hands.

She didn’t turn away. She closed her eyes.

He kissed her. Gently.

Her arms came up, slowly, and locked around his waist. The two of them stood in the small room, between the childhood photographs and the KARA poster and the worn-out rabbit on the pillow, and something between them rearranged — quietly, without announcement.

The door swung open.

“Ha-ra-ah, I just remembered there’s still fruit in the—”

Grandmother’s voice stopped dead.

They sprang apart. Ha-ra’s face achieved a shade of red that could have been used as a hazard signal.

Grandmother stood in the doorway for one full second. Then her face did something extraordinarily specific: the aigomoji — the auntie smile — the smile that says I saw everything and I approve of every single thing I saw.

“Aigoo, I didn’t see anything!” She flapped her hands, backing out. “You two carry on, carry on! I’ll call you when the food’s ready!”

The door closed with the decisive finality of someone who had seen exactly what she came to see.

Ha-ra covered her face with both hands.

“Ahhh— this is all YOUR fault!”

Song Ji-ho laughed — he couldn’t help it.

“Still laughing!” She hammered his chest. “Grandmother is going to tease me about this for weeks!”

“She won’t.” He caught her fists. “She’s too happy.”

“How do you know?!”

“That smile—” He reproduced Grandmother’s expression with uncanny accuracy. “— that was the ‘my granddaughter is amazing’ smile. Specifically.”

She had to laugh. She hit him again, softer.

✦ ✦ ✦

At dinner, Grandmother loaded his bowl to the structural limit.

“Eat more! This is Ha-ra’s favorite from when she was small!”

“This one too, try this!”

“Aigoo, you’re too thin — eat!”

The bowl was a mountain. He didn’t refuse a single addition. He ate everything she put in front of him and asked how each dish was made, and Grandmother — who had been cooking for one for years — began offering to teach him the braised beef recipe, and the soy-pickled radish, and the particular way she cut the spring onion so it curled.

“Grandmother’s cooking is the best I’ve had. Better than any restaurant.”

“Really?! Hahaha — eat more, eat more!”

Ha-ra watched and couldn’t stop smiling. Grandmother’s eyes were two crescents. She had not been this happy in a long, long time.

After dinner, Grandmother pulled Song Ji-ho into the kind of conversation that covers family, work, future plans, and the precise temperature at which rice should be served — and he sat there for all of it, answering with the same patience he’d brought to the scallions.

The sun went down.

“It’s so late…” Grandmother looked at Ha-ra, testing. “Should Song Ji-ho stay tonight?”

Ha-ra’s face went pink. Before she could answer:

“I’d be grateful, Grandmother.”

“Not at all, not at all!” She was already moving. “Ha-ra’s room — I’ll bring an extra blanket!”

Ha-ra looked at him, voice barely there.

“You agreed fast enough.”

“Unhappy about it?”

She turned her face away and said nothing. The tips of her ears answered for her.

Night Ha-ra’s Room · Apartment 302 one small bed, one old rabbit, one full heart

Washed, changed, the room dim. Ha-ra sat on the edge of her childhood bed, every limb reporting a different malfunction. Her room. Her bed. And now — one more body in it.

Song Ji-ho came out of the bathroom, sat beside her.

“Nervous?”

“…No.”

Liar.

He put an arm around her shoulders and drew her in. She went rigid for one second — then, slowly, like ice cream on a warm day, went soft.

He smelled clean. Body wash and something underneath that was just him.

He kissed her forehead. Then her eyes. Then the tip of her nose. Then her mouth.

Her arms went around his neck. His hand traced her back through the thin cotton of her sleep shirt, and she trembled — not from nerves this time, but from the sheer thermal fact of being held properly, after years of not being.

The corners that were never filled are filling.

When he moved to deepen things, she caught his hand, flushed, and whispered:

“Don’t — Grandmother is next door.”

“When we get back to Seoul, then.”

“You just need to be quiet.”

His breath, warm, against the small shell of her ear — and she shivered, hard, because her mind, traitor that it was, had already retrieved the morning’s footage: that body, that stamina, the entire unreasonable catalog. Her face went hot. She looked at the desk, away from him, because looking at him was not survivable.

“I can’t — I won’t be able to—”

Quiet. As if.

He lifted her chin with one finger, gently, until she was looking at him — at this distance, at this angle, at a face that had no bad side and no good side because every side was the good side.

“I’ll be gentle.”

Her eyes, already wet, went a shade wetter. Not from sadness. From the specific vertigo of being looked at like that, by someone who looked like that, in a room where you grew up.

“You… you’re really going to—”

“Who told you to be this beautiful?”

She looked away. Her face could have guided ships.

“Do you… really think I’m beautiful?”

“Ask ten people. Eleven would say yes.”

“Why eleven?”

“Because I’m counting myself twice.”

“Mm—” She bit her lip. “Stop saying things like that—”

I can’t hold out much longer.

She rolled over, presenting her back to him, the universal signal of a woman who has lost a negotiation and is hoping to salvage the appearance of resistance.

“I’m going to sleep.”

He curled behind her, chin on her shoulder, and breathed one warm breath against her ear.

“I can’t sleep.”

She sat up, fast.

“That’s your problem.”

He lay back, looking up at her with the lazy satisfaction of someone who had already won and was now simply enjoying the production.

“I’m a guest. If you won’t take care of me, I’ll go tell Grandmother. ‘Grandmother, she won’t take care of me.’”

She pictured Grandmother’s reaction to that complaint — and realized, with horror, that Grandmother might very well side with him.

“You—! You rogue!!”

“Thank you.” He opened both arms. “Come here.”

She hesitated. She debated. She gave in — slowly, in small increments, as if crossing a river one cautious step at a time — and lay down in his arms.

The bed was small. She was soft. She smelled good. He pulled the blanket over both of them.

After a moment, she shifted, pressing backward — away from something.

“What are you moving away from?”

“—Hot.”

“Hot? I’ll help you with that.” His fingers found the buttons of her shirt. “Less fabric, less heat.”

“You— you did that on purpose!”

“Hm — lace. I see. So you planned this.”

“I did NOT — I’m not — that’s not—”

“I like it.”

She stopped talking. Her eyes, dark and wet, held his.

“You said you’d be gentle.”

“Mm.”

The lamp went out. The room went dark. The night deepened.

After · Spoken Into the Dark

“…Ha-ra. When your brother finishes his service, send him to Seoul. I’ll find him work.”

“Mm…”

“And that apartment of yours — sell it.”

“I don’t like it. I’ll buy you a new one.”

“You don’t… need to do these things.”

“I said I’d give you a stable life. I said I’d take care of you.”

“Song Ji-ho!!”

She held him, hard, with everything she had.

Song Ji-ho, in earnest, was a man no woman could refuse. Even knowing about the others — even knowing the cost of admission — the warmth was real, and moths do not negotiate with fire. They just go.

Sulli… I think I understand you now.

In the dark, in the small room, in the bed where she’d grown up, Goo Ha-ra lay with her face against his chest and listened to his heartbeat and understood, at last, why Choi Jin-ri and Lee Ji-eun had walked into the same fire and stayed.

Because the fire was warm. And it knew your name. And it promised not to leave.
