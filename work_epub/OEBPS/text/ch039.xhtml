Chapter Thirty-Nine

The Pleaser

Through the door came a girl’s voice humming some nameless tune. Ji-ho leaned on the corridor wall and waited.

Fifteen minutes — not two — and the door opened again.

She stepped out light-footed in an ivory cotton-linen dress falling to her ankles, wide hem swaying with each step, hand-embroidered green vines ghosting in and out of the corridor light. A pale green cardigan hung loose off her shoulders; a straw bag rode her elbow; her hair was woven into one soft, careless braid over one shoulder, a few loose strands playing at her cheek and making the small face smaller.

And the face itself: a whisper of base lending her skin its own light back, peach blush swept across the apples of her cheeks like the season’s first bloom, a soft bean-rose lip that warmed her whole complexion. Clean. Sweet. Quietly devastating.

“Sorry, oppa — you waited forever, didn’t you?” Fingers pinching nervously at the hem, eyes not quite landing on his.

He straightened off the wall and gave her a serious, unhurried once-over before answering with plain honesty.

“Long wait. Worth every minute of it.”

Her eyes curved into crescents; the smile that broke out was pure undiluted sweetness. She armed herself with mask and sunglasses, then looked up.

“What do you want to eat, oppa?”

“Tonight you’re the guide. I follow orders.”

They moved through the hushed corridor, footsteps tapping bright on the tile. In the elevator she studied him sidelong: the straight carriage of him, the profile cut clean under the ceiling light — a person who somehow lit a steel box just by standing still in it.

Ding—

Ground floor. Outside the hospital doors the night wind came soft against their faces, carrying early-summer coolness and some faint drift of flowers. Above Seoul, improbably, a scatter of stars.

Sulli pulled in a long breath — her first taste of free air since admission — and it went to her head like wine. She turned to him, eyes bright in the dark.

“Oppa, let’s take a taxi. The place is a little far but the food is — I PROMISE you won’t regret it.”

They flagged a cab and dissolved into the river of taillights.

✦   ✦   ✦

Deep in a side alley of Gangnam, an unremarkable barbecue house glowed lamplight-yellow. In a second-floor private room, the grill hissed and crackled; beef smoke wove through the steam off seaweed soup until the whole room smelled like an argument for being alive.

Sulli sniffed — surreptitiously, she thought — eyes welded to the meat changing color on the grate, throat moving on its own. She hadn’t eaten properly in a full day; hospital trays were a crime against appetite.

“Starving, aren’t you?”

His voice tugged her back. He was working the tongs with a veteran’s economy, fat sizzling up fragrant where it dripped. He lifted a perfectly finished piece and set it on her plate.

“This one first. The timing’s exactly right.”

“Thank you, oppa!” Her eyes went to crescents again — and then her chopsticks stopped midair. Her gaze flicked, involuntary, to his still-empty plate. “Oppa, you… you’ve been grilling this whole time. You take this one. You first.”

“Weren’t you hungry? Don’t wait on me.”

“It’s fine, I can really wait—”

Her stomach growled again, loud enough to testify in court. Scarlet to the hairline, she clamped both hands over it, eyes darting away like a child caught stealing.

He laughed under his breath, laid more meat across the grill, and nudged the plate closer to her.

“Eat. It’s worse cold.”

This time she obeyed — carefully, almost ceremonially. The beef came apart tender on her tongue, sweet with the house sauce, and her eyes drifted shut entirely without permission. It was the first thing that had tasted like happiness since the hospital doors closed on her. Absurdly, overwhelmingly real.

“It’s really… so good.” Almost reverent.

The satisfaction survived approximately four seconds. Then she registered the arrangement of the table — his plate empty, hers full, him still working the grill on her behalf — and something in her seized like a caught gear.

“Oppa, let ME!” She was reaching before she finished the sentence, lifting the tongs out of his hand. “You’ve been doing it forever, you must be exhausted, it’s my turn—”

The movements came fast, flustered, nearly frantic — as if being served while eating were a sin requiring immediate expiation. Before he could respond she was flipping meat with studied competence, and the first finished batch went — all of it, every piece — directly onto his plate.

“Try it, oppa! How’s my grilling?” Chin on both hands, eyes locked on him unblinking — expectation, and underneath it a thread of genuine tension. A student awaiting the red pen.

He tasted a piece. Nodded with real approval.

“It’s excellent.”

She lit up like a struck match — the whole girl, instantly incandescent.

But she still didn’t touch her own food. Instead she rose, ladled out a steaming bowl of seaweed soup, and set it before him with both hands and an eagerness that was starting to ache to watch.

“Their seaweed soup is famous, oppa — the chef says it’s a family recipe — you HAVE to try it.”

He did. The seaweed’s brightness folded into the beef broth’s depth; the seasoning sat exactly where it should. He finished the bowl in a few long swallows.

“It’s delicious.”

The bowl had barely touched the table before a napkin materialized at his hand. She was on her feet again, tissue box in hand, hovering.

“You’ve got a little, at the corner—” Too shy to wipe it herself; she pressed the napkin into his hand instead and was already turning back toward the soup pot. “Another bowl? I’ll get—”

“Sulli-yah.”

She turned back slowly, bowl in hand, question marks in her eyes.

“Weren’t you the hungry one? So why have you spent this entire meal taking care of me?”

She went still. The bowl hung in the air. Her lips moved; her throat produced nothing. Several seconds passed before she assembled a smile that didn’t fit right.

“Because… because you saved me, oppa. And… you came to see me. I have to— I should do SOMETHING—”

Her fingers worried the rim of the bowl. Her gaze had gone somewhere over his shoulder, unable to land.

And in that moment he recognized it — recognized her — the desperate carefulness, the terror that being insufficiently good, insufficiently useful, insufficiently sweet would get her left behind. Again. He thought of the figure at the hospital window. Thin as paper. Sealed off from the whole world.

“Sulli.” He kept his voice low and even. “Being cared for isn’t a debt you have to repay. You deserve kindness because you’re you — not because of what you do for anyone.”

She set the bowl down slowly and folded her hands in her lap, fingers winding tight enough to whiten.

“I… I know.” No conviction in it at all — a phrase for self-soothing, worn smooth with use. “But Mom always said — girls have to be agreeable. The reason I got into SM, the reason I debuted — it’s because I’m ‘obedient.’ Because I read moods well. If I’m not thoughtful enough, not mature enough… people stop liking me…”

Her voice shrank word by word until it was nearly air:

“I’m just… used to it now.”

Field Notes

A childhood starved of love; a mother whose first instruction upon delivering her daughter to the company was please them. Please the executives. Please everyone. Year over year, the habit calcifies into architecture: others first, always; the self’s worth outsourced to external approval, because the internal verdict — I am not enough — was rendered long ago.

Hyper-attunement to every face, every tone, every shift in the air. Every negative emotion in the room instantly self-attributed, instantly compensated for with more pleasing. Approval purchased with compliance; safety purchased with surrender.

The pleaser hacks a survival path through the thorns by bowing to every branch — and forgets, somewhere along the way, that she was allowed to walk the sunlit road upright.

She’d run out of words and sat with her head bowed, folded small.

“Sulli-yah.”

She looked up. He had lifted a perfectly grilled piece of beef — and it went to neither plate. He held it out, directly to her lips.

“Habits can be rewritten. For example: right now. Open.”

Her eyes went wide. Full system stall.

“Ahh—” He demonstrated, patient, encouragement all over his face.

A wavering beat — then, obediently, she opened her mouth. The beef arrived; he withdrew the chopsticks and, only then, served himself.

“See how that works?” he said, chewing. “I can take care of you. You can let me. It doesn’t mean you’re falling short, and it doesn’t mean you owe me. It’s just…” He considered. “…how friends share a table.”

She chewed slowly, eyes fixed on the small flame dancing under the grill. Friends. Sharing a table. Familiar words; foreign language. In every friendship she’d ever kept, she’d been the mood-manager, the one who memorized everyone’s preferences, the first to apologize in every fight. So terrified of losing people. So why does keeping them make me this tired?

“I…” She looked up; the rims of her eyes had gone red. “The truth is I’m scared. All the time.”

“Of what?”

“That if I get something wrong — say something wrong — everyone will leave.” Her voice began to shake. “The way… the way my dad did.”

The sentence came out barely above a whisper, and cost her everything in it.

He didn’t push. He just listened.

“I was seven when he left.” Her voice had gone flat in the way that is worse than crying. “Before he walked out, he told me: ‘Jin-ri-yah. Be good, listen to your mom, be mature, work hard and earn money — and then Daddy will come back to see you.’”

The smile she produced was more wound than expression.

“I did everything. I got into SM. I debuted. I made so much money… He never came back. I’m in a hospital right now, and he hasn’t called. Not once.”

The grill crackled into the silence.

“Eventually I figured it out,” she said, very quietly. “Some people don’t leave because you weren’t good enough. They leave because they just… don’t care.”

She said it with such perfect calm that something in Ji-ho’s chest went down like a stone. And the other half of the picture he already knew: the mother who’d delivered a small child to an agency under the banner of chasing dreams and operated her like an asset ever since — present for the earnings, absent for everything else, first out the door at the first stumble.

“Sulli-yah.” He began laying grilled meat onto her plate, piece after piece, a small mountain, no refusals accepted. “Starting now, I want you to try one thing.”

“What?”

“Every day, do one small thing purely for yourself. Order the dish YOU actually want — not the one everyone else likes. When you’re tired, say ‘I need to rest’ — not ‘I’m fine.’ And for instance—”

He pointed at the mountain on her plate.

“—right now: eat all of that. Every piece. Without once checking whether I’ve had enough. Can you do that?”

She looked at the fragrant pile, then at the steady warmth of his eyes. A long moment. Then a small nod — and she picked up her chopsticks, and for the first time all night, fed herself first. Chew. Swallow. Another piece. Chew. Swallow.

Stiff at first — nearly mechanical. By the third piece, something unlocked; she sighed, deeply content, eyes curving shut.

“It’s really… so good.”

The same words as before. But the careful sweetness was gone from them — and what remained was hers alone: plain, unperformed pleasure.

He smiled, and finally served himself properly, and the two of them ate in easy quiet, the grill’s hiss the only soundtrack either of them needed.
