Chapter One Hundred Twenty-Seven

The Alps, with Yoon-a

Departure Day Incheon Airport — VIP Lounge an unexpected co-star

In the VIP lounge, Ji-ho found someone he had not been briefed to expect.

“Yoon-a nuna? What are you doing here?”

Yoon-a looked up from her phone wearing an expression that was not quite a smile.

“What — planning to steal MY half of the contract too?”

“Steal? The brand came knocking. Was I supposed to refuse?” He dropped into the seat beside her with zero ceremony. European endorsement fees, after all, were not the refusing kind.

“I’m just warning you.” She shrugged and pocketed the phone. “Careful — certain people have you on their lists now.”

“Let them list. I live my life. It changes nothing.”

His gaze drifted, uninvited, over her airport look: a pale-yellow minimalist dress against fair skin, hair soft at her neck — fresh and quietly devastating. She caught the glance, hitched her bag over her legs, and lifted her chin.

“What are you looking at?”

He laughed and shook his head. Wore it out to be seen; now deploying countermeasures. He offered a diplomatic exit instead.

“The shoot’s on Mont Blanc, isn’t it? Permanent snow. I was simply worried nuna might not survive the cold.”

“And THAT is where you reveal your inexperience.” Instantly, the sunbae posture assembled itself, and the lecture commenced.

Field Notes

Professor Im’s crash course in airport-fashion economics: the airport look matters. Fansite masters and fans stake out arrivals; appear at 10 AM and by lunch your outfit is dissected across every fan community and outlet. Those looks connect DIRECTLY to fashion opportunities and commercial value. Brands measure your selling power by same-item sell-through — one affordable tee, one niche bag, sold out after a single sighting, proves fan purchasing power and general-public pull, which attracts the next tier of offers. Wear a borrowed bracelet through arrivals and it’s free exposure for the brand; strong response and you’re on track for ambassador, even model. Fashion magazines rank airport looks; companies do PR to land those lists; trending tags get bought — “so-and-so’s hoodie SOLD OUT,” “such-and-such idol’s off-duty look flagged by stylists”—

— and above ALL: never, ever wear a competitor brand. Unless you enjoy paying penalty clauses.

Ji-ho listened with genuine attention. This was real tradecraft — SM trained it systematically; LOEN’s young management arm hadn’t gotten to half of it.

Encouraged by the attentive audience, she warmed further — today’s bracelet was a brand loan, this whole look was team-assembled — and, mid-lecture, went dry-mouthed, produced a thermos, and sipped carefully through a straw to protect her lip color.

Ji-ho’s eyes narrowed. He picked his moment.

“Nuna’s bought a fair number of those trending tags herself, then?”

Pfff— cough cough cough!

Water went everywhere. She doubled over, shoulders shaking, hand over her mouth, coughing until her eyes streamed. A reaction of this magnitude constituted, in legal terms, a confession.

He patted her back; a manager materialized with napkins. She wiped her mouth, mustered her deadliest glare, and ground out through her teeth:

“Song! Ji! HO!”

Two small fists — the famous “Powerful Yoon” fists, no joke at close range — hammered his shoulder. He caught the slim wrist and dropped his voice.

“Nuna. Public venue. Image.”

One sweep of the lounge — sparse, but eyes had turned. She inhaled through her nose.

“Let go.”

He let go. She turned away and checked her makeup in a compact — intact, mercifully — while filing the incident in a ledger already carrying one prior entry.

Ji-ho. The singing joke from before, STILL unavenged — and now this. Fine. The shoot is long. Cross Yoon-a, and you are a dead man walking.

✦   ✦   ✦

The journey was a marathon: Qatar Airways via Doha, landing in Geneva, then a minibus winding up to Chamonix on Mont Blanc’s southern flank — nearly eighteen hours door to door. The EIDER reception team had rooms ready; nobody managed a shower before collapsing.

The next day’s call time: 5 AM.

Mont Blanc’s dawn was bone-cold, every breath turning to frost. Ji-ho wore the newest cold-weather line — army-green windproof shell over a red tartan flannel, dark-grey waterproof trousers, technical boots. When the stylist fitted his goggles, he shivered despite himself.

“Cold?” Yoon-a’s voice arrived from the side.

She wore a blazing orange windbreaker that made her skin luminous, matched thermal leggings, a knit beanie — a single flame against all that white.

“A little,” he admitted.

She patted his shoulder with the gravity of a forty-year veteran.

“This is only the BEGINNING, rookie. Learn from your sunbaes. Persevere through hardship.”

He gave that exactly the “tch” it deserved.

First setup: the climb. The director wanted both of them under packs, trekking — the gear’s performance made visible. The trail was rough, and the brief required effortless joyful expressions on top of it; within half an hour Yoon-a’s calves were burning. Ji-ho — annoyingly — kept his easy stride throughout, occasionally glancing back with a look that translated precisely as sunbae, are we going to make it? Her teeth ached from grinding.

After a short lunch the team moved to a lake ringed with rock walls — warmer, but the wind came off the water like a physical object. One shot required him to hold her lightly from behind, both facing the lake, hair streaming.

“Softer expression!” the director called in French; the translator relayed.

He adjusted, arms loosely ringed at her waist. She leaned back against his chest, spine notably rigid.

“Nuna. Relax.”

“YOU relax,” she muttered — and relaxed anyway, a little.

Seven takes. When it finally passed, she stepped out of his arms at speed, ears faintly pink.

At dusk the crew built a bonfire at the forest’s edge for the night scenes — the easy part: two figures on a log, leaning close, firelight, jacket details, the occasional exchanged smile.

Day two: snowfield trekking. The chemistry had visibly improved overnight; brutal as the cold was, most setups passed in one or two takes. At 4 PM the director finally called it—

“CUT! Snow sequence complete!”

The crew cheered and hugged. Ji-ho exhaled a day’s tension, took the hot water his manager offered, drank deep—

—and a fistful of snow went down the back of his collar and slid the full length of his spine.

“Ssss—!”

He spun. Yoon-a stood several steps away, hands behind her back, blinking with maximal innocence, mouth betraying everything.

“Im! Yoon! A!”

“Oh dear. An accident.” Head tilt. Tone engineered for maximum aggravation. “The wind, probably? Blowing snow around?”

His eyes narrowed. He bent, unhurried, and began packing a snowball. She bolted — beanie pompom bouncing — leaving a line of gleeful footprints across the snow.

The sunset was turning Mont Blanc’s crown gold-pink; far below, Chamonix’s first lights pricked on. Ji-ho watched the orange figure zigzag away through the blue dusk, something amused flickering at the back of his eyes — then unfolded his long legs and ran her down without hurrying.

“Yah! Yoon-a! Stand still!”

“NEVER! Catch me if you can!”

She was laughing over her shoulder, breath ragged, provocation undimmed — and then the snow deepened, and her foot skidded, and the world pitched forward.

“Ah—!”

The cold impact never came. An arm hooked her waist from behind and hauled her back hard; momentum slammed her spine into a solid chest; his footing gave under the double weight; and they went down together into deep powder in an eruption of snow-spray.

“Kh— you okay?”

His voice arrived from above her head, arm still around her waist. Her heart was hammering — half fright, half something with no filed name. Snow dust clung to her lashes; the world smelled of cold pine and, underneath it, the clean scent that belonged to him.

“F-fine—”

Lower-voiced than usual. She tried to lever herself up; the soft snow betrayed her again; his arm reflexively cinched tighter, and the geometry became suddenly very close — her cheek grazing the freezing zipper of his shell.

Down the slope, the French crew packing equipment caught sight of the tangle and broke into good-natured laughter. Someone whistled. Accented English floated up: “Hey! Careful — romantic accident!” The mood stayed light; to everyone watching, it was just two young people fooling around in the snow.

Her ears went hot in earnest now. She elbowed backward, gently.

“Let GO already.”

He released her as if just noticing the arrangement, stood in one motion, and pulled her up after. She stood brushing snow off herself, head down, the mischief fully drained out of her — quiet in a way she rarely was.

He looked at her a moment. Then bent and scooped a fresh handful of snow.

She backed up a step, instantly wary.

“What are you doing?!”

“Revenge, obviously.”

He advanced, eyebrow up. She turned to run — and he caught the hood of her parka, gently, just enough to hold her in place.

“I was wrong! I was WRONG!” Instant capitulation, palms together, eyes enormous, all embarrassment dissolved back into comedy. “Ji-ho-ssi? Magnanimity? Mercy for your elders?”

The speed of the face-change broke his composure — but he didn’t drop the snowball. Instead, with great deliberation, he flicked the snow off her beanie with his free hand.

“You were fairly arrogant a minute ago.”

“A MISUNDERSTANDING! It was genuinely the wind—”

The snowball hand rose toward her cheek. She squeezed her eyes shut and shrank into her collar—

—and felt only a light press on top of her hat. She opened her eyes. He had simply set the loose snowball on her beanie’s pompom, like a tiny crown.

“Punishment.”

He released the hood and stepped back, eyes full of laughter, lit up by alpenglow and snow-shine.

She stood blinking — touched the snow on her hat — looked at him: a few steps off, draped in sunset, grinning like a boy. The embarrassment and the strange flutter smoothed out under it, replaced by something light and warm. She laughed, refused to lose, crouched, packed her own snowball, and wound up theatrically.

“Your revenge is DONE, right?! Then take THIS!”

He dodged, laughing, and the two of them chased each other between the dying daylight and the first lit windows until neither had breath left.

The last light sank behind the ridge; the crew was loading trucks. They walked back side by side, white breath tangling in the air between them.

“Cold?”

“A little.” Honest — the running-heat had burned off, and the mountain chill was reasserting itself.

He reached over, entirely naturally, and pulled up the hood she’d forgotten at her back, settling it carefully — fingertips brushing, in passing, the cool nape of her neck.

She shivered, minutely, and looked up at him. His expression was unbothered — a casual gesture, complete; hand already back in his own pocket.

“Come on. The van’s waiting.”

“Mm.”

She dropped her head and walked beside him — and under the hood’s shadow, the corner of her mouth curved up where no one could file it as evidence.

The ride down was warm and loose — two people in the back row, snowlit peaks scrolling past the glass, trading small shoot stories. And the day’s half-accidental touches, the brief accidental closeness, lay printed in the snow behind them like crossed footprints: soon to be covered by new weather, or kept — but already leaving, in both their private ledgers, one faint mark that would not wipe away.
