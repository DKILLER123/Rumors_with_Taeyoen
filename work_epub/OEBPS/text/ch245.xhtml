Net Worth Soars

August 14, 2013 Seoul · entertainment desks across the city one film leaves theaters, one song refuses to leave the charts, and a young chairman discovers the price of momentum
MOVIE WIRE · SEOUL EDITION · AUGUST 14, 2013
The Girl in the Sun closes at 12.84 million admissions, overtaking Miracle in Cell No. 7 to claim the year’s top spot

With its remaining screen share below one percent, the romantic drama has begun its final withdrawal from Korean theaters. Its cumulative audience places it fourth on the all-time admissions chart in the novel’s alternate entertainment timeline.

After weeks of sold-out evening screenings and stubbornly long weekend lines, The Girl in the Sun had finally reached its ceiling.

According to the latest figures attributed to the Korean Film Council, the film closed its principal theatrical run at 12.84 million admissions—just ahead of Miracle in Cell No. 7 at 12.81 million. Korea’s largest theater chains had already begun removing it from their schedules, leaving only scattered late-night and regional screenings behind.

For a romance released outside a major holiday corridor, the result was extraordinary. Ten million admissions remained the line separating a hit from a national event. Ji-ho’s first feature had not merely crossed it; the film had kept walking.

“A romance showing this degree of admissions power is rare even in a holiday season. To pass ten million during an ordinary release window—and then overtake a national phenomenon—proves that the film escaped its genre and became a cultural event.”

Film critic Park Chang-ho’s assessment was repeated across news portals throughout the morning.

The coverage returned again and again to the same person. Song Ji-ho had written the screenplay, played the male lead, and shaped the soundtrack. While the film occupied theaters, “Love Me Like You Do” and “Shallow” continued appearing on Billboard-linked coverage abroad. At nearly the same moment, Apink’s “NoNoNo”—credited to him within the novel’s alternate music industry—had become the summer’s most persistent digital hit. Then came his acquisition of LOEN Entertainment.

Actor. Screenwriter. Producer. Chairman.

Any one of those identities could sustain a career. Ji-ho had somehow stacked all four into a single summer.

Apink’s “NoNoNo” had now spent nine consecutive weeks at the top of the major digital services, including Melon and Genie. Its clean melody and fresh concept cut through a crowded market without attempting to outmuscle anyone. The song did not shout. It stayed—and staying proved more powerful.

INDUSTRY NOTE · THE “2.5 GENERATION” LABEL

Korean fan and media discourse often uses flexible generation labels rather than a single official system. In this chapter, “2.5 generation” refers broadly to girl groups debuting between approximately 2010 and 2013—after the defining second-generation boom and before the next major wave.

Frequently cited names include SISTAR, miss A, Apink, Girl’s Day, and Nine Muses. Their concepts expanded beyond the earlier purity-versus-sexiness divide: summer pop, cool glamour, model-like performance, retro styling, and refreshed innocent concepts all found distinct audiences.

Chinese-language fandoms have often used different boundaries, sometimes placing Apink in a later numbered generation and groups such as TWICE and Red Velvet later still. The terminology is debated; the music is not.

“Labels describe a market after the fact. A hit changes the market while it is happening.”

Digital competition among those groups had become ruthless. SISTAR and miss A were already trusted chart performers; Girl’s Day had built one of the era’s most dramatic upward trajectories. Apink’s advantage was not force but identity. At a time when every team searched for a sharper edge, “NoNoNo” made freshness feel decisive again.

On the music side, PSY, BIGBANG, Girls’ Generation, T-ara, Ailee, and Apink had filled the public’s playlists with songs that seemed to arrive already attached to a season.

And threaded through an implausible portion of that landscape was Ji-ho’s name.

The arithmetic was irresistible to magazine editors. Ji-ho had paid roughly ₩300 billion for his controlling stake. At the market’s latest closing price, that stake appeared to be worth about ₩520 billion. Add the liquidity he was believed to retain, and the headline number approached ₩700 billion.

Of course, market capitalization was not cash in a bank account. A controlling block could not be sold at the last quoted retail price without affecting the market, and negotiated acquisitions followed their own logic. But financial nuance rarely survived a glossy headline.

Cheongdam-dong · the Jung sisters’ apartment a finance magazine, a wardrobe negotiation, and an OST rumor

“Five hundred and twenty billion won…”

Jessica sat with the latest business magazine open across her knees, staring at the valuation printed beside Ji-ho’s photograph.

The acquisition notice had placed the purchase price near ₩300 billion. If the magazine’s estimate was accepted at face value, his stake had gained more than ₩200 billion in only a few months. Reports also claimed he still controlled substantial cash.

She looked down at the savings accumulated through years of rehearsals, flights, concerts, endorsements, and sleep sacrificed by the hour.

He had earned thousands of times that amount while she was deciding which airport coat would photograph best.

The comparison was not entirely fair. Jessica understood fashion margins better than securities markets. Market value, negotiated value, and spendable cash were three different creatures wearing one headline. Unfortunately, the headline remained very large.

Krystal glanced over from the opposite sofa.

“Eonni, what happened to your fashion magazines?”

“Yah. Mind your own business.”

Jessica lowered the magazine and gave her younger sister a long, assessing look.

Krystal noticed immediately.

“Why are you looking at me like that?”

“Why aren’t you wearing what I bought you?”

The magazine sailed across the room and landed against Krystal’s side.

“Yoon-a tried hers. The difference was obvious.”

Krystal planted both hands on her hips.

“How can you compare me to Yoon-a eonni?”

She straightened with all the dignity she could summon.

“I’m still ahead of her.”

“Stop posing. One is a tangerine and the other is a slightly larger tangerine.”

Krystal stared at her in betrayal.

How could a biological sister be this cruel?

Then again, Jessica had always considered tormenting her a private privilege. Krystal’s anger flared, discovered it had nowhere useful to go, and settled into strategy.

She moved closer with a sweet smile.

“Eonni.”

“No.”

“I haven’t asked yet.”

“Your voice already did.”

Krystal pinched the edge of Jessica’s sleeve between two fingers.

“Can I borrow some of your designer clothes? And two or three bags?”

Jessica’s refusal arrived before the last syllable.

“Have you lost your mind? I’m afraid of scratching them when I use them. You treat furniture like an obstacle course.”

“Eonni…”

“Absolutely not.”

“I joined a very good drama.”

Jessica’s expression changed.

“A drama?”

Krystal nodded quickly.

“The Heirs. I’m playing Lee Bo-na—a wealthy high-school student. The wardrobe budget isn’t generous, so several actors are bringing pieces from home. I can’t buy enough luxury items just for filming.”

For all their bickering, Jessica’s loyalty to her sister was absolute. She could tease Krystal without mercy; the outside world was not permitted to make her look unprepared.

Her posture softened into businesslike resolve.

“Fine. Take whatever suits the character. Choose several looks so you can rotate them.”

Krystal’s face lit.

“Really? Anything?”

Jessica took her hand and smiled with unnerving gentleness.

“Anything. But, Soojung-ah—if you damage one…”

A chill traveled neatly down Krystal’s spine.

She raised one hand as if taking an oath in court.

“I swear I’ll protect every stitch. Not one scratch. Not one loose thread.”

“Good. I trust you.”

“Ne, eonni.”

Krystal changed the subject before her sister could draft a written indemnity.

“I heard Ji-ho oppa is producing the drama’s OST.”

Jessica’s attention sharpened.

“Ji-ho?”

“Mm. And I know who’s singing one of the songs. You’ll be surprised.”

Jessica answered without hesitation.

“Taeyeon.”

“Eh? How did you know?”

Jessica did not answer.

Girls’ Generation’s third concert tour would take them to Jakarta the following month for their first solo concert in Indonesia. Rehearsals had already begun tightening around the members’ schedules. During the latest practices, the air between Taeyeon and Tiffany had felt wrong—too careful in one moment, too brittle in the next. Today, the two had gone to Jeonju together.

And Jessica had a proposal of her own, revised so many times that every page felt memorized.

Perhaps tonight was the night to place it in front of Ji-ho again.

She closed the magazine, reached for her bag, and stood.

“I’m going out.”

“Yah, eonni—where?”

The front door closed before Krystal received an answer.
