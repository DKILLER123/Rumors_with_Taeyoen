Chapter One Hundred Seventeen

Yeobo

The ramyeon was annihilated; most of the broth followed. They grinned at each other through the fading steam, fine sweat at both their hairlines.

She patted her stomach, watched him rise and gather the bowls with unconscious domesticity — it looks like parents keeping house — and went quietly sweet inside.

“Together.”

She confiscated an empty bowl and squeezed in beside him at the brand-new sink. He washed; she took each rinsed bowl and dried it with a soft cloth. Neither spoke. Arms brushed occasionally. Each time, the same exchanged smile.

Kitchen settled, she steered him into the bathroom, pulled open the vanity drawer, and produced — like a magician — two matching toothbrushes in different colors. Sky blue, and soft pink. She loaded both with toothpaste and set them side by side on the rim of the cup.

“When did you prepare these?” He picked up the blue one.

“This morning.” — delivered around a mouthful of foam, brushing with total diligence, eyes shining.

Mischief found him. As she closed her eyes to rinse, he touched one foam-dabbed fingertip to the tip of her nose.

“YAH!”

She opened her eyes on the white dot in the mirror, torn between outrage and laughter, and retaliated instantly — a swipe of foam across his cheek. The little bathroom filled with water-noise and undignified shrieking. When hostilities concluded, they rinsed each other’s faces clean, and she pulled two facial towels and dried his chin and hairline with great care.

“Come with me.”

Back in the bedroom she opened a drawer of neatly ranked bottles and jars, and patted the vanity stool.

“Sit.”

He sat, face tipped up, obedient. A warm towel first. Then toner, tapped in with soft fingertips — the cool touch closing his eyes for him, making her care legible through the skin. Essence next, warmed in her palms, pressed in by degrees, a faint botanical sweetness hanging between them. Finally a hydrating sheet mask, aligned to his features and smoothed flat, every air bubble chased out.

“Go wait on the bed. Don’t move.”

An order. She applied her own mask in record time, and then they lay side by side, two identical white mask-faces with only the eyes showing. Their gazes met. Both registered how ridiculous the other looked. Laughing would crease the masks — so they lay there pressing their lips shut, shoulders shaking in silence.

Time flowed by, quiet and thick.

Masks off, skin glowing, she smeared the leftover essence down his neck and over the backs of his hands, muttering:

“Artist management… has to be COMPREHENSIVE…”

Only then was she satisfied. She led him out by the hand; a single floor lamp made the living room amber and soft. The oversized new sofa received her first; she patted the spot beside her with complete naturalness, and the moment he sat, she folded herself into his arms like a bird returning to a nest, adjusting until the fit was perfect. He gathered her in, chin resting on her hair — the same clean scent as his own now, from the same bottles.

Outside, night thickened; the far bank’s lights smeared into a ribbon. Inside: two breaths, interleaved.

She tipped her face up. He bent, automatically.

This kiss was unlike any before it — no urgency, no heat, only gentleness: a slow verification of each other’s existence, a savoring of shared quiet. Her hand closed on the front of his pajamas; his palm lay against her back, furnace-warm through thin cotton.

A long time later, a small parting of lips, breath mingling. She retreated an inch, breathing unevenly, eyes curved to crescents.

“I missed you so much.”

“I missed you too.”

He brushed her lip with a thumb, then reached for the gift bag.

“Picked up on the way.”

“Wah— gorgeous~”

The pink-edged red roses conquered her instantly. She kissed his cheek, tore into the bag, twisted open the new lipstick shade, and her mouth climbed higher.

“Good eye.”

The sunscreen came out next and was set aside with a nod — “mine was almost empty” — and then she surveyed the loot, glowing.

“All things I love.”

She scooted close and waggled the lipstick at him.

“This gift — is it so I think of you every single time I use it?”

“That’s reason one.” He kissed her glossed lips once, unhurried — sweet. “Reason two: I’ve sampled so much of it lately, compensation seemed only fair.”

She pressed her lips together, laughing, shy. It was true — since acquiring a boyfriend, lip products had been depleting at an unprecedented rate.

She scratched her nose, glanced around her new house, and pulled him up by the hand.

“Let me show you around. I’ve barely finished setting up — you can give opinions.”

She towed him through the bright simple living room, opened the floor-length window onto a small untamed balcony with the Han glinting beyond it.

“Here — a lazy sofa, for sunbathing. Over there, a record player—”

She pointed and narrated blueprints, animated. He followed quietly, doing the other math: with her schedule and the team’s logistics, she’d still live mostly at the dorm. This place was minutes from LOEN — and notably far from SM. What it meant to buy a house that needed tending, positioned closer to him — he understood all of it.

“What made you suddenly buy here?” he asked, gently.

She was pushing open another door; her back stiffened for a single frame. Then she turned, leaned against the clean new doorframe, and pitched her voice at maximum offhandedness.

“INVESTMENT! Banpo-dong has huge upside. And I’d been meaning to move out of the dorm anyway. Extremely rational decision.” She blinked twice, adding credibility.

He looked at her — at the effort of wrapping something soft in the armor of “investment” — took one step, folded her in, and kissed the mouth still producing phrases like appreciation potential and loan rates.

“Mmph—”

One symbolic push at his shoulder. Then total structural failure.

However hard the mouth talks, it kisses soft.

The kiss was slow and thorough, and by the end of it every rational shell had dissolved and she hung in his arms with her hands locked behind his neck. He spoke against her ear, breath warming the reddened tip.

“Nuna… thank you. For all of it.”

She buried her burning face in his shoulder, and only after a long while admitted, small and muffled:

“We needed SOMEWHERE to date.”

“Don’t feel pressured, okay? Your future is going to be bigger than mine. I just… debuted earlier. That’s all my head start is.”

His arms tightened. He kissed the top of her head.

“Nuna. There’s no pressure. Only fuel.”

The afternoon had spent them thoroughly; the night asked nothing more. They lay wrapped together and simply talked — music, the past, the future.

He learned her family in full: a father who’d fronted a band and never let go of the singer’s dream, a mother who had sung children’s songs — a house that had music in its walls. When little Taeyeon said she wanted to sing, her father didn’t hesitate: every Sunday he drove her from Jeonju to Seoul for vocal lessons, and those long roads became the warmest thing in her childhood. Her mother, endlessly gentle, was the quiet floor under everything. An older brother, a younger sister, a home loose and loud and safe — the soft ground her soft heart had grown from.

Against all that warmth, his childhood arrived like weather from another country.

She learned, for the first time, what her boyfriend was carrying.

He told it evenly, like a story about someone else: the father who was simply not there one day when an eight-year-old came home from school in Koreatown; the mother whose last phone call reached him in a middle-school cafeteria, before the number went dark forever. The bars he’d sung in before he was old enough to be in them. The flight to a country that was supposed to be ancestral and felt, at eighteen, like the far side of the moon — taken not only to chase a dream, but because there was no home left standing behind him.

Her eyes went wide first. Then the shock drowned under something fiercer. Her eyes rimmed red in seconds; she sat up, and pure instinct pulled him into her arms.

“You—” Her voice came out wrecked; she cleared it and tried again. “Back then… you must have been so scared.”

“…Probably I was.” Too much time had passed; the edges had worn smooth. “It’s hard to remember the fear itself anymore.”

She looked down at his calm, unbothered profile, and the pieces reassembled themselves: the composure that read as effortless was a soul that had learned to walk by falling. The stillness and maturity that didn’t match his age weren’t temperament — they were scar tissue. He hadn’t crossed an ocean only for a dream. He’d crossed it because the shore behind him was gone.

Every additional minute of imagining — the boy singing in dim bars, the hungry stretches, the arrival in a foreign-familiar country with no one waiting — tightened the invisible hand around her heart another turn, and hardened the same resolve: protect him. Warm him.

“Sweetheart,” she said at last, gentler than she had ever said anything, “from now on — you have me beside you.”

Something in her grew up in that moment. She was no longer only his sweet, spoiled girlfriend. The deep maternal bedrock in her — the instinct to shelter — had been struck, and rung like a bell.

The bedroom was very quiet. Only two breaths, woven.

“It’s all in the past.” He was calm, and turned his hand to fold her cool fingers completely inside his warm ones. “And besides — now there’s you.”

That sentence outperformed every possible comfort. Her tears finally broke loose.

“Mm.” Thick with crying, she pressed the promise into his ear like a seal: “You have me. From now on, you have me.”

“For a lifetime?”

“Mm. A lifetime!”

She held him hard enough to compress a decade of missing safety into one embrace. He closed his eyes, chin on her soft hair.

Taeyeon-ah. Remember what you promised tonight.

A long while later she loosened her arms, eyes and nose pink, sniffled once, and burrowed back into his chest.

“Sweetheart. Let’s sleep.”

“Mm. Goodnight.”

“Goodnight.”

Field Notes

A note on the chemistry of love: a body in love brews a whole apothecary of hormones — and the one that produces the reckless, obsessive, must-be-near-you madness is phenylethylamine. Its peak concentration lasts somewhere between six months and four years. That, biochemically, is the length of one love. Humans are not, by wiring, faithful creatures; love-unto-death runs against the machinery. Which is why a life keeps falling in love with new people. Men. Women. Everyone.

But the Korean word for dating means, literally, meeting and talking. And there’s the loophole. Two people who have talked through their views of life and worth; talked plans and conduct; talked love, desire, family, dreams; talked flaws and virtues — who have gone from friends to lovers and built a second friendship inside the love — have braided far too much of themselves together to be cut apart by one molecule’s half-life.

Tonight’s conversation was that kind of talk. From tonight, as long as one of them refused to let go, the other could not fall out of this. A relationship that is only a shared bed, a stroll, a meal, a movie — when it cracks, nobody fights for it. This one had just become the other kind.

Morning. Heavy curtains held back the slanting sun; the bedroom swam in dim amber.

Ji-ho woke first, slipped out, and handled the essentials on silent feet.

Taeyeon slept on — deeply, and in a posture that would never clear her own image standards: escaped from his arms at some point in the night, sprawled half on her side, half out of the blanket, chestnut hair flung across the pillow and across milk-pale shoulders, breathing slow and even in a thin white camisole.

Under the Idol Aura’s ministrations, his own fatigue had been wiped to zero; he was, if anything, over-charged. He lifted the blanket and gathered her in from behind — fine warm skin against him, his palm settling feather-light at her waist.

“Sunny… quit it,” she mumbled, mostly submerged.

…Excuse me. WHO are you addressing?

He tightened his arm a fraction. Her brow creased; her breathing quickened; she surfaced halfway.

“…Ji-ho-ya?”

“Nuna. Call me yeobo.”

Sleep-fogged, defenseless, she answered on pure reflex, soft as cotton:

“…Yeobo.”

No word had ever landed on him harder. His arm closed; she came fully awake with a jolt.

“Yah— you…?!”

“Lunatic.”

“Yah…!”

“Nuna, don’t worry — I know my limits.” Low, velvet, and utterly shameless. “Once more. Yeobo. Go on.”

“…Not saying it.”

“Hm?”

“Don’t— I— fine— yeo… yeobo~”

“Yeo… yeobo~”

The small trembling syllables dissolved, blushing, into the morning light.

Breakfast: toast and fried eggs on the table. He came out carrying the milk.

“Nuna. Breakfast.”

She was already seated — and at the sight of him she turned her face away with performative fury.

“Still angry?” He sat beside her, set down the glasses, entirely unbothered. “Then I’ll return it. Yeobo — my wife. Eat your breakfast.”

W— wife?!

Heat flooded her ears. The sidelong glance she snuck at him was equal parts shyness and hope — and when their eyes met, hers fled straight to the milk glass.

“I remember… we didn’t buy milk?”

“I bought it.” Tone: perfectly ordinary. “Lately I keep having this one dream. It says — when a person meets the one they’ll love for a lifetime, the body secretes a special hormone. And under it, you can even… grow again. A second growth phase.”

A pause. Then, softly:

“I keep thinking dreams might carry omens. And I’ve met MY nuna. So — milk. Maybe I’ll get a little taller.”

She had woken up mid-sulk over the morning’s outrageous extracted vocabulary. But one “my wife” had already liquefied most of the sulk — and now this.

The one they’ll love for a lifetime. A special hormone. A second growth. Absurd. Fairy-tale. And — an omen? What omen? …Wait. “I’ve met MY nuna.” Nuna. That’s me. He met ME. So the person he’ll love for a lifetime is—

Her heart skipped, audibly, in her own ears. He wasn’t talking about height at all. He was saying their meeting was fated. He was saying their future was measured in lifetime.

A hot tide rose from her sternum and flooded outward to her fingertips; her eyes prickled without warning.

She had met her lifetime person too.

The knowledge melted her completely. She didn’t dare look at him. She lifted the glass and drank in small sips — and it tasted faintly, cleanly sweet, better than any milk she could remember.

He watched her drink it down, and smiled with great gentleness.

The glass in her hands was Height-Reforging Milk: two to five centimeters, delivered gently over a year, strongest effects at her end of the height curve. A dream was the most natural cover story available — on a peninsula that consulted shamans, scheduled surgeries by fortune-teller, and renamed debuting idols by hexagram, a prophetic dream wasn’t just plausible. It was PREFERRED. And when she actually grew… the dream would become her own private proof that they were written in the ledger of heaven — and every other woman circling him, a scheming interloper against fate itself.
