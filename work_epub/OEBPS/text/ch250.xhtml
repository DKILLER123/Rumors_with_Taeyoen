Chapter Two Hundred Fifty

Choosing the Business Path

Late morning Ji-ho’s hillside villa · guest wing a sober answer, a rejected first draft, and the beginnings of a second career

Ji-ho returned to the villa without making a sound.

Jessica was still asleep in the guest room. Morning light rested across her face, softening the disciplined elegance she wore in public. Her hair had escaped across the pillow in untidy strands; with her guard lowered, she looked younger and unexpectedly peaceful.

He chose not to wake her.

Instead, he folded a clean set of pale-lavender silk sleepwear and left it on the bedside table. Then he withdrew to the living room, opened his laptop, and began organizing the practical questions behind the decision he had already reached.

If Jessica wanted a second career, what kind of company should they build?

Sunlight strengthened by degrees, crossing the guest-room floor until it reached the bed.

Jessica frowned at the brightness and opened her eyes.

A dull ache pulsed behind her temples. She pressed two fingers against one side of her head while the previous night returned in fragments: the heat of liquor, Min-young’s advice, the stone steps beneath a streetlamp—and Ji-ho.

She sat up too quickly and immediately regretted it.

What, exactly, had she said?

THE NIGHT BEFORE · MEMORY RETURNING IN PIECES

“I’m willing to stake myself on the future I want.”

“Five years as your protected companion—or three years proving I can become your partner.”

“The alcohol gave me courage. It did not invent the decision.”

“If you can make me choose you freely, I’ll stop pretending I feel nothing.”

Heat flooded Jessica’s face. She covered it with both hands and groaned into her palms.

She had truly said all of it. More than once.

She also remembered Ji-ho’s expression: surprise first, then a complexity she could not read, and finally complete calm.

When you wake tomorrow, if you still stand behind it, I’ll give you my answer.

Then he had helped her into the car and brought her here.

Jessica looked around the unfamiliar bedroom. The sleepwear on the bedside table caught the light with an expensive sheen.

It was clearly not a man’s, nor had it been purchased for her.

Her fingers paused against the silk.

She already knew Ji-ho’s private life was crowded. Knowledge did not prevent the brief sting of imagining who else had worn something from this wardrobe.

Taeyeon was the likeliest answer. They were close enough in height that the fit would probably be exact.

Jessica shook the thought away, took the sleepwear, and entered the bathroom.

Warm water eased some of the hangover’s weight, but it could not wash the proposal out of her mind.

She had been serious.

It was not a drunken improvisation. She had met famous actors, executives, heirs, and men whose résumés were designed to impress before they entered a room. None had done what Ji-ho had done: arrive in Korea as a nearly penniless Korean-American trainee and, in little more than a year, become a charting artist, filmmaker, producer, and chairman of LOEN.

Admiration alone was dangerous. Admiration tied to need was worse.

But pretending she felt neither would not create the future she wanted.

✦ ✦ ✦

After her shower, Jessica changed into the lavender sleepwear. It fit as precisely as she had expected.

She studied herself in the mirror, drew one steadying breath, and opened the door.

Ji-ho sat on the living-room sofa with a laptop across his knees, brows drawn slightly together in concentration. Sunlight cut a clean line along his profile.

Jessica stopped for half a second.

Why did he have to look like that while making everything more difficult?

An absurd thought followed: if he chose the first option, spending five years beside a man this attractive did not sound entirely like a loss.

He heard her and looked up.

His gaze caught on the sleepwear. For one fleeting moment, recognition—or memory—crossed his face.

Jessica understood. The clothes belonged to another woman, and in them she must resemble that woman enough to disturb him.

She suppressed the sourness that rose in her chest and sat at the far end of the sofa.

Ji-ho closed the laptop.

“Headache?”

“Manageable.” She smoothed back her damp hair. “Thank you for bringing me here last night.”

He nodded. Several quiet seconds passed.

“Everything you proposed—does it still stand?”

Jessica’s heartbeat stumbled once.

She met his eyes without retreating.

“Every word.” Her voice was steady now. “It wasn’t drunken nonsense, and it wasn’t impulse. I’ve considered this for a long time. The alcohol only gave me enough courage to say it aloud.”

Ji-ho watched her carefully, searching for hesitation.

At last, he nodded.

“All right.”

Hope flashed across her face.

“You accept?”

“I choose the second path.”

DREAM LIFE · CHALLENGE ROUTE CONFIRMED

Ding—Businesswoman Development Route selected.

Mission OneCo-author a business proposal with Jung Soo-yeon, invest jointly, and establish a company.

RewardBusiness Store Refresh Card ×1

The refresh card accepts three business-related keywords and regenerates the corresponding store inventory.

Ji-ho lowered his eyes, concealing the brief light in them.

Jessica smiled. Relief loosened something that had been clenched inside her since the night before.

He had rejected the cage.

Whatever complications remained, he had chosen to invest in Jung Soo-yeon rather than merely possess Jessica.

“Wait here.”

She hurried back to the guest room and returned with a document folder from her bag. This time, she offered it with both hands.

“My business plan.”

Ji-ho opened it.

JUNG SOO-YEON · FOUNDING PROPOSAL · DRAFT 01

Mid-priced contemporary fashion label

Founder roleCreative director and principal designer

PositioningAccessible celebrity-led street and lifestyle fashion

Target customerYoung women influenced by Korean pop culture and Jessica’s public style

Launch advantageImmediate fandom awareness and earned media

The proposal is detailed, sincere—and built around an assumption Ji-ho intends to challenge.

It was not another sunglasses concept. Jessica wanted to design a complete contemporary label at a moderate price point. The document covered brand philosophy, product categories, target consumers, and promotional strategy with obvious care.

Ji-ho closed the folder.

“Why must it be fashion? And why do you need to be the designer?”

Jessica pressed her lips together. When she answered, a small trace of hurt entered her voice.

“Because it’s what I know.”

Once she began, the rest followed more easily.

“My family was comfortable. I grew up reading fashion magazines, studying styling, comparing cosmetics, and paying attention to how clothes change the way someone is perceived.”

“When Girls’ Generation debuted, half the cosmetics in the dorm came from my bags. I’ve lived around these things my whole life. I thought that meant I could build something from them.”

Ji-ho considered her answer.

“Then we need to discuss founder fit.”

Jessica sat straighter.

“Go on.”

“You want to create a fashion label, design the products yourself, and sell them under your image. Correct?”

She nodded.

“Have you considered that design may not be your strongest role?”

Her smile tightened.

Ji-ho did not look away.

“You have excellent taste. You understand proportion, styling, and presentation. But styling and product design are not the same discipline.”

“Most of your fashion instincts come from studying magazines, wearing established brands, and combining references with unusual precision. That makes you a strong curator. It does not automatically make you a trained designer.”

Jessica’s lips parted in protest, but no immediate argument came.

His tone softened without surrendering the point.

“Design begins before the finished look. Fabric behavior, pattern cutting, garment construction, grading, production tolerances—those are technical systems. A celebrity label could sell its first collection on attention alone. What happens after the attention moves on?”

Jessica fell quiet.

The question hurt because she had asked herself versions of it before.

“Then what do you think I should build?”

She sounded younger when disappointed.

Ji-ho allowed a hint of a smile.

“Something you understand more deeply than you realize.”

“What?”

“Beauty and skincare.”

Jessica frowned.

“That market is already crowded.”

“Crowded markets still reward trust, repeat purchasing, and clear positioning.”

FOUNDER FIT · CELEBRITY CONSUMER BRANDS

A durable celebrity business converts three assets into a product: public identity, genuine domain experience, and audience trust.

Fashion can create strong brand heat, but inventory complexity, sizing, seasonal collections, and trend risk make execution unforgiving. Beauty and personal care offer repeat purchasing, standardized formats, mature contract-manufacturing infrastructure, and products that can be demonstrated directly by the founder.

Entry is easier than mastery. Formula safety, stability testing, claims compliance, manufacturing quality, and customer retention remain non-negotiable.

“Fame can buy the first order. Only product performance buys the second.”

“You’ve worked with cosmetics since debut,” Ji-ho continued. “You know which textures performers tolerate under stage makeup, which cleansers punish the skin after repeated use, and which products survive travel, dry studios, and three hours of sleep.”

“You also hear what the other members, actresses, stylists, and makeup artists praise or complain about. That is a living archive of consumer testing.”

Jessica tried to object and discovered that the argument was already reorganizing itself inside her head.

“The most stable celebrity ventures turn personal authority into high-repeat, relatively asset-light consumer products,” Ji-ho said. “Beauty fits you better than pretending you can cut a pattern because you know how to wear one.”

Her eyes narrowed at the last sentence.

“You could have phrased that more kindly.”

“Kindly would cost you money.”

She wanted to remain offended. The logic made it difficult.

Ji-ho leaned back.

“Think beyond Korea. K-beauty has room to grow throughout Asia, especially China. A recognizable Korean face attached to credible products can cross borders more easily than a clothing line that needs constant collections and sizing.”

He drew on examples from the future he remembered.

“Jessica Alba turned personal trust into The Honest Company—a clean household and personal-care platform that later reached a billion-dollar valuation. Jamie Kern Lima built IT Cosmetics around a real skin problem and demonstrated the solution herself; her first QVC appearance in 2010 sold out within minutes, and the brand passed a million dollars in sales that year.”

“Closer to home, actresses such as Kim Hee-sun prove the underlying principle whenever their image moves beauty products across Korea and China: consumers buy the possibility of sharing the face, confidence, or routine they admire.”

“They’re different from me,” Jessica said, though without much force.

“Not at the starting line.”

He looked directly at her.

“In one respect, you have an advantage they didn’t.”

“What advantage?”

The corner of his mouth lifted.

“You have me.”

Jessica blinked. Color rose faintly into her face.

Ji-ho reopened the laptop.

REVISED BUSINESS DIRECTION · WORKING MODEL

Celebrity-led beauty and skincare company

Entry optionsAcquire a strong but under-marketed niche brand, or recruit experienced cosmetic chemists and operators to develop the line internally.

Launch productsSheet masks · hydrating toner · cleansing water · gentle recovery-focused basics

Founder roleBrand direction, wear testing, product feedback, campaign leadership, and public trust

Operating requirementProfessional formulation, safety and stability testing, regulatory review, scalable manufacturing, and transparent claims

Market promiseProducts that survive the real conditions of an idol’s skin—not fantasy in a bottle

“You use the products yourself and become the first face of the company,” he said. “Your skin is the proof people see before they read a single claim.”

“Then we recruit carefully. A small brand with excellent formulas but weak distribution could be acquired. Otherwise, we hire experienced researchers, a serious regulatory team, and operators who understand manufacturing.”

“Masks, toner, and cleansing water are sensible entry categories—not because quality is easy, but because consumers understand them and repurchase when they work.”

Jessica’s frown began to ease.

“And marketing?”

“You lead it. But we don’t rely on your name alone.”

He turned the laptop toward her and began arranging the launch sequence.

“Taeyeon and Yoon-a could participate as paid ambassadors or minority shareholders if they genuinely like the products. Their use would create the first wave of trust: Jessica’s mask, Yoon-a’s hydrating toner, Taeyeon’s cleansing water.”

“But endorsement only opens the door. If the mask irritates people or the toner does nothing, the same fame that creates the launch will accelerate the collapse.”

Jessica nodded slowly.

“So the formulas have to justify the campaign.”

“Exactly. People may arrive because of you. They must stay because the product earns a place in their routine.”

“You can participate deeply in testing and refinement. You know what long-haul flights, stage makeup, dry rehearsal rooms, and aggressive cleansing do to skin. That experience is useful—as long as professionals translate it into safe products.”

He added one final layer.

“LOEN is building a screen-production division. Successful dramas can turn objects, food, clothing, and cosmetics into regional demand overnight. Product placement inside our own projects gives us a distribution channel most startup founders could never buy.”

The Placement Reel · When Dramas Sell

The crystal snow globe of My Girl — gone from every shelf in the country.

Fried chicken and beer, ordered by the bucketload every time My Love from the Star aired — chi-mek by decree of Cheon Song-yi.

Descendants of the Sun — the lipstick shades, the skincare, the songs: sold out. All of it.

Guardian: The Lonely and Great God — every prop, every scarf, every trinket the Goblin ever touched: cleaned off the shelves.

When a hit drama touches a product, the product disappears.

“Imagine the product in the year’s biggest drama,” Ji-ho said. “The lead actress uses it naturally, viewers search for it that night, and the campaign is already waiting when they arrive.”

Jessica was no longer resisting.

Her expression had moved from wounded pride to concentration, then from concentration to the first unmistakable spark of excitement.

She looked at him as if seeing the architecture behind his success for the first time.

Product positioning. Founder fit. Consumer psychology. Manufacturing discipline. Marketing, distribution, and future media integration.

He had not offered a vague promise to make her rich. He had taken her ambition apart, discarded the weakest assumption, and rebuilt it into something that might survive beyond her fame.

How could one person think this many steps ahead?

Admiration settled quietly into her gaze—and with it, a more dangerous certainty.

Choosing the second path had not made the wager safer.

It had made the future feel possible.
