Chapter Two Hundred Twenty-Four

Signed and Taken

August 1, 2013 · 10:00 AM SK Group Headquarters, Seoul · 25th floor conference room the pen comes down — and an artist’s former CEO watches his newest boss sign the papers

The car drew to a stop before SK Group headquarters.

The peninsula’s third-largest multinational, one of its four great chaebol — the tower stood in central Seoul with the calm of a thing that had never once needed to raise its voice. Energy and chemicals on one pillar, information and communications on the other; semiconductors, EV batteries, hydrogen on the horizon; number 84 on the Fortune Global 500, revenues in the tens of billions of dollars. Its chairman, Chey Tae-won, was currently serving a prison sentence — and the empire had not missed a single step.

At that same hour, on another floor of the SK building, LOEN Entertainment CEO Cho Young-chul was frowning at a formal notice from the group’s planning division: his attendance was required at the signing ceremony for the transfer of LOEN’s controlling stake. The notice named no buyer. It said only: a young entrepreneur of exceptional potential.

“Young entrepreneur?” He ran the chaebol second-generation rosters through his head, then the new-capital names, and matched nothing. The blankness itself was unsettling. The sale was a settled fact — but the new owner’s temperament and philosophy would decide his future and his team’s, in detail. He straightened his suit and carried his doubts up early to the small waiting room on the signing floor.

The car door opened. Ji-ho stepped down, squared his collar with one hand, and walked toward the entrance as if he had done it a thousand times.

Waiting at the doors: SHIN & KIM’s lead counsel Lee Myung-ha, and SK’s reception delegation. Every eye settled on the approaching young man and stayed there.

The black suit was cut to the millimeter over the tall frame; behind the gold-rimmed glasses the eyes were still and sharp; the face was — objectively, unfairly — flawless. The whole figure gave off a cool, don’t-approach magnificence, as if a chaebol drama had misplaced its lead. Two of SK’s staffers forgot, briefly, what their jobs were.

“Mr. Song, the honor’s mine. Executive Director Kim Sung-hwan is waiting upstairs — this way, please.”

“Lead on.”

A party of a dozen crossed the lobby, Ji-ho at its center, Choi Young-jun guiding, everyone else falling naturally half a step back. His stride was unhurried; the field around him spread without a sound — and down the lobby, SK employees found themselves pausing, and then, without quite deciding to, inclining into small bows as he passed.

Only when the group was gone did they straighten and look at each other.

Within three minutes, the lobby photo was making its rounds through every internal chat in the tower.

In the small waiting room, Cho Young-chul’s phone buzzed with the same photo — and he froze mid-sip, eyes going wide. Song— Ji-ho? The trainee who’d joined LOEN a year ago. The golden goose. The twenty-year-old who bowed to HIM in hallways. THIS was SK’s “young entrepreneur?” The buyer of LOEN — was HIM?! His first instinct was a data error: maybe the boy fronted for other capital? But there was SK’s deputy director personally guiding, deferent; there was the boy walking dead center with the gravity of a keystone. That was no artist paying a visit. He scrolled every public fact he knew — the impossible catalog of hits, the record-breaking sales, the terrifying commercial instincts — and felt the floor of his understanding give way. Had all of that been… the surface? He drank his coffee too fast and made himself breathe. Whatever else was true, he was about to meet his new owner face to face. …Am I actually old now?

✦ ✦ ✦

SK Group, 25th floor. The conference room’s window-wall held the whole jagged skyline; documents lay squared along both sides of the long table.

When Ji-ho entered with Deputy Director Choi, Cho Young-chul — already seated in the observers’ row — felt his heart knock once, hard. Even forewarned, the sight of his own artist entering in THIS capacity, with THIS bearing, hit like cold water. A year ago: a company asset. Today: his owner. The vertigo refused to resolve.

“Executive Director Kim. It’s been a while.”

“Mr. Song, welcome.” Kim Sung-hwan shook his hand, then turned toward the head of the table. “May I present Director Choi Yoon-jung — the chairman’s eldest daughter. She’ll preside over today’s signing.”

Ji-ho’s gaze traveled over, and his smile opened, bright and easy.

“Sajang-nim. Good morning.”

“G-good morning, Mr. Song.”

Cho Young-chul was on his feet and bowing before his brain caught up, voice strung a note too tight.

Ji-ho gave him a small nod and turned to the head of the table.

Choi Yoon-jung. Eldest daughter of Chey Tae-won and Roh So-young — granddaughter of a former president — the eldest princess of SK. He set his posture with care and bowed, precisely.

“Director Choi. An honor. Ji-ho.”

She rose, shook briefly, released. Her voice was cool water.

“Mr. Song is more striking off camera than on.”

“You’re very kind. Next to your work in bioengineering, this face is a rounding error. I’ve long admired the decisiveness you’re known for — it’s a privilege to finally meet.”

He knows my FIELD— A flicker of surprise crossed her eyes, and several degrees of warmth entered the smile. Entertainers did not, as a rule, know what the quiet daughter of the family actually did.

“A man with your schedule, keeping track of a backroom researcher — now I’M flattered.” She raised a hand toward the chairs. “Please. Let’s sit.”

They settled. In the observers’ row, Cho Young-chul’s eyes tracked Ji-ho’s every movement, involuntarily, like a compass finding north.

“Noona, shall we begin?” Choi Young-jun asked, attentive.

“Deputy Director Choi.” One glance. “In the office, titles.”

“…Yes, Director Choi.”

Kim Sung-hwan smoothly took the thread, opening his folder.

“Director Choi prefers no fanfare, so today’s ceremony is internal only. Mr. Song won’t mind?”

“It’s exactly right.”

“Then formally: per the agreement reached between SK Group and Mr. Ji-ho, today we execute the purchase contract for fifty-five percent of LOEN Entertainment.”

The secretaries distributed the documents. Ji-ho accepted the heavy folder and did not reach for the pen. Instead he looked first to Choi Yoon-jung.

“Director — before I sign, allow me to thank SK properly. LOEN has been run soundly under the group for years; its foundations are solid and its people are professionals. Taking over a company like this is a privilege.”

On the words “its people,” his gaze drifted — just perceptibly — across the observers’ row. Cho Young-chul’s spine straightened by itself.

“You’re gracious, Mr. Song.” A small nod. “LOEN has performed adequately in entertainment, but SK must concentrate on core businesses going forward — hence the sale. We hope you take it further than we did.”

SK kept its face, as the great houses do. The Fair Trade Commission had mandated the divestment — and the parting words were still delivered with perfect grace.

“I’ll do everything in my power.”

Only then did he lower his eyes to the contract — unhurried, clause by clause, leaning aside twice to trade quiet words with Lee Myung-ha.

Choi Yoon-jung watched him work. Twenty years old. Already at the summit of his industry — and now acquiring control of LOEN, which put him outside every category the word “artist” could hold. And rarer still: in a room like this, at a moment like this, not one flicker of restlessness. That degree of stillness ran years ahead of his age.

“Mr. Song seems confident about entertainment’s future,” she said suddenly.

He looked up, gaze open.

“I am. Global streaming is rising; the industry is entering another golden decade.”

A measured pause.

“And LOEN’s particular advantage is the twin engine — content AND channel. Artist management and production on one side; a platform like Melon on the other. Keep investing in premium content, keep iterating the channel, and the road is long and wide.”

A gleam of real appraisal crossed her eyes. That was precisely the strategic core of SK’s original acquisition thesis — and it rhymed with the group’s push for LOEN’s own purchase of stakes elsewhere. She had assumed he wanted the music platform. He had priced the whole organism.

“You’ve done your homework, Mr. Song.”

“For an investment this important, anything less would be disrespectful.” He smiled — and turned the corner smoothly. “Though I’ll be honest with you, Director: an artist converting into an operator will face real management challenges in the near term. Role changes take calibration.”

Deft, thought Cho Young-chul, pulse ticking up. Self-awareness on display — and room reserved, in the same sentence, for personnel changes. “Calibration.” So there WOULD be movement. Would his own chair survive it?

“Mr. Song is too modest,” Kim Sung-hwan supplied at the right moment. “With the eye you’ve shown for content and commerce, LOEN is more likely to level up in your hands.”

“I’ll try to be worthy of the prediction.”

The review ended. Lee Myung-ha confirmed quietly: “All clauses as negotiated. No objections.”

“Good.”

Ji-ho lifted the pen and signed — one fluid line, no hesitation in it anywhere.

Choi Yoon-jung signed for SK. Kim Sung-hwan countersigned as witness. The folders crossed the table; the second set was signed; and when the final document settled, polite applause moved around the room.

DONE. Finally, FINALLY, done. Inside his chest, something detonated in pure gold. Outside, his face stayed exactly where he’d left it.

“Congratulations, Mr. Song.” She offered her hand first this time.

“Thank you, Director — and thank you, SK, for the trust.”

He rose and took her hand in both of his — respectful, and not a millimeter servile.

✦ ✦ ✦

A short celebration followed — champagne on silver, the room loosening by degrees.

He made a point of coming to Choi Yoon-jung first, rim of his glass a centimeter below hers as they touched.

“Again — thank you for your support. I hope there’ll be occasion to work with SK in other fields.”

“Talent finds occasions.” She took a sip — then, without preamble: “I hear Mr. Song is having some trouble with public opinion lately?”

The topic swerved hard enough that Choi Young-jun’s face flickered and Kim Sung-hwan glanced over. In the observers’ row, Cho Young-chul’s ears stood straight up. Ji-ho? An OPINION problem? How? Setting aside that the man now OWNED the company — even as mere golden goose, PR existed to keep him spotless. His CEO’s face began to darken on pure professional reflex. What was Yoo Jae-ha DOING over there?

Ji-ho’s expression didn’t move at all. The smile stayed light.

“Standard weather in my industry. There are always people fond of small tricks. But in the end, the work and the strength underneath it are the only ground anyone stands on.”

No defense mounted. No grievance aired. The whole scandal filed, in one clause, under “small tricks” — and the filing itself was a show of altitude. Choi Yoon-jung, who had watched dark water move under business her entire adult life, revised him upward once more.

“If you need help, say so plainly. SK keeps some resources in media.”

“You’re generous, Director. Though I suspect—” a beat, pleasant and exact, “—this particular problem resolves itself within the day.”

“Oh?” One eyebrow rose a precise millimeter.

He only smiled, and let it stand.

The reception ran half an hour, and he worked it flawlessly — a short exchange with every SK representative, each name and title held and returned correctly; attention total when listening, responses fitted to the listener. The details did the talking: leaning in a degree when Choi Young-jun spoke; the timed nod at Kim Sung-hwan’s observations; the glass lowered even for the secretaries and aides when rims touched.

Last of all, glass in hand, he arrived — naturally, as if by drift — in front of Cho Young-chul.

“Sajang-nim.” The register was warmer now than in session. “We’ll be working together from here on. I’ll be counting on your guidance.”

Cho Young-chul had his glass up instantly, rim well below. “Mr. So— Hoejang-nim. You honor me — it’s I who’ll be asking for YOUR consideration. Congratulations on the acquisition.” — Hoejang-nim, already. The man reads wind direction by the second, Ji-ho noted, interest rising. A CEO who could bend that fast was a CEO who could be USED. And heaven knew he had no intention of spending his own hours running payroll.

“LOEN’s foundations exist because of you and your people, Sajang-nim.” Their glasses touched. “I’m new to management; there’s a great deal I’ll lean on your experience for. Let’s speak soon — I want the transition seamless.”

“Yes — my full cooperation, of course. Whatever direction or instruction Hoejang-nim has, at any hour.”

“The specifics — we’ll discuss privately.”

A nod closed it, courteous and final.

On his way out he returned to Choi Yoon-jung and took his leave with full formality.

“Thank you for today. I hope to learn from you again soon.”

“Anytime.” And then — rare, according to everyone who knew her — a small real smile. “Mr. Song. The road ahead of you has no visible end.”

✦ ✦ ✦

One body-length behind Ji-ho, escorting him toward the elevators, Cho Young-chul was thinking harder than he had in a decade.

The first shock had burned off. What remained was heavier. The new owner was absurdly young — and the vision, bearing, and command he’d shown upstairs ran YEARS past the age on his ID. Which reframed everything: had the boy chosen LOEN, a year ago, already building toward THIS? And the mystery fund — was there deeper capital standing behind him still? Blessing or landmine? Where was LOEN going? And where, in the new map, was HIS chair?

One thing he knew with total clarity: the private talk to come was the most important conversation of his remaining career. It would decide his fate — and the fate of a great many people inside LOEN.
