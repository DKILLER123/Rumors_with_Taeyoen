In the club’s second room, the river side, where the tall glass looks west over the water instead of into the courtyard, Im Yoon-a arrived half an hour before the appointment, which is to say she arrived an hour before she expected him to be late.

Before the full-length mirror she conducted the final inspection.

Haze-blue silk. The collar open exactly as far as the third button: not a plunge, not a fortress, a collarbone arriving precisely midway between accident and appointment. Beneath it, engineering: a little padding and the right bra, and what are ordinarily little tangerines presented tonight as something nearer to pears, with a softness of line that only an expert could audit. She was not expecting experts. Or rather, she was expecting exactly one.

Below: black high-waisted wide-legs, lengthening everything, the hems brushing her ankles on the move; on her feet, silver stilettos thin enough to file a lawsuit.

The makeup was lighter than yesterday’s and twice as deliberate. Eyeliner drawn a fraction longer, the outer corners lifted a degree, so the clean doe eyes carry a thin blade of allure now; and on her mouth a water-gloss bean-paste rose that asks for no attention and gets all of it when the light crosses her.

She practiced the smile once. Corners up, aegyo-sal attending, friendly and lovely and calibrated. Then she nodded slightly at the mirror, one professional to another, and let it go.

When the door opened, that was the picture he walked in on: her at the tall glass, backlit, the sunset laying a rim of warm gold along her profile.

She didn’t turn at once. She held it for two seconds, long enough for the man at the door to receive the picture whole, short enough that the picture could still pretend not to have been staged, and then she brought her head around, unhurried, with the smile arriving like something she’d set by the door.

Light, easy, worn-in: the voice of a woman who has waited for this particular man many times, in this particular light, across a history neither of them has actually lived yet.

Im Yoon-a. You’re a degree off your usual calibration tonight.

She did not sit where she had sat yesterday. She took the middle of the sofa, one place left of center: not formal, not forward, and leaving him the whole remainder of the room to answer in, beside her or opposite.

He chose opposite.

Her eye registered it. Her smile did not.

The posture did exactly what the posture was built to do: the collarbone, the line of white skin, the soft new architecture. And her eyes stayed so open and so calm about it that noticing any of it felt like your own idea, which was, of course, the design.

“Then in your diagnosis,” she said slowly, “where is the root of it?”

“The schedule.” He answered before the question had fully landed. “You haven’t been allowed to live a life since debut. Every part you’ve played was chosen because it fit the image: idol dramas, bright girls, first loves. Have you ever played a woman with nothing? Carried a drama on your own shoulders? Played a villain and made the audience enjoy it?”

She shook her head, once.

“Every emotion you own is secondhand. Observed in others. Imagined. The face is a salary; I understand that. But an actress who is also a brand has made a deal with the devil, and the devil collects in range.”

She was quiet for a long moment.

When she lifted her head there was no defeat anywhere in her. There was, instead, a strange light: the light of a woman who has just been told the truth about herself by someone with no reason to soften it.

“Not… qualified.” She turned the words over once, tasting the metal. And then she laughed. “When you insult a person, you do it with remarkable precision.”

“It wasn’t an insult. It was a diagnosis.”

“Is there a prescription?”

“Two.” He sat, and reached for the pad on the side table, and began to write. “The long course you have no time for: go live. Take a semester of being nobody, of bad jobs and strangers and griefs that aren’t scheduled; fill the reservoir your acting keeps draining. That one is for after.”

“The short course, I can start tonight.”

Her eyes came up, bright as a switched-on marquee.

Acting lesson, first session · room two, river side · Saturday, nine o’clock · four prescriptions

The short course what a working actress can fix between now and the audition

1The biography. For the part you’re about to play, write the woman whole: her habits, her catchphrase, the small motion her hands make when she’s cornered. Then let her into the room. How does she drink that tea? How does she look at a man she has decided to study? Play her on Tuesdays, not premieres.

2Crying in gears. Real grief has a gearbox: the eyes going red first, the machinery starting; then the mouth losing its nerve and the voice going unstable; and only then, the breaking. Skip to third gear from a standstill and the audience doesn’t weep, it winces. You have been jumping to third gear for six years.

3The memory bank. Don’t play “sad.” Bank a real one, something that actually hurt, kept private, kept exact; and when the director calls for tears, go and get the feeling, not the display of it. The body will do the rest honestly, because it already knows how.

4The news-anchor problem. Your line delivery is too clean: every syllable ironed, the idol-diction training sitting on every word. People who are alive hesitate; they um, they ah— they that is— and they drop consonants when it matters. Record yourself talking to a friend tonight. Play it against your drama takes. The gap between the two recordings is the gap between you and the part.

Homework set at the door: the biography in writing by Thursday; the two recordings by whenever she stops being frightened of them.

— the diagnosis, the method, and the homework delivered by a chairman on a Saturday night, in a club he does not yet own, to a student who arrived believing she was something else entirely.

He ran her through the third prescription on the spot.

“Close your eyes. Find one thing that genuinely hurt you. Don’t tell me what it is. Just go there.”

She closed her eyes. Ten seconds. Fifteen.

And the room watched the rims of her eyes go quietly, accurately red.

“Stop.” His voice, even. “What are you feeling right now?”

“It — aches.” Barely above a whisper.

“That’s the entire mechanism. On a set, when the director calls for tears, you don’t think now I cry. You go and fetch that. Every time. The audience will never know why it looks real. It looks real because it is.”

She breathed out long, blinked the tide back down, and looked at him with something new in her face: a student’s respect, arriving sideways, wearing the disguise of a woman being difficult.

And then, abruptly, she laughed.

“What?”

“A date?”

“Obviously.” The blink, unrepentant, feather-light. “I dressed like this. I arrived half an hour early. I practiced my smile in a mirror. What else would a person call it?”

And there is no answering that. She says it with her whole face open. The boldest move available is telling the plain truth about your own calculations, and she plays it like a courtesy.

He looked at her, and laughed too. “Then: is Yoon-a satisfied with her date?”

He was quiet for two seconds.

“What do you think?”

From a man with the better part of a trillion won behind his name, that is—” she searched, found it, “—that is a very comfortable thing to be given. You made me feel like a person worth hearing out. “Second.” The smile went soft and matter-of-fact. “You are genuinely, unfairly handsome. I checked twice.”

Delivered like a weather report. She grades listening first and faces second, which is either the most flattering ordering available to a man or the most efficient attack; there is no telling which, and she knows there is no telling which.

“Then I’ll ask mine,” he said. “What did Yoon-a come here tonight wanting?”

She didn’t blink, didn’t drop the smile, didn’t rearrange anything.

Not a confession. Not a probe. Not a request. A preference, stated at par, with the exits clearly marked: advance, and it means everything; retreat, and it was ever only friendship, said plainly, between colleagues.

The room’s atmosphere turned a corner. Not into ambiguity; into something rarer: two people, evenly armed, discovering they enjoy the rally.

Not flirtation, exactly — the pleasure of an even match: you come, I go; one advances, one gives ground; neither rushes the attack, and neither is afraid of the return.

He stood first.

She stood there a second, caught between laughing and something warmer than laughing.

“You really do understand everything.”

She turned, took two steps, stopped with her back to him.

“That is what I meant. Exactly that.” A beat. “But I’m not in any hurry, Song Ji-ho. The line of women you already have to keep company with is long enough without me holding a number ticket.”

And she went, unhurried, the silver heels keeping their time all the way down the corridor, and she did not look back, which was its own final line.

He stood in the doorway of room two and watched the corridor take her, and then he laughed, once, alone, in genuine appreciation.

Technique. Real technique. She knows her strengths to the centimeter, she knows when to press and when to lift, and she plays everything so openly that guarding against her feels like an insult, and probing her feels redundant, because she’s already told you the truth twice. An opponent you cannot defend against and do not want to. What a thing to be handed on a Saturday.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the car going north, Im Yoon-a leaned her head against the seat and watched the city spend its lights, and then, with nobody to perform it for, laughed out loud.

“I was afraid of being distracted.” Aigo, this man. He admits the attraction, and installs it as my accomplishment, as if I did the distracting. He was moved, and played it as discipline. He surrendered, and made the surrender feel like a favor to him.

She counted the evening’s findings on her fingers, the way her mother had taught her to count anything that mattered.

One: he respects the person in front of him, and makes it look like the most natural thing in the room; which it is not, in this industry, for a woman in her line of work.

Two: the face. Entered into evidence twice, verified twice.

Three: he is serious. Homework on a Saturday, from a man who could have bought the Saturday instead.

Four: he can solve what he looks at. The crying gears, the anchor problem: a fix for each leak, named in one evening.

Five, she wiggled the thumb, five: the man can flirt. A class of his own. Possibly tenured.

And then the accounting cooled, because she is who she is, and the ledger closed itself.

These five are enough to make a woman fall in love. They are not enough to make a woman share. That is the wall Sica eonni signed a three-year lease against, and I watched her sign it with my own ears. And past the wall, the buried things: what he shows no one, or has shown exactly three people, and none of them will tell me. Oh, I want to dig. The digging itself is half of what I cannot stop smiling about.

She touched one ear. It was still warm, which was ridiculous, and private, and hers.

Tonight, call it a draw. On points, maybe. But the week is his to move in. Which day does a man like that choose? What will he have prepared when he does? Whatever it is, it sets what I am allowed to want next time. Song Ji-ho, the clock is running. Impress me.
