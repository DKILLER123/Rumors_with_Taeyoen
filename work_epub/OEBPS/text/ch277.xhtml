Chapter Two Hundred Seventy-Seven

Ms. Jiyeon’s Private Lesson

Sunday · 17:20 Napa · the gravel lane outside the co-op a car to return, a debt nobody can itemise

The old woman who had cut the engraving came out to see them off with a rag in her hand and a smudge of silver polish along one thumb. She looked at Jiyeon’s face, then at Ji-ho’s, then at the two of them standing close enough in the gravel that neither could step back without admitting something, and she said a sentence in English at the speed people talk when they have stopped caring whether they are understood.

“She says we look like the kind of couple she drew in her notebooks at twenty and stopped drawing at thirty-four, because reality disappointed her.” Ji-ho laughed, briefly, and translated the rest of it with the ease of a man who had been answering to English his whole life and to Korean all his adult one. “Then she says: hold on to it.”

“What does that mean, hold on to it?”

“That’s the part she said twice.”

Jiyeon had planned, on this occasion, to be the composed one. She had rehearsed a smile. The smile arrived, did the work it was supposed to do for two seconds, and then her eyes went hot and her chin did something she could not order back into line, and she turned away half-way through it and pressed the heel of her hand under her eye like a woman with a smudge on her glasses.

My darling girl. My own.

She knew — had always known, and had never once let anybody say it out loud where she could hear — that this had not begun clean. It had begun with a calculation so plain she had been able to write it on the back of an envelope: a man like that, and a girl in a group the country had decided to hate, and the arithmetic of what one could do for the other. For a name. For money. For an end to the years of being tired. For a harbour with a roof on it, and nobody’s schedule in the way.

That had been the door she walked in through. It was not the house she was standing in now. What she had felt on the gravel in a man’s shirt with polishing rag in her hand, and in the vineyard, and in the car with his neck under her cheek, was not a thing anybody had arranged, and not a thing she could itemise for the person who had paid for it. It was hot and unmanageable and hers, and the man she was crying in front of had his hand at the back of her neck and was waiting, with infinite patience, for her to stop being embarrassed about it.

“How can you,” she said, into his collar, where her voice went to pieces completely, “keep saying things — I can’t — in public, in front of strangers, in front of my secretary —”

“People are looking.”

“Let them look!”

She said it with the full volume of a woman who had already lost the argument with her own face, and then, realising exactly how that sounded, lifted her head, discovered the old woman still standing there with an expression of great kindness, and put both hands over her own face and made a small sound of surrender between her fingers.

He patted her back the way you pat a horse that has finally stopped pulling: evenly, and without hurrying it. When she got her face under control again there was red around her eyes and the chain at her throat had slipped under her shirt, and she touched it once, under the fabric, without meaning to, and then went in through the side door of the barn to find a mirror that did not exist.

The front room of the workshop had a counter, and behind the counter a willow basket of the maker’s own smaller work, the things she made at the kitchen table for the pleasure of it: leaf things, vine things, a row of hooks and a pair of earrings shaped like the tips of grape leaves, serrated at the edge, so thinly struck that the light came through parts of them.

Chae Soo-bin stood in front of that basket for exactly as long as a person stands in front of something they are going to have to stop standing in front of. She took out her wallet, looked at it — the particular way a person looks at their own wallet, which is not looking, it is remembering — and put it back, and set down the earring she had lifted, and turned around with an expression of pleasant nothing that fooled the room except for the one man watching her do it.

He said nothing about it. He asked the maker’s wife for the price, wrote it on the back of a receipt, and put the receipt in his coat.

Sunday · 18:40 Calistoga · the north end of the valley, where the steam comes up on its own a town built around warm water and the privacy of it

Calistoga is the last small town before the mountains close the valley, and it has spent a century and a half selling the same product: hot water, and the ability to not be seen in it. The house Soo-bin had booked was the best-known place in town for exactly that reason. There was no lobby with a queue in it. There were gates, a gravel path, and beyond each gate a courtyard with its own wooden fence and its own pool, and behind the fence the pool was steaming, because it came out of the ground that way and nobody had thought to charge extra for it.

The car stopped at the gate. Soo-bin turned off the engine, checked the mirrors out of habit, and sat with her hands in her lap for a moment before she opened her door.

“Hoejang-nim. There’s a café two streets over with a phone. I’ll be there until you send word.”

“You’re not coming in?”

The question had come out of Jiyeon before she had decided to ask it, the way a kind thing usually does, and Soo-bin went still in the open doorway in the way of a woman doing arithmetic about her own place in the world at speed.

“It’s a couples’ bath,” she said, and smiled, and shut the door softly enough that it made no sound. “I’d be a piece of furniture. Go on — have a good evening.”

That is what nunchi is for — the reading of a room so quickly that the person doing it can pretend it never happened. Nobody had taught Chae Soo-bin the word; she had been born inside it, had spent eleven weeks sharpening it, and had just declined a private hot spring on a cold evening because a piece of furniture does not bathe with the guests.

Jiyeon’s face went through four stages in a second and a half and settled, finally, on apology.

“I didn’t mean—”

“She meant it kindly,” Ji-ho said. “That’s what you’re upset about. Not that you asked.”

He got out, and walked around the boot, and stood for a moment where the roof of the car put him at the same height as the woman still sitting behind the wheel. Soo-bin looked up. His face was entirely unremarkable, which was the problem: it was the face that had, in eleven weeks of working for it, been shown things in filing cabinets that would have ended other people’s careers, and it had never once been told to react.

“Go in and soak,” he said. “On the clock. Tonight the hours you work are the hours you’re in the water, and if anyone from the office asks, I wrote it down.”

“Hoejang-nim, I’m not — there’s nothing wrong with me, I don’t need—”

“Soo-bin-ah.”

She stopped. Not because the tone had been loud; it hadn’t been loud. Because the tone had been the tone she had heard him use, exactly twice, on a man who had been three seats away and had also stopped talking mid-sentence.

She laughed at herself instead of arguing, which was a wise order to do it in. “Thank you. I’ll — I’ll take the second gate along, then.”

“That’s right.”

He went in with Jiyeon under the arch, and Soo-bin stood in the gravel with her hand on the roof of the car, eyes closed, face toward the last of the sun, for a long time — long enough that the driver in the second car behind her put his gear lever back into drive with the soft click of a man deciding not to ask.

The courtyard of the couple’s bath was not large and had not tried to be. What it had was judgement: a path of flat stones laid so you stepped where the water had sprayed and nowhere else; three stands of bamboo in a corner, cut back hard so the sound of them stayed small; a wooden pavilion with a roof that had been oiled the same autumn for forty years; and between all of it, the pool, cut from a single piece of local stone, with the water in it so clear that the chisel marks on the bottom were perfectly visible under a moving floor of steam.

Jiyeon crouched at the edge in her socks, put two fingers in, and let out a breath like a puncture.

“Oh — it’s perfect. It’s exactly — mm—” She turned her wet fingers on him accusingly. “This is how warm it’s meant to be?”

“It comes up at that temperature.”

“And you just — put it in a pool — in the ground—”

“That is what the last hundred and fifty years have been for, yes.”

He crouched down beside her, and she did not move away, and their shoulders touched, and she looked at the water with the bright suspicious face of a woman about to be spoiled.

“Now you’re not afraid I’ll take advantage of you?”

“We said — bathing!” Her ears went pink to prove the point. “Bathing, and looking at bamboo. That’s the plan.”

“The plan is good. I like the plan.” He stood, brushed the gravel off one knee, and raised both hands at her, palms out, the universal gesture of a man surrendering to a superior officer. “You soak. I’ll sit in the pavilion and be virtuous and read the notice about the water’s mineral content.”

He turned to go, unhurried, entirely pleased with himself, and had taken exactly three steps before she got up off her heels and caught the back of his shirt.

“Ah—” Her voice came out at the volume of a moth. “You can — you can get in too. If you want. It’s — it’s the same water.” She was looking past his shoulder at the bamboo, at the steam, anywhere. “But you have to keep your word. That’s the condition. You said you would.”

“I said I would.”

“Then—”

“Then I’ll keep it.” He put his hand on the back of her head, under the hair, and bent her forehead down to his, and stood there a moment in the steam with his eyes closed, and when he let her go she had to sit down on the stone edge of the pool, not from weakness, but because her legs had made a decision without consulting her.

The changing rooms were separate, as they were told to be, and had a bench and a hook and a shelf of folded robes in them, and nothing else to look at but a small mirror over the basin. Jiyeon put on the robe — cotton, thick, white with a grey stripe — and looked at herself for a while, and then tugged the collar closed with both hands and pressed it flat, and stood there breathing, and thought: I have been on a stage in front of twenty thousand people and I have never once needed to breathe like this.

She opened the door.

He was already in. In to the chest, both arms folded along the far rim of the pool, head back against the stone, eyes shut, his hair down and wet and a shade darker than it should have been, with the steam going across his face in slow pieces. The lines of him looked soft in it — not younger, softer, which was worse in a way, because she had never once seen him soft.

She went down the steps on the balls of her feet, holding the robe’s sleeve in her teeth so it wouldn’t rustle, and got within a hand’s reach, and was gathering herself for what she had decided would be a fright so terrible he would fall over — and her wrist was taken in the water and pulled, and she went in up to the chin with a sound that woke the bamboo.

“An ambush?”

“How did you — you had your eyes shut!”

“I know how the steps creak.” He had both hands on her now, steadying her, and the water came up around her ribs and then her shoulders, and the heat of it went through her like somebody turning a lamp on in a room she had forgotten was there. “I’ve been counting them since you came out of that door.”

“You horrible—”

“Mm.”

“— that’s not an apology.”

“It isn’t.”

She got herself settled against the stone where the edge had been worn smooth by a hundred and fifty years of other people’s elbows, and slid down until the water was at her chin and only her face was in the air, and made a small helpless noise at the back of her throat, not quite a word, the noise a cat makes when it has been betrayed by a warm place and has decided to forgive it.

“Is it all right?”

“It’s—” She had to stop and get her face back. “It’s the best thing anyone has ever done for me. Don’t tell anyone I said that. It’ll go to your head.”

“It’s already gone to my head. That’s why the water’s over there and I’m over here.”

The light in the courtyard came on by itself, low and amber, in the lanterns set into the fence posts, and the dusk came up behind it. Somewhere over the fence a cricket was working out an argument it had been having all summer. The steam moved. Nobody in the world needed anything from either of them for the next hour and a half, and both of them had worked out, independently, that this was the first time in a very long year that this was true.

They floated. Her hair floated out around her on the surface in a dark fan that he kept, without meaning to, pushing away from her face with one knuckle.

“Honey.”

“Mm?”

“Thank you.”

He turned his head. She was watching her own reflection in the water and not his face, which was how she said the serious things; he had learned that in Los Angeles in the back of a van at two in the morning.

“For the water? It’s the valley’s, technically.”

“For believing me.” One shoulder came up and went down again. “For being willing to spend money and time and — and your name, on me. On my unnies. You have no obligation to any of that. You could have let the whole thing fall where it fell and nobody would have written you a letter about it.”

Under a wooden roof, at the volume of steam

“There is no version of me where that’s an option, so let’s not spend the evening on it.”

“Why?”

“Because you’d ask me the same question about me and I’d have to sit here looking smug about it, and the water’s too good to waste on that.”

“…Answer me anyway.”

“Because you’re mine. Your business is my business, your people are my people, and if you thank me for it again I’m going to put you under.”

She went down under it herself, three breaths, and came up sputtering and laughing with her hair off her face, and the water rang on the stone for a while after, and the crickets, professionally, looked the other way.

Later — much later, when the heat had got into her so far that her thoughts moved through treacle — she said the thing she had been carrying into the courtyard without knowing it.

“I’m frightened a bit.”

“Of the water? It’s been here since before the town and it has never once taken a life.”

“Of waking up.” She was very still. “That’s what I’m frightened of. That this is a dream I’ve built in my own head out of kindness and grapes, and I’ll open my eyes tomorrow and you’ll be Song Ji-ho on his ninth floor and I’ll be the girl the whole country is spitting at, and none of this—” Her hand came up and waved at the lanterns, the fence, the steam, him, in one gesture that took in roughly the last five months “— will have happened at all. That I invented the happy part because the other part was too much to hold.”

He looked at her for a while with his chin on his forearm.

“Then pinch yourself.”

“…What?”

“Pinch yourself. Hard. See whether it hurts.”

“That’s the most — that’s not — you can’t test a dream with your own fingernail, everybody knows—”

He reached over and bit her shoulder, above the waterline, where the skin is thin and the surprise is total: not hard enough to mark, hard enough to be a fact.

“Ah! What are you doing — in public — in a—”

“There’s nobody here.”

“You bited me!”

“Does it hurt?”

“Of course it hurts!”

“Then it isn’t a dream.” He took the wet face of her hand and put it flat against his own chest under the water, over the ribs, where the heart was going at the pace of a man doing nothing more strenuous than being obstinate, and kept it there. “Park Ji-yeon. Listen to it. It’s yours, it’s real, and so is the rest of it. Me clearing your name and your group’s name is not a thing you invented, it’s a thing with a date on it and a folder in a safe in Seocho and a lawyer’s bills going through my company. When we get back, the truth comes out, and you can stand in the sun and be rude to the weather. Understood?”

Her eyes went red again, and she had nothing left at all for being embarrassed about it, and she got up on one elbow under the lantern light and kissed him, and it was not a small kiss, and it went on long enough that they were both breathless when the water started cooling around them and they broke apart, foreheads together, entirely ruined for speech.

“We agreed,” he said, in a voice that had gone an octave into the gravel, “that this was bathing.”

“You said I wasn’t allowed to let you take advantage of me.” She was grinning at his chin. “I never said I couldn’t kiss you. There’s no rule about kissing.”

“There is a rule. It’s the first one. It’s ‘don’t make me drown you in a public pool’.”

“Nobody’s watching.”

“Ji-yeon-ah.”

“Mm?”

“You’re going to be the death of a man who has done nothing wrong.”

He turned his head away, out of plain self-defence, and put the back of his hand over his eyes like a monk under siege, and she came up behind him, folded her wet arms around his shoulder from the side, and put her mouth against the place under his ear where the water had not reached, and said it, small and utterly shameless.

“You’re so good to me.”

He didn’t answer. He took her hand out of the water, counted her fingers into his own, and sat with them interlaced on the stone until the steam got thin.

They came out through the gate at twenty past nine, both of them pink, loose in the joints, walking like people with no trains to catch, which they were not — an aeroplane was in fact waiting for them with a hall of cameras under it, but not for another day and a half, and for tonight it was somebody else’s problem.

Soo-bin was at the gate with her phone dark in her hand the moment the latch went, and had his door open before she reached it, which was the sort of reflex that takes years and cannot be taught to anybody who hasn’t wanted it.

“Hoejang-nim. Back up the hill?”

“Back up the hill.”

“Your hair’s still wet, both of you — there are towels in the door pockets.”

“I’ll die of heat, unnie, honestly.”

“You’ll die of a draft at sixty miles an hour with wet hair,” said the rear-view mirror, without any mercy at all, “and then I’ll have to write it in a report.”

Jiyeon dried her hair with a towel in the dark, in the back of a car, while a woman she liked enormously watched her do it in a mirror, and there was no part of that sentence she could explain to anybody, so she didn’t, and the car climbed.

“Honey.”

“Mm.”

“Does she always work? From the moment we get up to when we’re already in bed? She doesn’t have any time that’s hers.”

He looked at the back of the front seat, which was a head held very still in the exact posture of a woman who was not listening to anything at all and had both hands ready on it.

“She does,” he said. “It’s a hard job.”

“Then pay her more.” There was that calculation she did, quick and shameless and entirely about other people: “Give her a bonus. A real one. Not the little company present where they send you a pen.”

“Out of your salary?”

“What salary!” She hit his arm, then apologised to his arm. “You have a whole building full of money and you count it in your sleep. The richer people are the meaner they get, everybody knows that, it’s — it’s practically science.”

“That isn’t science.”

“That’s what I mean.”

He said nothing at all about the bonus, which, if you had known him for eleven weeks, was the same as agreeing, and faster.

Sunday · 22:05 Calistoga · the top floor of the best hotel in town one suite, one terrace, one very bad plan she had made at 18:40

The hotel on the ridge was the sort of place that has eight rooms and prices them as though the eighth one comes with a title. Soo-bin had taken the top-floor suite with the terrace, without being asked, because she had worked out at the booking stage — three days earlier, in a lobby in Sonoma, in the only ten spare minutes she had had — that a couple who were being photographed in every other country on earth would prefer a balcony they could close a door behind.

“Soo-bin-ah. Nothing tomorrow until the car checks at eleven. Sleep, and if you wake before noon, that is a medical event and I want it reported.”

“Thank you, Hoejang-nim.”

“And take the second night off the clock. The room’s paid.”

The look that crossed the secretary’s face was quickly dismantled, but he got it, and so did the girl beside him, and neither of them said anything about it in the elevator, where there was a mirror, and where Jiyeon was suddenly very interested in the floor numbers.

Somebody had to say something, and she had said it first, at the headrest of a car, under some vines, with her chin on upholstery where it was safe. She had promised him compensation, in words, on purpose, and then the whole evening had gone into hot water and had come out smelling of sulphur and happiness, and now the doors opened on a room with a bed in it, and the promise had acquired an address.

There was one time, and that was with half a bottle of something in me, and the room was dark, and it’s not — it isn’t the same. Tonight I know exactly what I’m agreeing to, and I agreed first, and I can’t take it back now, and I can feel my face doing it.

“You’re hot again. The bath’s done that to you.”

“It hasn’t!”

“You’re red to your collar.”

“I am fine,” she said, with the desperate dignity of a woman whose voice went up half a key on the last word, and went out onto the terrace, and did not come back in for a while.

The valley at night is a different country from the valley at noon. Below the lights of the hotel there was nothing but the shape of it, and the vine rows in the dark were like somebody had thrown a whole bucket of ink across the floor of it and let it run in straight lines; a handful of farm lights stood out in it at enormous distances, one here, one four miles off; and above all of that, with nothing whatsoever in the way, was a sky full of stars in the numbers people use when they are trying not to say that they were frightened as children.

“Honey — come here. Come here. Look at it. You can see where the road goes, you can see it, it’s like a—”

“I can see it.”

“Do you like it?”

He put his chin on the top of her head and his arms round her waist and his chin stayed exactly where it was, which is a way of answering that a woman who has been asked a hundred careless questions can read.

“I like it,” he said. “I like that you’re standing on a terrace in California in my shirt, complaining about the stars.”

She turned round in his arms, because the sentence had been too much to take from behind, and put both hands in his hair, and stood looking at him with an expression that had no defence whatsoever in it, and said: “Honey.”

“Mm.”

“I’m going to take a shower. And then — and then I’ll—”

“You’ll what?”

“Make it up to you!” The words came out at a volume that brought a light on two hundred yards away and then put it out again. “That’s what I said. In the car. I said it.”

“I know what you said.” He put both hands on her face, and his thumbs on the corner of her eyes, and he was looking at her the way a man looks at a thing he has waited a long time for and has decided, on inspection, to be patient about anyway. “You’re shaking.”

“I’m not — I’m not tired, either, so if that’s the excuse—”

“That isn’t an excuse. That’s a doctor’s note. You’ve been on your feet since six, in a vineyard, in the sun, and then you spent two hours in water that would fell a horse. In the shower. Loosen up.”

“And you’ll be—”

“Right here. Reading the mineral content of the water. Go.”

She went up on her toes and put her mouth to his and took the last half of the argument with her, and ran off across the room in the bare way of a woman who has decided that if she stops moving she will start thinking, and the bathroom light came on, and the water.

He stood on the terrace with his hands on the rail and breathed in cold air for a while, because he was capable of it and because the alternative was going in there, and in there was not what she had asked for — not tonight, not with her shaking, not out of a promise.

Cho had called at eleven in the morning his time to say the handover files were logged and the hospital in Sinchon had been paid a year forward, which is the sort of sentence you do not think about standing up in California. In two days there would be a hall of cameras at Incheon that had already written the captions for the photographs they intended to take. The album was six weeks out and had a war attached to it. Every piece on the board was where he had put it, and the next three moves would decide whether the board could be turned over.

And then, underneath all of that, unhurried, in no particular order: a girl in a man’s shirt on the gravel outside a barn, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. A girl saying I believe you into an upholstery seam at forty miles an hour. A girl on her toes in a doorway.

Yes. All of it. The whole bill.

The water stopped. The door opened a crack, and half a face came out of it, with wet hair lying on the forehead and skin done pink from the inside, and the voice that came with it was very small.

“Honey.”

“Mm.”

“I — I didn’t bring any pyjamas.”

“Wear the robe. There’s a heat pump and it’s set to seventy-four, and if you say one word about the electricity bill I’m putting you under the bath myself.”

“…Okay.”

She came out five minutes later in the white robe with the towel twisted into her hair like a turban and the collar done up so tight that she looked like a very young doctor who has been sent home for the first time. She crossed the carpet to him with her head down, and stood in front of the lamp, and worked the belt of the robe with both hands, in and out, in and out.

He reached up and undid the towel.

“It’ll dry on you.”

“It’ll dry on the pillow,” he said, “and your head will ache at four in the morning and you’ll pretend it doesn’t and then you’ll be horrible to everybody in the room until breakfast. Sit.”

She sat on the sofa like a girl being marched, and he found the dryer in a drawer in the bathroom, and came back and plugged it into the wall by the floor lamp, and put his hand in her hair to get the length of it, and turned it on.

Hot air. His fingers going through the whole length of it, and once in a while the flat of his knuckle against her scalp, and the heat between her shoulders where the robe had gone open at the back. She sat with her hands on her knees and a heart going like an animal in a box, and did not once get told to relax, which is the only thing that has ever made it worse.

He dried it properly — all of it, the roots at the nape, the ends, the small short hairs at her temples that nobody else had ever dried, and then he put the dryer on the couch, and ran his fingers through it once more to lay it right, and bent over the crown of her head, and left his mouth there in the damp hair for a beat longer than the courtesy of the moment required.

“There.”

“There what.”

“It’s done. You’re dry. Turn around and tell me what face you’re making.”

“I’m not making a face!”

She was, of course, making a face; she had been making it under the dryer, and the turning-around did nothing to fix it. The collar of the robe had come open with all the moving, and her collarbone sat in it with a patch of skin above it so pale in the lamp that it looked like a strike through a page, and she saw him look at it, and — for the first time in an entire day of shamelessness — went red from the neck up.

“Honey. Can I ask you something stupid?”

“Ask me.”

“You’ve never — you haven’t once — since we started, you have never once made me feel like I’m a thing you picked up because you could.” She had her knees up on the sofa by then, and she was talking at the corner of the cushion. “I feel respected. Every day. And I don’t know how to say that to a person without it sounding like — like I’m thanking them for the bare minimum.”

“It isn’t the bare minimum. It’s rarer than the album.”

“Rich people are arrogant.” The words were out of her before she had permission for them, and she took them back as fast as she could. “Not — I don’t mean — you aren’t — I mean as a class, generally—”

“Say it. You were nearly clever.”

“They look down on what we do. On the schedule, on the fan service, on — on the fact that I have to be pleasant to men I don’t like in rooms I didn’t choose, and that’s my job, and if I say so I’m ungrateful, and if I don’t say so I’m — I’m the girl who got lucky and needs to know it. I’ve had it said to me, the whole year, in every tone, by everyone from a music-show PD to my own—” She stopped, and started again, quieter. “So. You have every reason in the world to be like that, and you’re not, and I lie awake trying to work out what the catch is, and I can’t find one, and that’s more frightening than the bullying.”

He took her face in both hands, and her hands came out of the way of his thumbs, and her face sat in his palms with the eyes going red at once out of pure frustration at how well that works.

“Ji-yeon-ah.”

“Mm.”

“People who have been through the cold of it keep the respect for everybody after.”

“The cold of what?”

“The way people are.” He thought about it, because she deserved the sentence finished, and because he had a habit of finishing things that a man raised in a house with a grandfather’s hands in it does not get out of. “When you’re up, they’re around. When you’re down, they’re not. That’s the whole of it, and it isn’t a Korean thing and it isn’t a money thing, it’s just what people do when nobody is watching them do it.”

“When I’m up, they’re around.” She turned it over, once, and then it stopped being somebody else’s sentence. “When I’m up, they’re around. When I’m down—”

They had all been around, at the top. The whole industry had been around, generous, funny, familiar: the year the group was red hot, everybody she met at a broadcast wanted a photo with her, and uncles and aunties, all of them, and PDs who remembered her birthday. And after, after there were five names she could count without taking her shoes off. Ji-eun, who had picked up at two in the morning and never once asked her to explain. Dani, who had thrown her own schedule at the wall beside the group’s. Ha-ra, who had said the plain thing on a broadcast where saying it cost her something. Sunny unni and Yoon-a unni, who had both managed it in such a way that nobody in the press could find the seam to pull on.

Everyone else had developed a schedule. Everyone else had sudden, urgent business in another city.

And if I make it? If this all comes out and I’m standing in the sun with money in my account and my own name in front of me — do I look down on them? Do I find out who wasn’t at the airport and make them sorry?

No. There isn’t the time. There isn’t the point. You saw what people are when you were nothing; the useful lesson is not to be them and not to be surprised by them either. It costs nothing to be polite to a man who is going to be rude to you anyway.

She turned into him and put her forehead under his chin and stayed there, and both of them were quiet for long enough that the terrace door needed closing, which he did one-handed, and then the quiet stopped being the room’s problem.

“I understand,” she said into his chest, and then, a second later, with a whole different voice: “You haven’t asked me about it yet, though.”

“Asked you about what.”

“The thing you don’t know.” She was trying so hard not to look at him that she looked at the lamp instead. “The — the thing that Ji-eun unnie can’t do.”

“You told me on the headrest of a car that you were going to teach me something.”

“I’m a teacher,” said Jiyeon, with the dignity of a woman whose ears were the colour of the inside of a grapefruit. “I have been teaching you for months and you have been failing.”

He put his chin down on top of her head and let the laugh go into her hair, and she came up onto her knees on the sofa, and put her mouth at his ear, and said the thing she had been saving since Sunday morning, in a voice so soft it wasn’t going to reach a wall, let alone a microphone.

His eyes went flat and dark and stayed that way for a second, and when he spoke the voice had gone somewhere lower down the room.

“Are you sure.”

“Mm.” She nodded, and then shook her head at her own nod, and then nodded again with the whole of her face.

He stood up with her in his arms sideways, the way you carry a child across a puddle, and she put her face into the hollow of his throat and made a sound like somebody losing a bet she had wanted to lose, and the lamp went out on its timer, and the stars over the valley did their arithmetic, and the night in the room got long.

Private lesson · taught at the far end of the sofa · after eleven

Marjaryasana–Bitilasana “cat and cow”

1On hands and knees: wrists under shoulders, knees under hips.

2Inhale — cow. Chest forward, head lifts, belly goes toward the floor, sit bones reach back and up.

3Exhale — cat. Round the spine to the ceiling, chin to the chest, press the floor away with both hands.

4Repeat, and let the breath do the counting rather than the head.

Benefits, according to the instructor: opens a chest that has been folded over a mixing desk for a decade, undoes the shoulders of a woman who carries a bag for a living, stretches the belly, feeds the spine, and takes the tiredness out of a body that has been awake since six.

— taught by Ms Park, who has wanted to be somebody’s teacher for eleven months and has never once had a pupil this slow.

“That’s yoga!”

“Obviously it’s yoga. What did you think it was? Come here — hands down, knees where I put them, and stop looking at me, look at your own back, it’s on fire, that’s the point.”

“I’ve got it. This is easy. She really can’t do this? The woman does a live broadcast in heels.”

“Ji-eun unnie has hips like a door and the coordination of a wet staircase.” Jiyeon’s face went into a cat’s round-backed arch with a satisfaction that was frankly aggressive. “She hates yoga. She told the Pilates instructor she had a headache and left. That is the only flexibility she believes in.”

“You’re enjoying this too much.”

“Shh, you’re out of breath, that’s not correct breathing. And there’s another one. I’m teaching you the other one.”

Second movement · the one she picked for the shoulders

Bhujangasana “cobra”

1Lie face down, legs together, tops of the feet flat, hands under the ribs with the elbows pinned in against the body.

2Inhale and press the floor down — the arms do a quarter of the work, the back does the rest. Chest lifts, pelvis stays heavy on the mat.

3Shoulders down the back of the neck. Neck long, not cranked. Look at the wall in front of you, not the ceiling.

4Hold four breaths. Come down on the last one, chin to chest, and don’t be proud about it.

What it is for: opens the front of a body that spends its life leaning toward a screen, lifts a rounded back, stretches the belly, gives the spine blood, and — per the instructor, who counted this twice on her fingers — makes a man in his own hotel suite say something in English he will deny in the morning.

He held it, and he breathed, and at the third breath he found out — with the distant, indignant interest of a man who has been proven wrong about his own body — that the ache along the tops of his shoulders from nine hours in a vineyard had begun, during the fourth exhale of the first movement, to stop being a thing he was carrying.

So this is what they do. No wonder every actress on the peninsula has a mat in her living room and a bad temper about it. Whatever this is, it does something to the shape of a person, and it takes about eleven minutes.

“You’re smirking. You’re smirking in a cobra.”

“I’m breathing.”

“You’re breathing at me.” She folded down on the mat with her hair everywhere and her face in her arms, and laughed until the whole sofa moved, and kicked her heels once like a child, and then lay still with her cheek on her own forearm and said, in a voice that had gone soft at the end, “Again. From the top.”

The stars over the valley went round the way stars do, taking their time about it, and the last of the light in the neighbouring vineyard went out of its own accord at about one, and neither of them noticed; and if the woman two doors down heard the second lesson, the one with the counting in it, she had the sense to put a pillow over it, because in this business you learn early that the happy ones are nobody’s business but their own.

In two days there would be a gate at Incheon with thirty cameras on it, and the captions had already been written by people who had not met either of them.

Nobody in the valley had yet worked out that the storm had a date, and that the date had been chosen in a boardroom in Seocho by a man who had been waiting five months to use it.
