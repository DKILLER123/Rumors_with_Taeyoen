Singapore. The Indoor Stadium on the Kallang side of the river, where the Girls & Peace world tour had put in for the week, in a harbor the old Chinese pilots used to call the Dragon-Teeth Gate, because there used to be a rock at the mouth of it that looked like exactly that, and because sailors name a place by what it can do to a hull.

Rehearsal had just ended. The last of the soundcheck’s held note went out over the empty seats and never came back, and the floor went to the crew: cases latched, cables coiled, risers marked in tape that glinted under the work lights like a city seen from the air at night. Nine women came off the stage in loose formation, gathering bottles and cardigans and each other, the tour running in their legs the way a tide stays in sand.

Back at the hotel, before the dusk had finished turning the bay into hammered gold, Kim Tae-yeon’s phone rang. She looked at it once, said nothing, and took the call out in the corridor alone, the door closing on the last syllable of somebody’s joke.

When she came back, her face was doing arithmetic.

“The company just called.” She paused, in the specific way leaders pause when the next sentence belongs to the company and not to them. “The fourth mini album has been moved up. January. The title track is decided. ‘Mr.Mr.’”

For one second the suite was quiet enough to hear the air conditioning think.

Then the room lit up, face by face, like a stage coming on. A comeback meant work, stages, income, the golden exposure seam where one year is traded for the next: festivals, specials, year-end spotlights with the whole country’s remote controls in play. Kim Hyo-yeon whistled, low and appreciative, the whistle of a woman watching a wave she intends to catch.

“Work again. Good.”

Only Im Yoon-a said nothing.

She had gone to the window, and stood there with one fingertip resting against the glass, tapping it very lightly, at intervals, the way you test whether a surface is real. Below her, Singapore was running its evening lights along the water in long unbroken strings, a city with its collar open, and she looked at none of it.

Memory · what the window knows

The script had come to her in September with his name on the bag: My Love from the Star, Park Ji-eun’s ink, an alien, a promise crossing centuries, a vain top actress with a wound under the gloss. She had read two episodes and forgotten the arriving dishes. After that, everything she did was a transaction she kept telling herself wasn’t one.

There was a program: image release, comedy, the private wound, scene method. A formal audition before the writer, the director, the production. No guarantee, stated twice, in writing. There were lessons in rooms with the lights low: a diagnosis delivered gently — you are always Im Yoon-a; as an actress, not yet qualified — and homework taken like a soldier, and a grade she gave the evenings on her fingers in a car going home.

And there was the evening she tried to pay for it the way the industry has always paid, flat-palmed, like a bank draft, terms attached, and the man across the low table looked at the draft, and at her, and declined the math, and asked for the heart instead. She had walked out of that restaurant with everything she came with and one sentence following her down the corridor. Under somebody else’s fireworks, weeks later, she had paid the heart anyway.

None of it is written anywhere SM can read. That is the design of it, and also the trap: what she traded is invisible, and what it was traded for sits on the company’s calendar, and as of Wednesday evening the company has spent it.

Because that was the circle, wasn’t it. He gave her the script. She gave him everything she could reach of herself, including the parts she had sworn were not in the deal.

Then he went out and bought SM’s trainees, and the girl group that should have eaten those resources died in its cradle, and SM poured the resources back into Girls’ Generation, and the comeback moved up two months, and the window she had traded September through her own ribs to hold open was bricked over on a Wednesday evening by a phone call signed with her own group’s name.

Around the whole circle. And what is standing at the end of it, in the last corner, is me.

Was it… for nothing? All of it? The fourteen garments, the perfume, the offer, the heart?

A secret bargain has one design flaw: the other side of it can be spent by people who never knew the deal existed.

She stood at the window and said nothing, and did not move. If she spoke, it would have to be a question, and every question she had was addressed to a man three thousand kilometers north, and the answers all ran through the one room in Korea she could not enter without detonating it.

Out past the glass, Singapore laid out its whole beautiful evening for her, street by jeweled street, the harbor lights doubled in dark water, the Dragon-Teeth Gate running warm and gold.

She saw black.

(In Korea a full name runs three syllables almost without exception: one for the family, two for the given. Two-syllable full names are so rare that you could search a whole industry and come back with a parking ticket. History, the household register, and Confucius hold that door shut together. The register is built for three-part names, and a parent who insists on one given character anyway has chosen eccentricity, and will be understood to have done so.

The Confucian system is stricter still: a given name should carry a generation syllable — the one all the cousins of a generation share — plus a meaning syllable. Family, generation, meaning: the standard, respectable, classical shape, and the genealogy book will not seat you without it. History did the rest: in the old kingdom only the aristocracy and the scholar-officials owned full three-part names, commoners made do with childhood names and nicknames, and when the modern registers arrived and everyone suddenly had to have a name, the whole country copied the aristocracy’s format inside a generation.

Park Jin-young · the choreography, taught from the center of the room

“Let Me Down Slowly” · light and quick, keyed to the drums one arrangement, one shape, two troupes: the trainee five for the peninsula week, T-ara for America

1The arrangement is the map, so learn the map first. Solo piano up front, instruments joining lane by lane, everything falling away to the solo again at the end. Four-four, middle tempo. And the kit does not land on the beat; it comes in on the back half of it. Which decides everything: you do not attack this song. You ride it. The body stays light, the weight stays low, and the bounce never announces itself.

2The intro is eight counts, and the eight counts belong to the feet. Right heel, right heel; left heel, left heel. Knees soft. The body sways on the horizontal, like a boat that is happy at its mooring. And the arms (this is the discipline) hang dead weight at your sides. Do not decorate them. The drums live downstairs, and your hands have not been invited.

3The chorus is the memory point, and the memory point gets amplitude. The slide. The finger-gun, diagonal up, wrist locked at the top, and the eyes go up the barrel and stay there: the audience learns the move from your eyeline or not at all. Then the wave, waist to chest to arms, one vertebra teaching the next, hands drawing their arc from low to high.

4The finish is a small jump, both feet, arms opening overhead; the landing takes it all back through bent knees, in silence. The jump is loud, the landing is silent, and that contrast is the signature. If the room hears your landing, you have spent the ending’s whole fortune on the take-off.

He ran it clean twice, and then it was theirs. “Groups. First group, front and center. Go.”

Song Ji-ho stood in the first group, in the position where the light would be if there were lights.

Eight counts of intro: heel taps, soft knees, the horizontal sway. Arms hanging, weightless, not one gram of decoration. Into the chorus: the slide ran smooth as a drawer on felt, the finger-gun came up diagonal with the wrist locked and a look behind it that could have signed paperwork, and the wave went waist to chest to arms like water finding its level, the whole passage carried off without a single visible instruction reaching it from anywhere.

First pass. Near enough to zero deviation that the zero was visible from the walls.

Out of the corner of his eye, Park Jin-young watched him do it, and something in his chest filed a claim for which there was no paperwork and never had been.

A perfect idol, factory-assembled. One try. That does not happen; I have watched it not happen for thirty years.

And the proportions, look at him: the same eight counts I gave everybody, and in his body they come out with an extra cleanness, as if the counts were longer when they ran through him.

He stood dead center of the half-circle with his arms folded, and did not blink, and did not stop watching.

This was the self he had imagined being.

The one the face had voted down, decades ago, in a small room with a cassette deck and a mirror that pulled no punches. The one he had spent a career building out of other people’s bodies instead: a girl group’s line here, a boy group’s attack there, three national epidemics of rhythm constructed from a chair in a rehearsal room. It had come down the floor at him now at a walk, wearing somebody else’s twenty years, executing his own choreography back at him better than his memory had, and calling him hyung.

Park Jin-young watched, and did not blink, and started counting the eight counts along with him, the way you do when a thing is too good and too late and yours, all three at once.
