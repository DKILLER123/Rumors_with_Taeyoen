Chapter Two Hundred Forty-One

Red Velvet, Secured

August 9, 2013 SM Entertainment · the Chief Producer’s office, 6th floor a teacher, a former trainee, one USB drive — and a negotiation with teeth on both sides of the table

“Chief Producer — Chairman Song has arrived.”

Chairman Song.

Lee Soo-man looked up, and the title sat strangely in the air for a moment. Beyond the window-wall lay Gangnam’s ranked towers; sunlight crossed the rosewood desk and lit the framed photographs of SM’s generations of teams. The young man who had once stood in this very office and said, with impossible nerve, “Seonsaeng-nim — I want to go solo,” had grown into someone he now had to address by TITLE.

At the start he’d rated the boy a gifted seedling — fine voice, real ideas in his writing, reminiscent of a certain genius he’d once let slip through his fingers, perhaps even a shade beyond. What he had never imagined was the seedling breaking ground and becoming a tree that blotted out the sky. Two songs onto the American charts, shattering the old ceiling for Asian acts in the West. Then, within the same year, LOEN itself — a dreaming trainee transformed into the capital hand steering the peninsula’s largest music platform. If the phrase HEAVEN-SENT meant anything, it meant this.

He gathered his thoughts, tapped the desk once, and let his voice settle into its old steadiness.

“Show him in. And bring two cups of the good tea — the tin my friend from China sent.”

“Yes, Chief Producer.”

Moments later the door opened. Ji-ho entered in a black suit cut to the millimeter, the boyishness of the old days entirely gone from his face, replaced by the unhurried gravity of a man who owned things. And the instant his eyes found Lee Soo-man, all of it softened. His steps slowed. He inclined into a bow.

“Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim. It’s been too long.”

One word — SEONSAENG-NIM — and the tension went quietly out of the older man’s shoulders.

“Sit, Chairman Song.” He gestured at the sofa, mouth curving with genuine pleasure. “I never imagined that when we met again, you’d have come this far.”

“However far I go, you are still my teacher.” Ji-ho sat, and there was real feeling under the courtesy. “I’m grateful you broke your own rules to give me the solo chance. Perhaps SM and I were never fated to walk together—”

He paused, and his eyes were plain and honest.

“—but I have never once regretted my months at SM, and never once forgotten your hand on the ladder. The respect has not changed. It won’t.”

Lee Soo-man looked at the young man across from him, and the memory of the day Ji-ho left came back uninvited. Honestly — at the time, he had NOT regretted it. SM was the peninsula’s premier star factory precisely because its rules did not bend: team first, iron discipline, standardized development, no rookie contract rewritten for one boy’s talent, however bright. The system was the dynasty. One genius did not outweigh it. So when the boy asked to go, he had barely tried to hold him.

And then he had watched him cut through the industry like a blade through paper — the debut album people called a masterpiece, and lately Taeyeon and Tiffany carried onto the American charts on his songs — and on the nights when the whole internet chewed over THE GENIUS SM LET GO, yes, there had been regret. More than once, lying awake: if I had bent one rule, built one bespoke plan, kept one man — would SM be standing in the Western market today? The regret had been a fine needle, well placed. Right up until Ji-ho bought LOEN and became the capital shark holding the peninsula’s music jugular — at which point the regret simply evaporated. Some fish are not kept in anyone’s pond.

“Talk like that makes us strangers.” His gaze was level and easy now. “Every man has his own road. What you’ve built, you built yourself. If anything — I should be glad I let you go.”

Ji-ho blinked, genuinely caught.

“If you had stayed at SM, tied down by our rules, perhaps there would be no Chairman Song today — no one reckless enough to attempt miracles and actually land them.” The tone carried release, and under the release, real admiration. “That SM could be of use to you in the early days — call it SM’s honor.”

Something moved in Ji-ho’s chest. This was why the man had co-authored an entire cultural wave: the scale of him. He’d walked in braced for old grievance, and found none. He had over-planned, as usual.

They talked a while longer — the old practice rooms, the state of the industry, the acquisition everyone was still dissecting, SM’s current slate — and the air between them warmed by degrees. But he hadn’t come to reminisce, and eventually his expression gathered itself and he stepped onto the real ground.

“Seonsaeng-nim. Today, besides paying my respects — there’s a matter I need your help with.”

Lee Soo-man’s eyebrow rose. So the visit had cargo after all. Young men never could hold out long.

“Oh? The Chairman of LOEN still has problems that require ME?”

“You’re teasing me, seonsaeng-nim.” The flattery went on first, expertly fitted. “In this country, for star-making — for the depth and caliber of a trainee bench — if SM calls itself second, no one alive dares claim first. The training system you built with your own hands has fed this company talent for decades. There isn’t a person in the industry who doesn’t study it.”

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Of everything in his life, the trainee system was the achievement Lee Soo-man wore closest to the skin — decades of watching raw, blinking teenagers walk in and idols walk out under the lights. Watching the younger man’s respect for THAT, specifically, was… agreeable.

“Since acquiring LOEN, I intend to expand into full management — a girl group, launching next year. But LOEN is an infant in that business. No elite trainee bench. So I’ve come to ask my teacher: would you part with a few trainees, to give my new group its spine?”

“And beyond that — I’d like SM’s help building LOEN a complete trainee system.”

There it was. Lee Soo-man smiled inwardly — all that varnish had to be waterproofing SOMETHING. Still: trainee transfers between agencies were routine commerce, and half the industry’s training programs were photocopies of SM’s anyway. The system wasn’t the secret. The teacher was. So instead of refusing, he leaned in, interested despite himself.

“Oh? And which of our trainees has Chairman Song’s eye fallen on? Let’s hear it.”

No circling. Ji-ho drew a folded slip from his inside pocket and passed it across.

“I won’t hide the ball, seonsaeng-nim. The five I want are: Bae Joo-hyun. Kang Seul-gi. Son Seung-wan. Park Soo-young. Kim Ye-rim.”

The smile on Lee Soo-man’s face stopped where it stood.

His eyes moved down the five names and his face lowered itself degree by degree, fingers tightening on the paper, a coal of real anger lighting behind his eyes. The AUDACITY of this boy. Wendy and Yeri — talented, yes, but not the crown jewels; those could be let go with a wave. But Irene and Seulgi were the TOP of the current trainee class: Joo-hyun the born centerpiece, face and bearing both; Seul-gi — voice recovering, yes, but a complete performer and the obvious main dancer — both long since marked for priority development. And Joy: secretly cultivated for YEARS, skilled, likable, already penciled into the core of the next girl group. THE next girl group — the one that would hold SM’s flag when Girls’ Generation’s members turned toward solo work. These three WERE the plan. And this boy wanted to buy the plan out of the drawer.

The urge to slam the desk and end the meeting rose fast — and stopped against arithmetic. LOEN was the largest music platform in the country. SM’s whole business was idols; idols lived and died on chart performance; one does not casually make an enemy of the chart. And the boy had spent the entire morning bowing and saying SEONSAENG-NIM, giving him face by the armful. Blowing up now would make the elder look small.

He breathed in slowly, pressed the anger flat, and refused with a black face and a cold voice.

“Chairman Song. Impossible. These five are priority development targets. SM will not sell them to LOEN. Choose others.”

“As for the trainee system — THAT we can discuss. Send your people to observe and learn. You spent months here yourself; building LOEN’s version will not be difficult.”

Ji-ho showed no flicker of alarm. Instead, with perfect calm, he took a USB drive from his briefcase, set it gently on the desk, and slid it across.

“Seonsaeng-nim — don’t refuse just yet. I know exactly what those five are worth to SM, and I don’t intend for you to part with them for nothing. On that drive is a demo — the title track I’ve written for f(x)’s new album. It’s called ‘First Wisdom Tooth.’ Listen first.”

A new Ji-ho composition. Lee Soo-man’s eyes settled on the little drive, and the anger receded before a rising professional hunger. The boy’s musical gift was a matter of public record — the flashes of it visible even back in the SM months, then the debut album that could fund a retirement on publishing alone, then the American charts. A song he was willing to spend as a NEGOTIATING CHIP would not be a small song.

He plugged it in and pressed play.

The intro alone lifted his eyebrows. A strange, delicious rhythmic dislocation — guitar line, snare, and vocal refusing to land on the same downbeat, pulling against each other in a tug-of-war groove that hooked in under the ribs and would not release. Verses running spare — a lean guitar figure over crisp marching snare, like a first crush’s heartbeat; the chorus detonating all at once, every instrument in, dense snare rolls under massed vocal lines, melody drilling straight through the skull on first contact. The bridge stripping back down to let the voices breathe, emotion falling and then climbing to a higher peak than before. And the palette! — that Middle-Eastern guitar color against classic military snare against modern electronic pop: forward-leaning, experimental, and still unmistakably a girl group’s song, sweetness intact, limits broken. The whole arrangement was welded to its concept — the unstable rhythm, the sudden aching accents, melody erupting where you least expected: a first love arriving like a wisdom tooth, unannounced, uncomfortable, unforgettable. Every bar in service of one metaphor.

The demo ended. The office stayed silent.

Lee Soo-man leaned back, eyes closed, the melody still circling. What remained in him was mostly awe. This song was CUT for f(x) — as if measured against their bodies. Han-pop, Western dance floors, and now this — experimental concept work — the boy drove every genre he touched to its ceiling. Heaven-sent. There was no other honest word.

Ji-ho read the older man’s face and knew the chip had landed. He didn’t push. Between clever men, silence negotiates better than pressure.

At length, Lee Soo-man opened his eyes. His gaze was complicated.

“Speak. What else are you offering?”

Ji-ho smiled, entirely at ease.

“Seonsaeng-nim. I know the new girl group launches next year — because Girls’ Generation is seven years in, the members will turn toward individual work, and SM needs continuity to hold the market. That’s the logic.”

A slight nod. The plan was unannounced but hardly a secret at this altitude.

“But run that plan, and f(x) gets sacrificed.”

Regret colored the words, and it wasn’t performed.

“You know better than I do what that team is. A main vocal like Luna. Victoria — a genuine main dancer who is also a visual. The multinational construction. Amber’s androgyny, Krystal’s coolness, Sulli’s sweet-sharp edge — together it makes that rare thing: a girl group that doesn’t perform for the male gaze at all. There’s nothing else like them on the market.”

“Even with Girls’ Generation going individual, f(x) can carry the flag. And that’s before you count the TTS unit.”

Lee Soo-man said nothing — because it was true. f(x) was the most distinctive team SM had ever fielded, strong and liked, and perpetually eclipsed: first by Girls’ Generation’s sun, then by the company’s own tilted resource decisions. Launch a new group, and f(x)’s rations would thin further until the market quietly forgot them.

And Ji-ho, who had watched the original film of this decade, knew what the older man couldn’t: how the collapse had actually gone — a member expelled from Girls’ Generation and her sister’s career buried in the fallout; Sulli meeting exactly the wrong man and spinning away from the group and the world. But this time the film was being reshot. Sulli had HIM — her acting career armored, her orbit stable. Jessica had never met the man from the original timeline and, if he had any say, never would; her sister’s face and popularity would keep SM invested. The dominoes had been unbolted from each other. Which meant the new girl group was, for SM, both necessary and — quietly — not.

“Seonsaeng-nim,” he went on, “Sulli is my little sister. ‘First Wisdom Tooth’ exists because SHE came and begged me for an f(x) song. I dote on her — film roles, songs, whatever she needs, I’ll provide. f(x)’s caliber, plus my songs, plus real support from SM — that team will write a second golden age.”

The words pressed directly on the older man’s oldest bruise: he had ALWAYS believed f(x) was uncut jade, lacking only the one dazzling work to break them loose. And here was the work, sitting on his desk in a plastic drive.

Before he could answer, Ji-ho laid out more.

“Beyond that. The two songs I wrote that charted in America — from now on, for Taeyeon’s and Tiffany’s solo projects, I’ll personally produce.”

“And EXO. Their comeback is imminent — the title track ‘Growl’ is my composition, as you know. There’s another song of mine SM has wanted for a while. Close this deal, and EXO gets it as the follow-up single.”

“Film and television: LOEN is building a production division. Deep cooperation is on the table — SM artists crossing into acting, OST placements — LOEN can tilt resources toward SM. Your screen pipeline has always been the thin spot. Together, that changes.”

A beat. Then, sincerely:

“Seonsaeng-nim — no one can eat the whole table alone. Everyone eating together is what lasts. This deal is good for LOEN. It’s BETTER for SM.”

And then the turn — the temperature dropping several degrees as the bottom line came out of its sheath.

“Of course — if my teacher prefers not to cooperate, that’s also fine. The five of them have already told me they’ll come to LOEN. I can simply pay their penalty fees and meet SM in court. Even with delays, a year at most — and I launch my group next year regardless.”

“Only then, LOEN and SM are finished with each other. Openly.”

His eyes fixed on Lee Soo-man’s, and the words came one at a time.

“You know your position with CJ is already strained. Cross LOEN too, and both of the peninsula’s major music platforms stand against SM — platforms that carry ninety percent of the market between them. Tell me, seonsaeng-nim: after that, what happens to SM’s chart results?”

“And Taeyeon — she’s my girlfriend. If LOEN and SM go to war and I take her with me when I leave… do you believe SM keeps her?”

“To say nothing of Girls’ Generation entire. If I opened my checkbook and made offers no sane person refuses — I could pry loose one or two at least. Because SM cannot put Taeyeon and Tiffany on the American charts. I can. SM cannot hand them the Western market. I already have.”

Every sentence was a blade set precisely against an artery — and privately he knew the threat was real: Taeyeon, Jessica, Tiffany, he could take all three if it came to that. But it would shatter Girls’ Generation, and Taeyeon would be torn apart choosing between company, team, and him. He had no intention of walking that road. The knife existed to never be used.

Lee Soo-man’s face went fully dark, fury boiling up with nowhere to go. The words YOU DARE — GET OUT — SM AND LOEN ARE ENEMIES FROM THIS DAY sat loaded behind his teeth. And could not be fired. Because the arithmetic was merciless: streaming meant two doors, Melon and Genie — LOEN and CJ. Melon WAS the chart standard; its award show sat at the industry’s center; CJ’s Mnet ran the biggest ceremony in Asia and its distribution arm had been circling SM’s record business for years — SM had co-founded a rival distributor and once boycotted that ceremony outright over it. Offend both platforms and you didn’t lose leverage; you lost the OXYGEN. LOEN was asking for trainees. CJ wanted the company’s spine. And what counter-threat did SM even hold? Pull its catalog off Melon? LOEN loses one supplier; SM loses half its digital revenue. The boy owned sixty percent of his company and answered to no board. Lee Soo-man was HIS company’s largest shareholder — at well under half — with losses to explain to everyone else and a certain CEO watching his flank with undisguised patience.

And against all that anger stood the offers, and the offers were maddeningly good. The screen-resources gap plugged. Sulli’s film had already shown what the boy’s backing could do for an SM face. His production credit on Taeyeon’s and Tiffany’s solos — two Americans-raised vocalists, aimed by the one producer who had actually cracked that market — if it worked twice more, SM wouldn’t just enter the West, it would BILL in dollars. And the alternative: lose the five trainees anyway, in court, publicly, and gain a platform-owning enemy for the decade.

He leaned back, arms crossed, face thunderous, and the room’s temperature fell to freezing while he sat with it. Ji-ho waited, calm as furniture. He knew what was happening on the other side of the desk, and knew equally that pressure now would only cost him. In the end, before capital and interest, pride always yielded. It was the one law of this industry that never took a day off.

At last, Lee Soo-man exhaled, long and slow, and looked at him with an expression layered beyond reading.

“You’ve grown ruthless, boy.”

“Seonsaeng-nim, I was cornered into it.” The voice gentled again, instantly the student’s. “I want to build one good girl group — and I want SM and LOEN to both win. That’s all this is.”

A silence. Fingers drumming the desk, each tap a decision being weighed. Then the head came up, and the eyes were sharp and settled.

“Fine. I agree. Irene and the other four — SM will transfer them to LOEN. But I have conditions.”

“Name them, seonsaeng-nim. If it’s within my power, it’s done.”

“The first three, no problem. On the screen resources — I’ll look after SM’s artists, but within limits, and I choose the people.”

Lee Soo-man frowned slightly — and then let it go. Strictly reckoned, the boy had already given more than face required; with THIS package he could quiet every shareholder in the building. EXO and Girls’ Generation were the aces; Taeyeon among the most valuable of all; and above everything, one fact stood like a wall: SM had no capital for a war with LOEN.

“Acceptable.”

“Then, seonsaeng-nim—” the smile opened, warm as the greeting had been, “—to a happy partnership.”

“A happy partnership.” The older man’s mouth curled, wry and half-defeated. “And boy — don’t make me regret today.”

“You have my word. I won’t disappoint you.”

He meant it, more or less. He had never wanted open war with SM anyway — dragging Taeyeon and Tiffany bodily into LOEN would have meant daily armed conflict with a certain Ji-eun, and no acquisition was worth that particular battlefield. As for the four conditions: they cost him almost nothing. The boy group was getting a song he’d always intended to hand over eventually, and it would pay him in both royalties and reputation. Writing his own women’s solo titles was not a concession; it was the plan. And the acting clause — Sulli, Yoon-a, Soo-young, and a certain younger Jung sister were ALL SM’s actresses, technically speaking. Ahem. As to who was taking advantage of whom in that arrangement — and whether the lambs sent into the wolf’s den would come back entirely as they left — well. He’d signed nothing about THAT.
