Chapter Seventy-One

I Want to Go Solo

The other trainees drifted in around him, questions tumbling over each other.

“Ji-ho — what did you WRITE this time? Can we hear the full version?”

“Another one after ‘A Boy’?? DAEBAK!!”

“When you debut — write songs for us too, okay?!”

The instructor clapped for order — though even her eyes carried open wonder now.

“All right, back to training. Ji-ho — focus on the practice in front of you. It matters too.” A beat. “…But well done.”

Training resumed. The air, however, never went back. Every few steps, someone’s gaze slid sideways toward him; every rest break, the whispers in the corners orbited his name and the scene the two producers had just performed at the door.

The news grew wings.

“Did you hear? The new Korean-American trainee, Ji-ho — he wrote ANOTHER incredible song, and Producer Choi from Team 1 and Producer Yoo from Team 3 both went to grab it IN PERSON!”

“And he turned them BOTH down flat!”

“A true songwriting prodigy… first place at his very first monthly. Insane.”

“The face, the skills, AND he writes? How is anyone supposed to compete?”

“Is he secretly the company’s hidden ace project?”

By evening, changing in the locker room, he could hear passersby outside — “look, that’s the prodigy who refused the big producers” — and the walk to the cafeteria came with an honor guard of stares and pointed fingers.

Wendy, walking with a knot of trainee friends, spotted the tall straight back from a distance and couldn’t help herself.

“See? I TOLD you he was special.”

“He’s genuinely famous now. ‘Prodigy writer Ji-ho’… bet it reaches the top floors within days.”

The man himself ate his dinner in perfect, unbothered calm — mind elsewhere entirely: on the melody of “Some,” and on exactly how a freshly minted “prodigy” reputation could be spent in a conversation with Lee Soo-man.

✦   ✦   ✦

The “prodigy writer” ripple spread wider and faster than even he projected. He never got the chance to request the meeting — a few afternoons later, the meeting requested him.

The staffer’s tone was deferential; the message just seven words: a producer would like to see you. Third floor, new building. But when he pushed open the conference-room door and saw the lean figure standing with its back to him, gazing out at the Han River — he already knew.

The figure turned. Behind the signature glasses, the gaze was calm and surgical. Founder. Chief producer. The man the industry called the godfather of K-pop: Lee Soo-man.

“Ji-ho. Sit.”

The voice was quiet — almost kindly. He indicated the chair opposite and took the head seat himself. On the table: one iPad, one glass of water. Nothing else.

Ji-ho bowed properly and sat — spine straight, neither servile nor stiff. He had guessed the agenda, and he had rehearsed for it. Extensively.

“Lately, the company’s producers keep repeating one name — and one demo called ‘Some.’” No preamble; fingers moved across the iPad. “Ji-ho. ‘Sol’ — Latin and Spanish for the sun.” A glance up. “It suits you.”

“Thank you.”

“This ‘Some’ — lyrics, melody, arrangement. All yours, independently?”

“Yes, Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim.”

“An excellent piece of work.”

Four words — but from this man, they weighed kilograms. The eyes behind the glasses studied Ji-ho the way one studies raw jade that has just cracked open to show unexpected color.

“The grasp of the commercial pulse. The fineness of the emotional weave. It is not what a rookie trainee writes. That Choi Sung-woo and Yoo Ji-hoon both came for it surprises me not at all.” A pause, tone level as ever. “I hear you refused them both.”

“Yes. I’m grateful for their regard — but I have no plans to license that song. For now.”

“Oh?”

The founder leaned forward slightly, fingers lacing on the tabletop — the posture of a man preparing to genuinely listen.

“Then tell me why. You understand, I assume, what a place on BoA’s album — or in a flagship collaboration — means for a newcomer.”

The room was very quiet; the soundproofed glass strained the city down to nothing but the whisper of climate control.

Ji-ho drew one slow breath. Here. Now. The real negotiation starts here. Before this particular pair of eyes, every species of pretense was worthless.

“Seonsaeng-nim.” He lifted his gaze and held the older man’s directly. “I don’t want to hand that song over as its composer.”

The eyes behind the lenses flickered — a millimeter — and made no interruption.

“I want to sing it myself. I want to debut — as a solo artist.”

The air seemed to set for an instant, like cooling wax. Lee Soo-man’s face showed nothing overt, but his laced fingers tapped, once, almost imperceptibly, against the back of his hand. The silence stretched — and the scrutiny in his gaze thickened.

“Solo…” He repeated the word slowly, as if weighing it on a jeweler’s scale. “Do you understand what you’re saying, Ji-ho-ssi? EXO debuted weeks ago. The company’s boy-group planning for the next several years is committed there. And a trainee of not yet three months — a foreigner — sits across from me, from SM, and proposes a solo debut?”

The tone never rose. The weight landed anyway — each obstacle laid out with clinical precision: the corporate roadmap. The three-month résumé. And the keystone of the wall: the passport.

“I know how it sounds. Sudden. Possibly arrogant.” No retreat in the voice. “But I believe my situation is a special case.”

“Then make the case.”

Lee Soo-man settled back, unreadable.

“First — time. EXO took years to build; the next boy group won’t launch for three or four more. I can’t wait that long. And the company…” a careful beat, “…may not wish to wait either.”

No reaction. Neither yes nor no.

“Second — my value. ‘A Boy’ and ‘Some’ establish the writing. And I believe they’re only the opening pages. A singer-songwriter who produces at that level, consistently, builds a company’s musical brand in a way even a top boy group can’t — and can’t be replaced the way a group member can.”

“Third—” he paused, and set the key chip on the table with perfect calm, “—my cost, and my risk. I’m an A-rank trainee of three months. My contract penalty amounts to a few months’ training fees. For any company hungry for original music, that figure isn’t an obstacle — it’s a rounding error. I could pay it myself, walk out with these two songs, and find a company that WILL let me go solo.”

The eyes behind the glasses went sharp as drawn steel.
