Chapter Two Hundred Seventy-Six

The Girl Who Kept the Messages

Sunday · 10:42 Seoul · Sinchon · a walk-up built the year of the IMF bailout two men, one envelope, and a door that is not going to open quickly

Is this the place?

Sinchon was the sort of neighbourhood that rents itself to the young and then watches them leave. Four flights, no lift, a corridor that smelled of someone else’s garlic, and a door with a brass plate reading 1101 in plastic numbers that had begun to curl at one corner.

Ryu Dong-hwan pressed the bell and stepped back half a pace, hands folded, face arranged into something he clearly thought was gentle and was, in fact, the expression he used on studio executives. Beside him, Lee Hyun-woo checked his cuff buttons the way other men check a watch.

Brrring— brrring—

“Who is it?”

“Annyeong haseyo. It’s Ryu, from the telephone.”

A silence with movement in it: chairs, a bolt, the chain taking weight. The door opened a hand’s width and a woman in her fifties looked out through the gap with the specific wariness of somebody who has been sorry she opened a door before.

“Please come in.”

The flat held its breath the way rooms do when nobody has opened a window in days. Curtains drawn against a bright morning. A stack of delivery containers on the low table, lids not quite closed. The television on, muted, a variety show laughing at itself in colour with no sound.

In the far corner of the sofa, a young woman sat with her knees drawn up under her, wearing a man’s cardigan that swallowed her. Hair clipped back any which way. She stared at the screen with an attention so complete it was obvious nothing on it reached her.

“Ah-reum-ah,” the woman said quietly. “We have guests.”

The girl turned her head slowly, looked at the two of them for a moment, and gave one small nod. A greeting delivered the way a person returns a form.

Fourteen months out of a life that had been eleven hours a day in a practice room since she was ten, and you could see the arithmetic in her face: colour gone, the skin under both eyes bruised with it, cheekbones where cheekbones have no business being. She had been pretty in the way the internet argues about. Now she was only thin.

They sat where they were asked, on the opposite sofa, close enough to talk and far enough not to crowd her. Dong-hwan put the envelope on the low table, next to the plastic containers, where it looked obscene.

“Lee Ah-reum-ssi.” He kept his voice low, unhurried, the register he saved for people who have already been shouted at by better men than him. “On the telephone we told you part of it. The rest we should say in the room. The matter of the bullying — the company’s version, the public’s version. We would like to hear yours.”

The word bullying went through her like a hand through water. Her shoulders came up. Both hands found the hem of the cardigan and worked it into a rope between her fingers.

She said nothing.

Her mother sat down beside her and put a hand over the rope of knit. “She isn’t well. The doctor at the hospital in Sinchon says she should be admitted — she has said it three times now. But admission costs, and we…”

“That isn’t a thing you need to solve.” Dong-hwan slid the envelope the length of his arm, so that it rested within her reach rather than in front of him. “It’s a small arrangement from us.”

The woman looked at the envelope as if it might bite. Her eyes went red at the rims and her mouth worked.

“Now, really, we couldn’t—”

“Ajumma.” Hyun-woo’s tone had a hinge in it, a place where the sentence could turn either way, and he let it stay there. “The Hoejang-nim was direct about this. He said: in the affair of the bullying, Ah-reum-ssi is the injured party. That no person should have been left to hold what happened to her. And that if we are able to carry part of it, then carrying it isn’t generosity. It’s the part that was always ours to do.”

Lee Ah-reum lifted her head so fast the clip gave and half her hair came down.

“The injured party.” Her voice had been used up on something, lately; there was no room in it. “You believe me?”

“We do.” Dong-hwan met her straight on, and did not add anything to it, because a lie of that size needs company and the truth does not. “It’s why we’re standing in your corridor on a Sunday.”

What came after was not a sob. It was the sound of a person discovering that a thing she had been holding up had been let go of from the other end. She covered her face with both hands and her shoulders shook hard enough to make the sofa creak, and the noise she made — small, embarrassed, apologising for itself — was the worst part of the morning, because that was what fourteen months of being a girl who had to be quiet had done to her crying.

Her mother gathered her in and cried with her.

Neither man moved. The muted television gesticulated. Hyun-woo looked at the window and gave the room as much time as the room wanted, and when Dong-hwan started to shift, a finger touched his wrist to stop him, and after that they sat still until the shaking thinned.

It was a long while. Ah-reum pressed her sleeve across her eyes, breathed in through her nose twice the way the hospital had taught her, and spoke without looking at either of them.

“What is it you want to know?”

“About Hwa-young-ssi. And her sister.” From inside his jacket Dong-hwan took a recorder no bigger than a pen and set it on the table between them, not on her side. “If you’re willing, we’d like to hear what happened at the time. And if the messages are still—”

“The messages.” Something moved across her face: fear, and under the fear an exhaustion so old it had become furniture. “I kept them.”

She got up, stood a moment holding the back of the sofa, and went into the bedroom. She came back with a phone two generations old, the corner of the case cracked and repaired with a square of tape.

Two swipes, and she held it out screen-first.

Dong-hwan read them once. Then he read them again, which is what a man does when he is trying to be certain he is not about to exaggerate to a colleague.

“Thirteen messages,” Ah-reum said. “That day. There were more after, in August, but I stopped opening them.”

“You were nineteen.”

“I was nineteen.” She said it the way you’d say a number someone had read out at you. “I’d never had anything like that happen, and I didn’t know yet what you do. I went to the representative crying. I thought — a company protects the person it put on the schedule. That’s the arrangement.”

“And the arrangement?”

A smile arrived and did not stay for the photograph. “He told me to shut my mouth. He said a scandal like that is worse for the company than for me, and if I want to keep working, I do what I’m told. Then he told me if any of it leaves this room, there will be nothing for me in this industry. No schedules. No station appearances. No musical. Nothing.” She looked at her own hands, finishing it neatly, the way an accountant finishes a page. “He kept his word. Nobody has ever called me for a shoot since. I was the newest member for eleven days.”

INDUSTRY NOTE · WHAT A COMPANY ACTUALLY THREATENS

Korean audiences hear maejang — literally burial — as a soft word for a hard thing. It is not a suspension, a fine, or a warning. It is the withdrawal of every door: the broadcaster’s schedule that “filled up,” the musical whose casting closed that week, the agency whose contract quietly runs out with no renewal conversation attached. Nothing is ever said, because nothing needs to be. A trainee who has spent seven years learning to read a room can feel it arrive, and knows better than to name it in writing. Which is why the most reliable evidence in these cases is never a document from the company. It is the thing somebody kept on their own phone, at their own risk, for years.

The recorder had been running for four minutes and nobody spoke for three of them.

“So you carried it,” Hyun-woo said. “Alone. Through everything the public said about the group.”

“What was the alternative? Speak, and who believes the girl nobody chose for a single schedule?” She laughed once, without amusement, a sound with the safety taken off. “When it all came out, everyone hated us. Not the two sisters — us. They hated T-ara. They hated Jiyeon and Eunjung and Hyomin unni and So-yeon unni and Boram unni and Qri unni, and they hated me most, because I was the one who came in late and took the place. Do you know what it is, to be hated by forty million people and to have your own company tell you that being hated is the part you caused?”

“I know what it is,” Dong-hwan said, “to have a man with more money than judgement decide your silence is cheaper than his problem. But that isn’t what I came to ask about—”

“I was afraid she would come.”

The sentence stopped him.

“Hyo-young-ssi,” Ah-reum said. “I believed she would come to the practice room and hit me, and that people would stand behind her, because she was the unni who had been there longer and because the man in the office on the ninth floor would already have decided I was the one at fault. So I stopped going to the practice room. And then the sleeping—” She drew a breath along her ribs and stopped, and the room waited, and when she started again her voice had steadied out of pure stubbornness. “It isn’t an excuse. I know it isn’t. There are people who get worse things sent to them and stand up.”

“There are people,” Hyun-woo said, “who get worse things sent to them and get no one at all to send them to. You had a phone and you kept it. That is not a person who failed. That’s a person who did the only thing nobody told her she couldn’t.”

Her mother said, in a voice made of very little, “She hasn’t been outside this flat since Tuesday.”

Which was when Dong-hwan saw what he had come for: not the messages. The messages, the investigator had already bought. He had come for this — for the sound of a girl explaining her own courage to strangers as though it were a confession.

She nodded. Once, slowly, the way you sign something. Her mother pressed both hands over her mouth and turned away toward the window and the curtains stayed where they were.

“She’s frightened to answer the door,” the mother said, with the flat clarity of somebody who has memorised a leaflet. “She’s frightened of the sound of the group chat on somebody else’s phone. He says there’s a name for it and that it came from what happened, and that if she keeps living alone in the dark with it, the name won’t matter because she’ll stop getting out of bed at all. “We’ll set it up,” Dong-hwan said. “The envelope covers treatment, and the rest of it is arranged. Ah-reum-ssi.” He stood, and she had to look up at him, and he stayed low enough that she didn’t. “Nothing else is yours to carry for the moment. Get well. When this comes out, you’ll be standing in the sun with your own name in front of you, and not apologising for the weather.”

She sat looking at him with tears going down without any sound at all, which was somehow the least comfortable thing in the room.
