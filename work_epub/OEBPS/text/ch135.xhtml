Chapter One Hundred Thirty-Five

As It Flows

“As It Flows” had exactly one right lead, and he’d known it from the first bar: Irene.

Performance

THE TREATMENT · a trainee’s story, told straight.

SCENE ONE — PRACTICE ROOM. An empty mirror wall; a girl drilling the same eight-count alone until her training clothes soak through. Close on the bitten lip; the eyes that refuse. He enters, sets down two bottles of water, and falls in behind her without a word. Their eyes meet occasionally in the mirror. She always looks away first.

SCENE TWO — EVALUATION DAY. Over-trained and under-slept, she stumbles the assessment; the instructor’s critique is a blade. Head down, fingers twisting her hem. Later he finds her crying in the stairwell, hands her a tissue, says nothing, and sits down beside her. Beyond the window: a sky the color of wet cement.

SCENE THREE — STARLIGHT. Deep night; two people left in the building. She lands the impossible passage clean at last — and he’s the first to applaud, admiration completely uncovered. Even the instructor cracks: “Joo-hyun — good. And you’ve improved this month too.” They leave at dawn-adjacent hours, and on the empty street she looks up and finds a handful of surviving stars.

SCENE FOUR — LIGHT. The finale under simulated stage lights, two figures side by side — intercut with the mirrors, the sweat, the night streets, the sky slowly brightening — every grey frame pouring into one bright one. Because everything passes. Above the grey sky there is always a brighter light waiting; behind the clouds, the brilliant days.

— and if the song loosened one turn of the wire she kept wound around herself, it would have done its real work.

The shoot wrapped as the sunset burned down to deep blue. Crew packed; Joo-hyun kneaded an aching shoulder on her way to the green room — and found him leaning by the door, waiting.

“Good work today.” A warm honey-citron tea, handed over. “I’ll drive you back. It’s on my way.”

The heater chased the early-autumn chill out of the car. She sank into the seat, watching the city lights pour past, still half-inside the song’s feeling — and registered, distantly, that the route had gone unfamiliar. Fatigue filed the observation without acting on it.

The car climbed a winding road and stopped at a quiet terrace near the octagonal pavilion overlook. Wind-sound instead of traffic. Below, Seoul’s lights ran to the horizon like a spilled river of stars; N Seoul Tower stood off in the dark.

“Why here?” She turned, puzzled.

He didn’t answer. He lowered the window, and the night air came in carrying pine and far-off mountains. Then he held out one earbud.

The intro of “As It Flows” started against her ear.

“Look.” He pointed at the burning city below. “Inside those lights, how many people are struggling exactly like us, holding on exactly like us? And from this far away — they’re just quiet, beautiful points of light.”

She followed his hand — and felt the day’s whole crushing inventory of anxiety shrink, at this height and this distance, into something that could be carried.

The song rose toward its chorus, and his voice came in low beside it.

“Joo-hyun. You’ve pushed hard enough. Seung-wan told me — practicing till one in the morning, every night.”

Her hands closed. A month of five-hour nights. Concealer losing its war with the shadows under her eyes.

“But I can’t stop,” she said, very quietly — a voice convincing itself. “I keep seeing my own reflection in you.”

“Except you’ve already debuted. And you’ve already WON.”

“I still can’t see where my road even IS.”

“Which is exactly why you have to be kinder to yourself.” He turned, and made her hold his eyes. “You don’t need to prove anything by burning yourself for fuel.”

“You’re beautiful — that alone is already enough. It doesn’t need your health as a down payment.”

“And you REALLY don’t need to measure against me.”

— because, he did not say aloud, I play this game with cheats enabled.

The mountain wind lifted the loose hair at her forehead. And she noticed — late, and then completely — that for the first time since they’d met, he hadn’t called her nuna. Just her name.

Silence spread between them, with only the song still moving through it. When the last bright line about being shone upon faded out, she pulled the earbud free — and asked the question that had been circling since the first camera rolled that morning.

“This song — did you write it for me?”

She regretted it before the echo died. Whatever the answer was, nothing between them would fit back in its old box.

“Never mind — you don’t have to answ—”

He didn’t let her finish.

He unclipped his seatbelt, leaned across the console, and took her face gently in one hand — slowly, slowly enough that she had every chance in the world to pull away.

She didn’t.

The kiss was light, and brief — a question mark more than a sentence — and he was already back, hand still warm at her cheek, before she’d caught up to it. Her face went furnace-hot; her heart hammered the walls.

“Still need the answer?” His voice had gone low and rough.

Her mouth opened. Nothing came out. Reason filed a motion: be angry, push him away. Her body declined to second it — she couldn’t even relocate her eyes.

“May I continue?”

He asked — and didn’t wait. The second kiss was nothing like the first: his arm around her shoulders, his hand at the back of her neck, unhurried and entirely certain. Her palms came up against his chest to push — and the push had no strength anywhere in it.

“Mm— mmh— let go—”

The protest drowned somewhere between them. And strangest of all: the part of her that should have resisted was the part waking UP. He smelled faintly of cedar and a thread of sandalwood, and it read to her nervous system as SAFE — and at some point the hands pushing at his chest had become hands holding fistfuls of his shirt. When she caught herself answering the kiss, shame and panic surged — and were flooded flat by the older, larger thing underneath: the liking she had been sitting on for the better part of a year.

His hand found the curve of her waist through the thin knit, and the warmth of it jolted her all the way sober. She shoved him back — for real this time.

“Mmh— Ji-ho!” Gasping, scarlet to the ears. “I— I’m ANGRY!”

He didn’t retreat far. He looked at her wet eyes and burning face and asked, quietly:

“Angry at what, exactly?”

“At the kiss itself? At the person kissing you? Or—” a pause, precise as a scalpel, “—at the fear that this could touch your training. Your debut.”

Every question went in like a needle finding the exact nerve. Her mouth opened; not one of the three had an answer she could say out loud. Yes — she was afraid for the debut. And also: if ANY other person had tried this, they’d never have gotten within arm’s reach.

He read the hesitation for what it was.

Ji-yeon needed patience; IU needed chess. Irene needed someone to simply take the wheel she was white-knuckling and pry her fingers off it, one by one.

“Nuna,” he said — the honorific restored, the distance not — leaning slowly back in. “You and me — like the song. We let it flow. Alright?”

And he kissed her again.

Her eyes flew wide. In WHAT sense is this letting things flow naturally?! — but the thought frayed as his warmth folded around her, as his hand cradled the back of her head, as she registered the faint mint-candy trace on his breath; and the last procedural objections blurred, and her eyes closed, and her arms went — slowly, entirely voluntarily — around his shoulders.

The kiss was long, and gentle, and said something, and promised something. Below them the city burned on, oblivious. On the quiet height, in the dark, something changed owners without a word.

A long time later he drew back, forehead resting against hers, both of them short of air.

“You— I—” She found her voice and discovered it had no sentences in it.

“Don’t be scared. Trust me.” He smiled, and with one thumb, carefully, tidied the lip color the evening had disarranged. “Nothing changes. Except one thing.”

“…Except?”

“Except now I know. You like me too.”

She drew breath to argue — and saw, reflected small and bright in his eyes, exactly one person — and gave up the pretense entirely, and hid her burning face against his shoulder.

The mountain wind moved through, carrying the first cold of autumn and the far-down murmur of the city. The song had ended long ago. Two heartbeats, unhurried, were finding the same tempo — and below them, Seoul’s lights went on flowing, a river of stars that did not burn out.
