Chapter Sixty-Nine

Good Songs Are Fought Over

The Next Day SM Entertainment — Production Center where songs are hunted

“Sung-woo hyung — get over here. Listen to this demo.”

Choi Sung-woo crossed the room and picked up the headphones. He was one of SM’s working producers — his team handled the crown jewels: Kangta, BoA, Girls’ Generation — production, management, and career strategy for the old guard and the reigning girl-group royalty alike.

He listened all the way through, then pulled the headphones off with open astonishment.

“In-jun-ah. The female vocal on this — that’s Taeyeon. Tell me I’m wrong.”

“You’re not wrong, hyung.” Park In-jun was practically vibrating. “It’s her.”

Sung-woo drummed the desk.

“And the song? Your read?”

“I’ve looped it five times already.” The younger producer launched in, fully armed: “Listen to the arrangement — it builds this catchy, circling harmonic loop out of simple materials, and the circling IS the point: the music itself keeps wavering back and forth, exactly like the lyric’s push-pull. Whoever arranged this is a killer, hyung. This song HITS.”

“It’s genuinely good.” Sung-woo nodded slowly. “Verse to hook, the emotion climbs on tiny instrumental adds and vocal pressure — no bombast — and those clean, soft electronic colors make the whole thing feel like two people mid-flirtation, mid-conversation. Run this with even modest promotion and it goes big.”

Nobody made it into SM’s production center without ears. Both men had reached the same verdict inside one listen: a rare, genuinely commercial gem.

“Hyung — BoA’s seventh album is still two tracks short, isn’t it? This belongs on it.”

Sung-woo stroked his chin. “Only One” was slated for ten tracks with eight locked; a male-female duet was unusual for the tracklist, but quality like this could move BoA herself. Decision forming, he stood and grabbed his phone.

“Who’s the writer?”

“Some rookie registered as ‘Sol.’ Only two songs uploaded ever.” A hesitant pause. “‘A Boy’… and this one, ‘Some.’”

Sung-woo froze mid-step.

“‘A Boy’? …It’s HIM?”

“Him who? Hyung, you KNOW this writer?”

Sung-woo clapped his junior on the shoulder with fatherly pity.

“In-jun-ah. Make more friends at this company. Listen to more gossip.”

And he was moving. In-jun blinked after him — ‘A Boy’? Is that song famous?? — then scrambled to follow.

“Hyung — where are you going?! Wait for me!”

“Practice rooms.”

And elsewhere in the building, other producers who had heard the same demo were converging on the same corridor — because at SM Entertainment, a good song wasn’t assigned. It was taken.

Field Notes

How songs get distributed at a giant agency — the real rules:

SM ran roughly five production teams, each attached to its own artists: Team 1 — Kangta, BoA, Girls’ Generation. Team 2 — SHINee, EXO. Team 3 — TVXQ, Super Junior. And so on. When the company library produced a gem, all five teams could audition the demo — and if two teams wanted it, the company’s rule gave it to the team with the more URGENT comeback, not the more famous artist. Take the song, and you had to USE it: try hoarding it for a future album and the company clawed it back the next day and handed it to whoever was next in the release queue.

Above the library scrum sat two aristocratic channels. Commissions — artist seeks writer, cash and connections required: the songs that built TWICE (“TT,” “Cheer Up,” “Likey”) were JYP commissions from Black Eyed Pilseung; a Girls’ Generation comeback helmed personally by Yoo Young-jin or ace writer KENZIE was the in-house equivalent. And designations — writer names the artist: solo-era Taeyeon received so many designated submissions that other teams only ever saw her leftovers.

No budget, no connections? Then you lived on library luck. Catch a good song — fly. Miss — flop; and a flopped artist soon lost even the right to browse. Their team’s income sank with them; layoffs followed. Which is why, the instant a certified gem hit the library, every A&R in the building locked onto the writer like a starving predator. “A Boy” was brilliant but branded — too personal, welded to its writer. “Some,” though? “Some,” ANYONE could sing.

The writer in question was, at that moment, in runway-walk class.

An idol who walked badly was a contradiction in terms, so a professional instructor corrected every habit step by step — how to move with presence, men sharp, women magnetic. Enrollment was small; no gender split today. With Ji-ho, Wendy, and Joy, the class totaled seven rookies.

Seung-wan strode down the line, face grave as a magistrate, one step per beat — chasing “hip sway” by manually swinging everything she owned, which produced instead a distinct vertical bobbing.

“Seung-wan-ah. Are you a DUCK? What is this waddling?”

Nobody laughed. Everyone here had BEEN her. Seung-wan herself, however, briefly died inside.

“…Sorry, seonsaengnim.”

“Hands on hips. The pelvis rotates NATURALLY with the stride — you don’t crank it. Back — STRAIGHT. Eyes in the mirror, gaze FIXED. No drifting.”

One full circuit later, forehead shining, she flopped down cross-legged next to Ji-ho while Soo-young took the floor.

“Who knew WALKING had this much theory…” A sigh from the soul. “I don’t want this class. Can’t I just walk the way that feels comfortable?”

“Month-end evaluation’s coming. Lose posture points and miss A-rank again — you’ll take walking very seriously very fast.”

“Ughhh. Fine.” The prospect visibly deflated her. “Tonight I’ll ask Joo-hyun eonni to coach me. Everyone says eonni’s deportment scores were the best.”

Irene? Walking coach? Into his mind came, unbidden, the image of her actual gait — that busy, side-to-side little roll, exactly like an extremely dignified corgi. Core strength ran a touch low, so the hips compensated, and the whole walk swung — but on a figure like hers the flaw had somehow converted itself into a signature, complete with a devoted fan contingent who followed her purely for the walk.

Learning hip sway from Joo-hyun nuna. Seung-wan-ah, credit where due — you’ve found yourself a true master of the discipline.
