Chapter Two Hundred Sixty-One

Reunion with T-ara

LOEN Entertainment · executive reception room CCM, cross-shareholding, and the price of buying tomorrow’s value with today’s distress

Ji-ho studied the woman across from him.

On quoted market values, Kyung-hoo’s proposal was not absurd. That did not make it attractive.

LOEN had Ji-ho, IU, Melon, and a Western-facing album close to production. Its direction was upward.

What did CCM bring in return?

T-ara at the lowest point of its public standing. Davichi, commercially reliable but never built for idol-scale expansion. Buildings, catalog rights, and contracts were valuable—but not enough to justify giving away fifteen percent of a company whose market value could change dramatically after one global release.

One business was sunrise.

The other was being sold because its owner no longer wanted to hold it through the night.

Ji-ho lifted his tea.

“Director Lee, I’m not interested in that exchange.”

Kyung-hoo’s brows rose slightly.

In the Chinese etiquette Ji-ho had studied, lifting tea could signal that a meeting was ending. Korean chaebol heirs understood dismissal without needing imported customs.

Her aunt had predicted this. CCM alone would never purchase fifteen percent of LOEN.

Kyung-hoo leaned back rather than stand.

“I genuinely believe in LOEN’s future. Name the structure you would accept.”

Persistent.

Ji-ho’s expression cooled.

“I have never considered selling LOEN shares. It was a pleasure meeting you, but I have work waiting.”

He began to rise.

“What if CJ equity is part of the exchange?”

Ji-ho stopped.

“Say that again.”

Kyung-hoo stood as well.

“I’m serious about a strategic partnership. Are you truly uninterested in LOEN and CJ owning part of each other?”

Of course he was interested.

CJ ENTERTAINMENT ECOSYSTEM · 2013 STRATEGIC VIEW

TelevisionMnet · tvN · O’live · OnStyle and associated cable-channel operations under the broader CJ E&M structure

MusicMnet distribution and programming · MAMA · artist and content channels

Deep cooperation with CJ could give Ji-ho access to film, television, variety, cable promotion, and cinema distribution at a scale LOEN could not build quickly.

From Kyung-hoo’s side, LOEN controlled Korea’s most important digital-music platform and a major record-distribution network. Ji-ho supplied creative momentum CJ could not manufacture through capital alone.

“You have authority to negotiate CJ equity?”

Kyung-hoo’s posture straightened.

“I do. I was raised to inherit responsibility for CJ’s entertainment business.”

Her aunt was more enthusiastic than she was, asking twice that week for updates—although Miky Lee’s questions had focused suspiciously often on Ji-ho himself rather than LOEN’s balance sheet.

Ji-ho returned to his seat.

“What kind of partnership?”

“Comprehensive cooperation and cross-shareholding.”

Kyung-hoo placed both hands over the file.

“You are already one of Korea’s most visible producers. Your first feature entered the all-time top five. I see no reason television should be beyond you.”

“LOEN brings Melon, record distribution, artists, and digital reach. CJ brings television channels, MAMA, film distribution, theaters, and Asian-market infrastructure.”

“Together, we would influence nearly every route from creation to audience.”

The proposal was genuinely powerful.

In the future Ji-ho remembered, CJ ENM and Kakao Entertainment would become two of the largest forces in Korean content. He was already accumulating a position connected to Kakao’s eventual corporate structure. A meaningful CJ stake would place him at the intersection of both ecosystems.

That position mattered enough to justify compromise.

DREAM LIFE · PASSIVE EFFECT

Business TailwindHigh-quality opportunities appear more often; professional goodwill compounds; avoidable resistance decreases.

The effect creates openings, not favorable terms. Negotiation remains the player’s responsibility.

“What numbers are you proposing?”

Kyung-hoo raised two fingers.

“CCM transferred to LOEN at an agreed package value of one hundred billion won.”

“In addition, one percent of CJ Entertainment in exchange for fifteen percent of LOEN.”

In the story’s financial snapshot, CJ Entertainment’s underlying value was estimated near four trillion won, though political pressure had pushed its listed-equity reference closer to three trillion. LOEN’s current market capitalization had climbed toward nine hundred thirty billion.

On paper, the proposal was not wildly imbalanced.

It was still not the deal Ji-ho wanted.

“You’re using an unwanted subsidiary to purchase part of LOEN,” Ji-ho said. “That calculation is loud enough to hear from the hallway.”

Kyung-hoo did not yield.

“CCM includes an office building, recording rooms, practice facilities, equipment, distribution relationships, and valuable contracts.”

“T-ara, Davichi, Hong Jin-young, Yangpa, Son Ho-jun, and Ha Seok-jin. Master rights, publishing interests where contracted, and domestic and overseas channels.”

“CJ wants to sell. That does not make the assets imaginary.”

“Then let LOEN buy CCM in cash.”

Ji-ho leaned back.

“For equity: nine percent of LOEN for three percent of CJ Entertainment.”

Kyung-hoo stared.

“Now you’re the one taking advantage.”

“CJ’s political discount may last years. My album is approaching release. One value is under pressure; the other may be about to re-rate.”

“That is not generosity, Chairman Song.”

“I didn’t label it generosity.”

Kyung-hoo’s mouth twitched. Her favorite artist, it appeared, negotiated without shame.

“The cross-shareholding question requires boards, lawyers, valuation opinions, and regulatory review. We won’t settle it today.”

“Agreed.”

Delay favored Ji-ho if the album lifted LOEN’s value. He had no reason to resist it.

Kyung-hoo closed the equity file.

“Then let’s separate CCM from the share exchange.”

“That is more productive.”

She stood.

“Come inspect the company with me.”

CCM.

Jiyeon’s face entered Ji-ho’s mind.

“Lead the way.”

✦ ✦ ✦

“Ride with me,” Kyung-hoo said. “I have questions.”

“I’ll take my car.”

“Then I’ll take yours.”

She entered before he could object.

Ji-ho stood beside the open door for half a second.

Kyung-hoo’s attention felt warmer than ordinary executive curiosity. That did not mean romance. She was also a fan, newly returned from years abroad, and apparently determined to compress several months of questions into one drive.

Even so, Ji-ho intended to maintain a careful boundary. A chaebol heir carried complications no idol scandal could match.

He entered the car and left a polite distance between them.

Kyung-hoo watched the city through the window.

“Artists have official fan communities, don’t they? Do you ever enter yours personally?”

“Occasionally. A team handles most moderation and announcements.”

“That explains why the account claiming to be ‘Ji-ho Himself’ never sounds consistent.”

Ji-ho turned.

Kyung-hoo smiled innocently.

“Nothing. Another question.”

She shifted toward a less formal tone.

“‘Chairman Song’ and ‘Director Lee’ feel exhausting. We get along. Shouldn’t we speak comfortably?”

Ji-ho moved one fraction farther toward the door.

“Use whatever form you prefer, Director Lee.”

“Then I’ll call you Ji-ho. I’m older, so you can call me nuna.”

“Director Lee is perfectly clear.”

She ignored that.

“Ji-ho, how is the album progressing?”

“Well.”

“Do you really dislike spicy food?”

“I eat it occasionally.”

“Your profiles say one eighty-two, but you look taller.”

“One eighty-five.”

Kyung-hoo immediately made a note on her phone.

“So the fan profiles are wrong.”

“Which profiles?”

“Unimportant.”

She paused, then asked far too casually:

“Do you have a girlfriend?”

“No comment.”

The car finally reached CCM. Ji-ho exited with visible relief.

“Director Lee?”

Kyung-hoo remained in her seat.

“Call me nuna.”

She stepped out before he answered.

Core Contents Media due diligence begins with people before paper

Kim Kwang-soo hurried forward wearing his most welcoming smile.

“Director Lee, what brings you here?”

Kyung-hoo’s expression became executive-neutral.

“Do I need to submit a report before visiting a CJ subsidiary?”

“Aigoo, of course not.”

His gaze shifted to Ji-ho.

“Chairman Song?”

Kim Kwang-soo offered his hand.

“Kim Kwang-soo, president of CCM. Welcome.”

“Song Ji-ho. Thank you for receiving us.”

Kyung-hoo glanced at Ji-ho.

“Ji-ho is my guest and wants to inspect the company. Please guide us.”

The timing tightened Kim Kwang-soo’s smile, but he bowed.

“It would be my honor. This way.”

They passed offices, recording rooms, equipment storage, and smaller rehearsal studios before stopping outside a practice room where two women were working through a vocal-and-movement rehearsal.

“Min-kyung. Hae-ri.”

The performers turned, straightened their clothing, and bowed.

“Annyeong haseyo, sajang-nim. Director Lee.”

Kim Kwang-soo made the introductions.

“Kang Min-kyung and Lee Hae-ri of Davichi. This is LOEN chairman Song Ji-ho.”

Both women bowed again.

“Annyeong haseyo, Hoejang-nim.”

“It’s a pleasure. Please continue.”

The group moved on. Once the door closed, Hae-ri released the breath she had been holding.

“That was terrifying.”

Min-kyung’s nerves converted quickly into excitement.

“Eonni, he’s even more handsome in person.”

“He really is.”

✦ ✦ ✦

At the larger practice room, Kim Kwang-soo stopped.

“T-ara uses this room most often.”

Inside, six women were already lined up.

“One, two, three. Annyeong haseyo. We are T-ara.”

Jiyeon stood near the center. Her eyes found Ji-ho immediately and struggled to leave him.

Kim Kwang-soo introduced the lineup.

“Qri, Boram, Soyeon, Eunjung, Hyomin, and Jiyeon.”

Ji-ho’s gaze moved across the members and paused for one discreet beat on the familiar face.

Jiyeon’s ears turned red.

“Annyeong haseyo. Song Ji-ho.”

The six bowed.

“Annyeong haseyo, Hoejang-nim.”

Kyung-hoo noticed the exchange and smiled to herself.

“President Kim, I would like to inspect the remaining area privately with Ji-ho. Please return to your work.”

Kim Kwang-soo hesitated and recovered.

“Of course. Director Lee, Chairman Song—please take your time.”

He withdrew with the staff, but not before giving T-ara a warning look.

The practice room became awkwardly quiet.

“Continue,” Kyung-hoo said. “I want Ji-ho to see the group’s current level.”

Eunjung organized the members. Music began: “Bo Peep Bo Peep.”

CCM Practice Room · T-ara · “Bo Peep Bo Peep” The opening bars hit. Six bodies moved — sharp, synchronized, the muscle memory of a thousand rehearsals wearing its smoothest groove. Their dancing was clean. T-ara’s foundational technique was, undeniably, good. Six sets of movements locked in unison; expression management dialed to professional. Each girl carried her own magnetism — Qri’s cool, Boram’s compact precision, So-yeon’s vocal-thread poise, Eun-jung’s stage authority, Hyo-min’s fluid line. And Ji-yeon — center — whose gaze kept drifting, every four counts, to the man at the console.

Kyung-hoo leaned closer to him.

“The maknae has watched you since you entered. Do you know each other?”

“Of course.”

“That answer concealed more than it revealed.”

Ji-ho kept his face neutral.

“Director Lee, weren’t we inspecting the company?”

“Call me nuna.”

He ignored her and studied the performance.

T-ara remained a strong group. Timing, expression, and individual color survived beneath the scandal. Each member held a recognizable identity rather than disappearing into one uniform concept.

Kyung-hoo’s tone became serious.

“You see the problem. The foundation is not weak. Management and public trust collapsed around them.”

“CCM’s largest asset is still T-ara. Davichi is dependable, but the ceiling is different. With the right strategy, T-ara can recover.”

“And you believe I should be the strategy.”

“I wanted you to see the people behind the balance sheet.”

Ji-ho considered the six women before him.

“Business is still business. If I buy, I price the company as it exists today—not as it might exist after I repair it.”

Kyung-hoo did not object.

“If they were still at their peak, would you have the chance to buy them?”

“No.”

“Then today’s distress is part of the opportunity.”

Ji-ho met her eyes.

“You truly intend to sell CCM to LOEN?”

“Yes.”

“Ninety-five billion won, subject to financial, legal, rights, and contract due diligence.”

Kyung-hoo’s gaze flickered.

“Accepted as the provisional cash price.”

Then mischief returned.

“But you have to call me nuna.”

Ji-ho did not miss the opening.

“Nuna, reduce it by another five billion.”

Kyung-hoo stared at him.

“That word is not worth five billion won.”

“Then it appears overpriced.”

“Ninety-five is the floor.”

“Stingy.”

“That is five billion won!”

Ji-ho relented with a laugh.

“Fine. I’ll appoint accountants and counsel for due diligence.”

“CJ has already completed an internal review. I’ll provide the data room. I’m not bringing you a poisoned company.”

He looked at her carefully.

“I’ll trust the data after my team verifies it.”

A clear voice interrupted them.

“Ji-ho.”

Both turned.

Jiyeon had hurried over and stopped two steps away. Exercise had warmed her face; fine strands of hair clung near her temples. Nervousness and happiness competed openly in her eyes.

Eunjung called from behind.

“Jiyeon-ah, what are you doing?”

Jiyeon heard only Ji-ho.

“Why are you here?”

She tried to sound composed and failed.

Ji-ho’s expression softened.

“Inspecting the company.”

He added:

“And checking on you.”

Jiyeon’s heart visibly forgot one beat.

She straightened as though reporting to a superior.

“I’m doing well. Practicing every day, eating properly, sleeping properly. I haven’t been lazy.”

Only after finishing did she realize how young the report sounded. Her face reddened further.

Ji-ho laughed.

“Good.”

Eunjung reached them and pulled Jiyeon back toward the formation.

Jiyeon looked over her shoulder twice.

Kyung-hoo watched with open amusement.

“She likes you.”

“That is not part of your due diligence.”

“I’m your nuna.”

Ji-ho made no response.

Before leaving, he looked back once. Jiyeon was still watching. She raised one hand immediately.

Late sunlight crossed the practice-room window and warmed her face.

He nodded and left.

Behind him, Hyomin seized Jiyeon’s hands.

“He looked at you. He smiled. What are you two?”

Jiyeon’s answer was almost too quiet to hear.

“I think… he’s my boyfriend.”

“You think?”

“He is.”

“Your expression changed when you looked at her,” she said.

Ji-ho watched the city outside.

“You observe too much.”

“I’m a woman. Some things are obvious.”

She smiled.

“Relax. I’m not interested in exposing your private life. And I’m glad there are people you care about.”

Ji-ho finally turned.

“Director Lee—”

“Nuna.”

He exhaled.

“Nuna. We met today.”

“I’ve followed your career much longer than one day.”

That was not the same thing, but arguing seemed unlikely to help.

Kyung-hoo shifted back to business.

“Consider the cross-shareholding structure seriously. My aunt and I both believe in LOEN—and in you.”

Ji-ho was quiet for a moment.

“The ninety-five-billion provisional price was genuine?”

“I keep my word.”

He nodded.

Outside, sunset turned Seoul warm orange.

Kyung-hoo looked at Ji-ho’s profile and thought that returning to Korea had already proven more interesting than every year she had spent planning it.
