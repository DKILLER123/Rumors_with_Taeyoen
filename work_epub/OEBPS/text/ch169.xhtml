Chapter One Hundred Sixty-Nine

Cameras Roll — A Cameo from Son Ye-jin

February → March 1, 2013 Seoul — pre-production, then the first set script breakdowns, an opening ceremony, and a legend drops by

Elsewhere that morning, Girls’ Generation had drawn a rare free half-day.

Sunny had slept badly — fate-stars and diverted destinies chasing each other around her skull until the small hours — and surfaced past ten with a headache and hair like a bird sanctuary. She shuffled out of the guest room mid-yawn.

From the sofa came Taeyeon’s unmistakable timbre — curled under a soft blanket, phone in hand, contentedly humming her way through “Strawberry Gummies,” radiating the specific happiness of a woman whose week had started right.

“Mmn~ah.” Sunny stretched until something crackled. “Morning, Taeyeon.”

“Good morning, Sunny-yah~ go wash up and come eat.”

On the table: one boiled egg, one washed red apple — the sacred activity-period breakfast of female idols everywhere.

Cold water restored Sunny to roughly functional. She sat, started peeling the egg, and scanned the room.

“He’s gone?”

“Mm, left at nine — audition work today.” Taeyeon sat up cross-legged, then frowned at a thought. “Wait — do we fly to Japan right after today’s fan-sign, or tomorrow? I didn’t check the schedule properly.”

“YOU’RE the leader. If you don’t know, how would I?”

“It’s been a busy stretch, okay—” Tongue out, sheepish; phone already lifting. “I’ll message the manager eonni.”

Three months of the second Japan arena tour loomed — months of shuttling between countries at idol pace.

Sunny worked on the egg, gaze idling across the face opposite — and slowed mid-chew. Something was off. They had ALL spent a month sleeping four hours a night through promotions; the mirrors had receipts — shadows, dullness, concealer working double shifts. And the couple in the master bedroom had, ahem, demonstrably not turned in early. By every law of biology, Taeyeon should look wrecked.

Instead: bare-faced skin near-translucent, zero shadows, cheeks lit from inside — the finish of a woman leaving a luxury spa, not a comeback grind. This was NOT science.

Sunny got up, crossed over, and pressed a palm flat against Taeyeon’s cheek.

“Mwo-ya? Pervert?” Taeyeon reared back, staring at her like she’d grown a second head.

Sunny ignored this entirely and tested the other cheek. The texture was — no exaggeration — approximately that of the peeled egg in her other hand.

“Taeyeon-ah, what is going ON with your skin today? It’s practically emitting light! Confess — new products? Or did you get the good injections somewhere? Introduce me!”

Taeyeon swatted the hand away — then touched her own cheek, and couldn’t argue with the data. A private sweetness crossed her eyes; her chin tipped up, shameless.

“No injections! Maybe I just… rest well.” A pause; the smile went soft and smug at once. “I don’t know if it’s psychological, but— whenever I sleep in his arms, I sleep SO deep. And mornings after, my skin is always like this. Everything feels light.”

Ears pink. Eyes bright. Not lying — and completely unaware of what she was confirming.

“…Is that so?”

Sunny returned to her egg with her brows in a knot. Good rest improves skin — over months, incrementally. It does not erase a promotion-cycle’s damage overnight. Yesterday Taeyeon had ended the broadcast visibly running on fumes. One night later: factory reset. “Resting well” did not cover this. Which put her right back at the same address: Ji-ho. And his midnight theater about fate-stars and dream-voices — performed to be laughed at, obviously fake, ha ha… except. Looking at this face across the breakfast table, a genuinely absurd thought crept in and sat down: what if the nonsense wasn’t ALL nonsense?

His own February belonged to the film. The pure-love picture wasn’t a big-budget shoot, but the cast ran deep and the relationships ran fine-grained, and none of it tolerated sloppiness. The audition machine ran in order: two days of principal-role sessions judged by Director Kwak, the assistant director, the screenwriter, and the casting director; then three days of large-scale supporting and bit-part rounds, sifted by the casting team and stamped by Kwak.

And alongside the judging, he carried one long, meticulous chore: script breakdown. Actors never receive the god’s-eye screenplay — they receive THEIR script: every scene they appear in, every line and action cue and psychological note, written from their character’s point of view alone. The second lead’s packet reads as if the whole story orbits him. Someone has to split the master script into those per-role documents, scene by scene, with every entrance, emotional beat, and interaction mapped clean. That someone was the screenwriter.

After breakdown would come the table-read phase — the director gathering writer, principals, and heads of camera and art to read, argue motivation, test dialogue aloud, and amend on the spot; the stage where actors build chemistry, and directors of romance films quietly engineer extra proximity between their leads. And for depth and privacy, the industry’s half-open secret applied: nine of ten major directors kept — or owned — a discreet high-end lounge for exactly these sessions, and the tenth had simply built one into his house. He made a covetous mental note: get one. THAT was living. A few rounds in the private room, then the formal all-hands read in a proper conference room, cameras collecting behind-the-scenes material for the marketing cycle. Film scripts, he was learning, got rewritten a dozen times before anyone rolled.

February burned down in that fashion, the cold lifted off the streets, and March arrived green at the edges.

March 1, 2013 · Morning A leased office tower, Seoul The Girl in the Sun — first day of principal photography

The production had lucked into its main location: through LOEN’s network, a subsidiary of SK Group had just vacated an office tower, and the crew leased it at friendly rates — modern interiors that dressed easily into the story’s ad-agency setting, with a suitably urban exterior. Multiple locations, one address.

He arrived in ordinary black suit and backpack — office-worker drag — to find the ground floor already a hive of equipment and tape marks.

“Oh? Our great screenwriter-slash-leading-man has arrived!”

Director Kwak Jae-yong, bright-eyed in a comfortable jacket, waved him over and made the introductions.

“Director of photography — Kim Myung-ho seonsaeng-nim. My partner of many years. The frame is in good hands.”

“Seonsaeng-nim. I’m in your care.”

“And our art director, Lee Sun-mi seonsaeng-nim.”

“Ji-ho-ssi, your script reads with real scene-sense — you saved us a great deal of work. Looking forward to the performance.”

“You’re too kind. Please teach me well.”

Sulli arrived in an ivory trench over knits, fresh-faced, bowing her way around the circle before landing on him with crescent eyes: “Oppa! It’s finally starting. I’m SO nervous.” One tiny theatrical breath. Kwak laughed — “Relax, Sulli-ssi. And Ji-ho: your leading lady, your responsibility.” — “We’ll work hard together.”

The veterans came next — Kim Hae-sook and Sung Dong-il together, warm with everyone; then Lee Kwang-soo in stiffly proper shirt-and-slacks that made the room laugh anyway (“Ani-yo, sunbae-nim! Today I am the VERY serious Team Leader Park!”), Lee Min-ki quiet and observant, rookie Park Gun-woo bowing to everything that moved, and Chae Soo-bin making her respectful rounds.

Then a different kind of stir moved through the doorway — staff voices dropping to excited whispers.

A graceful figure entered in an impeccably cut tweed set, long hair loose, carrying that particular soft-focus serenity that needed no lighting rig: Son Ye-jin. The nation’s melodrama queen, and the reason half the crew suddenly remembered urgent business near the door.

“Aigoo — our most beautiful cameo has arrived!” Kwak’s face split wide.

“Director Kwak. It’s been too long. Congratulations on the new picture.”

They embraced lightly, old colleagues — The Classic had crowned her the queen of pure-love cinema and won him Best Director honors; the partnership ran deep. A cameo in the opening of another pure-love film was friendship first and shrewd marketing second: her name alone raised the picture’s temperature.

“With you here, my heart can finally sit down.” Kwak turned to the room. “Everyone — no introduction needed, yes? Son Ye-jin-ssi, gracing our opening. Welcome her!”

Applause. The veterans greeted her like family. Sulli bowed with stars fully visible in her eyes — “Sunbae-nim! I’m Sulli — The Classic is one of my favorite films ever!” — and got her hand held warmly for it: “I’ve seen your work. A vivid one, you. Take good care of Ji-ho-ssi’s Go-i.” Then the gaze moved to him, and Kwak supplied the frame: “Our screenwriter AND male lead. Young. Alarming amounts of talent.”

She offered her hand first, smile genuine.

“Ji-ho-ssi. The real person, at last. I should confess — I’m a fan. ‘Eyes, Nose, Lips,’ and that ‘Strawberry Gummies’ song — I play them often. The sweet one especially. It improves a person’s whole day, even at my age.”

“Sunbae-nim — the honor is entirely mine.” He took her hand with a respectful half-bow. “The Classic, A Moment to Remember — classics among classics. They shaped how I think about this genre. Sharing a set with you, even for a single scene, is luck I intend to learn from.”

“Mutual admiration, then.” She released his hand, laughing toward Kwak. “Director, this one’s box office is safe. That face alone sells the tickets.”

“Obviously. Why do you think I took the job?”

The room laughed; he managed to look abashed, which made it laugh harder. Lee Kwang-soo surged in — “Sunbae-nim! Lee Kwang-soo! DEVOTED fan! I watched My Wife Got Married five times!” — with gestures that reactivated the whole room. She received all of it with unhurried warmth, a masterclass in top-tier temperament, then excused herself to makeup: “Just the one shot, Director — then you owe me a proper meal.” — “Noted and honored.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Half an hour later, the first AD clapped the room toward the atrium: time for the opening ceremony. Korean film had inherited the ritual — a legacy, like so much of the industry’s craft culture, of the Hong Kong cinema era — and the form held: a white-clothed altar beneath the production banner; rice cakes stacked for ascent; red apples, pears, persimmons for fortune; soju bottles in a row; and at center, the boiled pig’s head, eyes serene, red pepper in its mouth, the traditional plea for the work to be received with honor. At one corner: the first-draft script bound in white ribbon, and a small polished camera model.

The room went properly quiet. Kwak straightened his jacket and led: three bows with lit incense, the sticks set into the burner; then producer, investors, DP, art director, each in turn; then the actors by seniority — the veterans first, then the two leads side by side. Standing with the smoke curling up past his face, he could hear Sulli’s breathing go quick beside him, and feel the weight of a hundred hopeful eyes at his back. Bow. Plant the incense. Step back.

Kwak then read the formal invocation — commissioned, in the old style, from a master calligrapher of classical Chinese — asking clear skies and smooth days, safety for every member, inspiration for the work, and a warm reception when the film met its audience. He touched a corner of the page to the incense flame and let it burn out in the brass basin, the ask carried upward with the smoke.

Then trays of clear rice wine went around, one small cup to every hand. Kwak raised his first.

“For The Girl in the Sun—”

A forest of arms rose.

“SMOOTH SHOOTING! EVERYONE SAFE! BOX OFFICE VICTORY—!!”

A hundred voices in one, punching up through the atrium into the early-spring sky. The cups touched lips; the ritual sip went down clean and faintly hot.

“Good!” Kwak set his cup down, solemnity flipping to voltage. “I hereby declare — The Girl in the Sun: CAMERAS UP!”

Cheers; applause; and the crew detonated into motion — fruit and rice cakes from the altar cut and passed hand to hand so everyone shared the luck, department heads mobbing thekey creatives for final confirmations, actors peeled off toward makeup chairs. He skipped the altar snacks, caught Sulli’s eye in her mirror across the floor — she winked and mouthed “hwaiting” through the powder cloud — and headed for his mark.

The first set — the agency’s open-plan office — stood lit and ready. Gaffers trimmed the last lamp; the dolly grip walked the track once more; the clapper loader took position.

Kwak settled behind the monitors, headphones on, and keyed the radio.

“Final checks, all departments!”

“Camera OK!”

“Lighting OK!”

“Sound OK!”

“Actors — positions!”

Entering the Character — The Late Commuter

BodyShoulders forward, weight on the balls of the feet — a man borrowing seconds he doesn’t have.

EyesNothing exists but turnstiles and elevator numbers. The lens is deleted before “action” is called.

BreathRun the corridor twice before the take — arrive at the mark already winded, so the camera photographs truth instead of acting.

The idol reflex is to find the camera. The actor’s discipline is to lose it.

He walked to his tape mark, drew one slow breath, and let the world outside the frame close like a door — sinking down, degree by degree, into the skin of an office worker running late.

The slate swung into frame.

“The Girl in the Sun! Scene one, shot one, take one—”

Clack.

“Action!”

The camera began its silent glide. Early-spring sunlight poured through the tower’s glass wall, lighting the dust adrift over the office floor — reception staffed, extras mid-commute, a working Seoul morning assembling itself in frame.
