Chapter Eighty-Six

Fault Lines

The penalty section came first.

Exclusive Artist Contract (Draft) · Breach & Penalty
Obligations of the Artist

The Artist shall unconditionally accept all entertainment activities, commercial endorsements, media exposure, and personal schedules arranged by the Company, and shall maintain optimal condition throughout. The Artist shall not independently solicit or participate in any entertainment-related activity without the Company’s written consent.

The Artist’s public image, private conduct, and social-media statements shall conform to guidelines established by the Company and remain subject to the Company’s management.

Liquidated Damages

Should the Artist unilaterally breach this contract without cause, the Artist shall pay the Company compensation calculated as the greater of:

A. The Artist’s average monthly income over the twenty-four (24) months preceding the breach × remaining contract months × 3; or

B. Three (3) times the Company’s total investment in developing the Artist, inclusive of the trainee period.

Injunctive Relief

The Company further reserves the right to seek an injunction barring the breaching Artist from all public entertainment activity for the remainder of the original contract term.

Clause after clause after clause — dense enough to make the eyes swim.

Ji-ho rubbed his face, then flipped straight to the sections that actually mattered: Music Copyright and Revenue Distribution.

His gaze raked down the packed print. Line by line, his brow drew tighter.

Chapter Four · Music Copyright
Works Created During the Term

All musical works (lyrics and/or composition) independently created by the Artist during the contract term shall — including but not limited to the rights of reproduction, distribution, rental, exhibition, performance, screening, broadcast, network transmission, cinematization, adaptation, translation, and compilation — be automatically licensed to the Company from the date of completion.

The Company shall owe no additional consideration for said license; the value of such creation is deemed reflected in the Artist’s revenue share and in the training and debut resources provided by the Company.

Co-Created Works

Where a work is co-created with the Company’s in-house producers (e.g., Director Yoo Young-jin, KENZIE seonsaeng-nim), copyright shall be held jointly by the Company and the creators. Specific allocation shall be separately agreed according to internal contribution — provided that the Company retains final decision-making authority and all commercial operating rights.

Chapter Five · Revenue Distribution
Settlement Mechanism

All income arising from the Artist’s entertainment activities shall first be deposited into the account designated by the Company. Following deduction of: (i) direct activity costs — production, promotion, travel; (ii) image maintenance and team operating costs; the resulting net income shall be distributed between Company and Artist per the schedule below.

Specific ratios may be adjusted by appendix according to the Artist’s development stage and contribution.

Note — where the Artist is the credited creator (lyrics/composition) of a recorded work, an additional 10% of net revenue accrues as creator’s royalty, calculated in parallel with the performance share. Settlement semi-annually; 22% income tax withheld at source (foreign-national artists per applicable bilateral tax treaty).

Foreign-national artists per applicable bilateral tax treaty. That line, at least, someone had drafted with an American passport in mind.

It was, at a glance, the standard SM rookie schedule — with Ji-ho’s extra ten percent creator’s royalty grafted on. Other agencies ran similar numbers, physical albums aside.

Set aside the album and digital lines and the picture looked almost generous: sixty–forty, roughly. But rookies never landed CF deals or acting roles. Variety appearance fees were pocket change. The money that mattered — the only money that mattered — was albums and digital. And there, SM took ninety-five and left the artist five.

Which meant: sign this, and on his own compositions Ji-ho would collect fifteen percent. On anything he didn’t write — five.

JYP and YG started their rookies at fifty–fifty on albums and digital. Everyone in the industry knew it.

And the songs he wrote himself? Automatically licensed to SM — gratis — with the proceeds folded back into that same fifteen percent. His publishing, his catalog, his future, priced into a rounding error.

Ji-ho lifted his head. The deference was gone from his eyes; what replaced it was sharp and cold as a scalpel.

“Bujang-nim. This contract — is it the standard template for all rookies, or was it ’specially prepared’ for me?”

Jo Woo-chul’s smile did not move.

“It is the standard framework the company has developed, over many years, for solo rookies — designed to balance the interests of company and artist and to safeguard a long, stable partnership.”

“Balance?” Ji-ho’s finger came down hard on the copyright clause. “Works I create independently — licensed ‘automatically.’ ‘Without additional consideration.’”

“Which means ‘Some’ and ‘A Boy’ would belong to me on paper while every downstream won they ever generate — OSTs, CF placements, covers by other artists — flows primarily to the company. That’s balance?”

Kim Ah-ju adjusted her glasses, voice cool and level.

“Ji-ho-ssi, please understand. The company invests enormous capital in building your image, engineering your promotion, laying your distribution channels — giving your works the maximum possible exposure and market value.”

“That investment, and that risk, require corresponding rights as security. Licensing copyright to the company is the necessary arrangement for efficient integration of resources and the avoidance of future operational disputes. Many top creator-idols signed precisely such terms in their early contracts.”

“Then look at the revenue.” He ignored the legalese entirely, finger sliding to the split schedule. “Ninety-five to five on the recording, plus the creator’s royalty — fifteen percent, total. A song earns a hundred, and I — its writer and its singer — take home fifteen while the company takes eighty-five.”

He had always known SM drew blood. But bloodletting at this concentration was genuinely educational. Small wonder every artist this company ever raised ended up suing their way out the door the moment they got famous.

His voice rose despite himself, anger and disappointment pressed flat under control:

“Bujang-nim, this contract pours me into the exact same mold as every rookie the company has to package from zero and feed songs to!”

“There is no basic respect in it for what I create. It’s a rock-bottom buyout dressed as a partnership — my future, locked into the vault as company-owned copyright inventory.”

Jo Woo-chul leaned forward, tone gentling into management-speak.

“Ji-ho. The company sees your value — of course it does. But at the rookie stage, the market risk sits entirely on our side. This contract is a starting point, not a destination. As your profile rises and your contribution grows, the renewal ratios have enormous room to climb. Many of your sunbaes walked exactly this road.”

“Painting the future to discount the present?” Ji-ho shook his head once, flat and final. “I don’t accept that starting point.”

“The company has trained me for three months. Don’t run the seven-year-investment speech on me.”

“If the company insists on pricing me like someone it spent five or six years teaching to sing and dance — then debuting at SM loses its entire point for me.”

Jo Woo-chul’s expression never flickered. The copyright clause was wordplay, yes — but if you failed to catch it, that was your dullness, and once signed you’d have no standing to complain. If you did catch it… then the real negotiation could begin.

He smiled.

“Ji-ho-ssi’s situation is somewhat special. The splits — we can discuss further.”

“Tell me what you have in mind.”
