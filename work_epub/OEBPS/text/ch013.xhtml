Chapter Thirteen

Let the Evaluation Begin

Ji-ho took a beat — thoughts sorting themselves at speed behind a calm face — then answered, unhurried:

“First, I’d confirm what I actually saw. Misunderstandings happen. Maybe the contact was incidental; maybe there’s context I don’t have. I’d want facts before anything else.”

“If it turned out to be a genuine breach, I’d talk to him privately first. Lay out plainly what this could cost — him, the team, the company. A broken contract sets off a chain of consequences, and hidden information poisons every schedule built on top of it.”

“Then I’d push him to go to the staff himself and own it — that’s the version of this story where he keeps his future. If he refused… I’d seriously consider reporting it through the proper channel myself, while protecting his privacy as far as I could.”

“Because in the end, solving the problem is the point. We’re all carrying the same dream up the same mountain. I won’t watch one mistake destroy his climb — but I also won’t let it pull the whole rope team down with him.”

Airtight. Was it warm-blooded and heartfelt? Not entirely — and everyone present knew that too. But fielding a question built as a trap, at full speed, without a flicker, in flawless diplomatic Korean? That was a professional skill in its own right, and he had just demonstrated it at a high level.

Yoon Su-jin inclined her head a few millimeters — from her, a standing ovation — and moved on.

“Next question. One month, twelve hours of training a day, and your instructors reject your work at every checkpoint. How do you regulate yourself? Have you experienced anything comparable?”

Ji-ho very nearly smiled. Sunbae-nim, you’re lobbing them straight over the plate now.

This past week, Irene’s ferocity had genuinely awed him — the madwoman had gone to 4 a.m. twice, and never left before one on the other five nights. And every single night, he had outlasted her by his ritual ten minutes. Within the company’s walls, his name already carried a certain reputation because of it.

This question wasn’t a trap. It was a gift.

“It took me thirteen failures to earn this chance — I promise you, no one in this building prices it higher than I do.”

“Since the day I entered the company, I have been the last person to leave the practice rooms. Every night, when I go, every light on the trainee floors is already dark. Twice this week, an instructor flagged my dancing as weak — both nights, I trained until four in the morning before I let myself stop.”

“A dream is a lighthouse — it doesn’t row the boat for you, it just proves the shore exists. Maybe I row slower than others. Maybe I drift off-course some nights. But backward? That stroke isn’t in me.”

As the artist-management judge, Yoon Su-jin’s brief was character and pressure tolerance — and she had done her homework in advance. The reports were unanimous: this week, no one in the company had bled more hours than the boy in front of her. On her sheet she wrote: Character — undetermined, continue observation. Improvisation and public speaking — excellent; candidate for team spokesperson. Under pressure tolerance, she marked a 9.

The battery of assessments rolled on, category by category. Stage presence: a confident, infectious 8. Physical coordination: an honest 5. Rhythm: 7. Camera sense: a serviceable 6. Audience rapport — that easy, magnetic approachability: 8. And languages, his home turf: flawless Korean, native English, not a seam showing — a perfect 10.

Which left the finale. The heart of the entire evaluation: vocals.

And the vocal examiner today was SM’s crown jewel herself — Taeyeon.

It was standing practice at SM to seat artists on major audition panels, usually the senior ones — BoA, the company’s reigning queen; Super Junior’s Eunhyuk; SHINee’s Key. Ji-ho had been flagged and fast-tracked on the strength of his singing; it was his core weapon, and the company intended to test it against the harshest whetstone available. So they had brought in the woman who had once risen above nine thousand competitors on the strength of her voice, to rule on his pitch, breath, technique, and interpretation — and decide whether his ceiling was worth the company’s investment.

Finally. My turn. Taeyeon sat up straight, all business and barely-contained anticipation. When she spoke, her voice was gentleness itself — soft-textured, fine-grained, level and light, like a spring breeze that had learned manners.

“Ji-ho-ssi. May I hear you sing?”

“Ne, Taeyeon sunbae-nim.” A moment’s thought. Ballads were his home country — and one classic in particular had paid his rent across a hundred lounge nights. “I’ve prepared ‘Yesterday Once More,’ by the Carpenters.”

Evaluation rules: a cappella only, no accompaniment, no exceptions.

The reasoning was cold and correct. A backing track guides the melody and carpets the rhythm; strip it away, and there is nowhere left to hide. Pitch stability, breath continuity, vocal placement — all of it stands naked in the silence. A singer who drifts or wavers gets quietly rescued by accompaniment; a cappella, every flaw steps into the light. And the reverse held too: bare of instruments, a voice’s true timbre, its clarity, its resonance, arrive at the judges’ ears unfiltered — and so does the honesty of its feeling, the precision of its diction, everything that makes the difference between singing notes and telling the truth.

Ji-ho drew a slow breath and let his eyes fall closed, sealing the room away, gathering the feeling the way you cup water in two hands. A soft exhale. Then, gently:

Yesterday Once More · Carpenters · 1973

When I was young,

I’d listen to the radio,

Waiting for my favorite songs —

When they played I’d sing along…

The first phrase poured into the air like aged liquor decanted slow — that warm, dense texture spreading through the room, sinking straight past the sternum.

The song unspooled from him like a story told across a kitchen table — a life’s worth of might-have-beens folded into melody. Years of smoke-filled rooms had ground exactly the right grain into his voice: in the low passages it dropped to something like a lover’s murmur against the ear; when it lifted, it cut through clean and bright as birdsong through cloud, carrying to the far wall without ever seeming to try.
