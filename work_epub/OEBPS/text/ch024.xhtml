Chapter Twenty-Four

Depression

Tiffany tilted her head a few degrees and let her gaze rest on Nichkhun — one long, appraising beat — then settled back, expression giving away nothing.

She was, and had always been, unapologetically a visuals person: her type was handsome faces over solid muscle, full stop. And by that metric, Nichkhun scored embarrassingly well.

But her eyes kept sliding back to the other side of the table — to the wide-faced older man planted beside Sulli, and his friend with the full tattoo sleeve. And what exactly are these two?

“Who’s the crusty older guy?” Her eyes cut toward Choi Jae in the smallest possible gesture, voice pitched under the music.

“That’s Choi Jae. He and Sulli are close, apparently. Tonight’s his tab — don’t be shy about it.”

Tiffany’s brow creased. Sulli and… that? He’s got twenty years on her, easily. The math refused to work no matter how she ran it. But the industry had exactly one commandment about other people’s private lives — don’t touch it — and she and Sulli weren’t particularly close anyway. Whoever the girl chose to know was the girl’s business.

She ordered something mid-priced, and the table dissolved into industry gossip, glasses rising and falling like a tide.

✦   ✦   ✦

At 23:30, Ji-ho closed out the first set to genuine cheers, took his bow with unhurried grace, and slipped backstage to the break room. He fished his silenced phone from his pocket: two missed calls. Both from Wendy.

“Ji-ho! That set was UNREAL!” The floor manager pushed in beaming, a fold of bills in hand. “Here — tips. The guests sent these up for you.”

He blinked at the wad as it landed in his palm. Two hundred–some thousand won. On a warm-up night.

“This much?”

“You sang like that, what did you expect?” A clap on the shoulder, the money pressed firmly into his hand. “No wonder Mi-yeon nuna vouched for you like her life depended on it.”

“Manager-nim — you know my teacher well?”

“Know her? Mi-yeon nuna and our boss are cousins. She drinks here half her evenings.”

So that’s the shape of it. The ten-thousand-won wage suddenly made perfect sense — and the debt to Kim Mi-yeon quietly doubled itself in his ledger. He’d remember it.

He tucked the money away carefully and lifted his phone.

“Manager-nim, a friend was trying to reach me — I’ll go return the call.”

“Go, go. Next set’s at one — grab some food in the meantime. Staff discount on drinks, if you’re tempted.”

“Thank you, Manager-nim.”

✦   ✦   ✦

Back in the booth, the temperature was climbing nicely. Tiffany had migrated — quite naturally, somehow — to the seat beside Nichkhun, and the conversation between them had found its rhythm.

“Tiffany — I know this incredible Michelin place. Their desserts are criminal. I guarantee — guarantee — you’d love it.”

“Oh? Which one?”

“Ah — the name’s escaping me right now, of all times. But I’ve got the head chef’s card. Next time I’ll just take you. How about it?”

Is he asking me out?

So far, the impression was… not bad. But Tiffany Hwang was not a girl who came when called on the first whistle. She tipped her head, regretful and impregnable at once.

“Girls’ Generation and 2PM schedules both being what they are right now? Finding an overlapping free day is basically a miracle. Better not.”

He’d expected nothing less — a Girls’ Generation member who was easy to book would have been the real shock. The chase was half the flavor anyway.

“Then let’s at least trade numbers. I’ll add you on Kakao and send the name and address — for the miracle day.”

She considered it… and decided a phone number cost her nothing. They swapped.

Across the table, a different arithmetic was running. Sulli sat with her glass held high and her count long lost — cheeks glowing pink over porcelain, tipsy and luminous and far too young for the company she was keeping.

Choi Jae’s hand darted out and plucked the glass away, tender as a stage magician.

“Sulli — enough, hm? You’re drunk. I’ll take you home.”

Something in her snapped abruptly clear — a cold pilot light of alarm.

“No — it’s okay, oppa. I can get home by myself.”

“Look at the state of you. Let me take you. Or what — you don’t trust me? You think I’d do something to you?”

“Of course oppa isn’t like that.” Her head shook quickly, too quickly. “It’s just — I’m really not drunk, and I don’t want to be more trouble than I’ve—”

And there it was, the old reflex: her eyes flicking up to read his face, checking the weather. He’s only worried about me. If I refuse him, he’ll hate me. He’s going to be angry—

“…I’m sorry, oppa.”

He knew this girl folded. He knew exactly how she folded. He leaned in, patient as a fisherman.

“Just to your building’s door, that’s all. You think I could relax, letting you wander off alone like this? What if something happened to you?”

I don’t want to. No. NO. Two syllables. The simplest sentence in any language — and it would not come out. He was being kind, wasn’t he? He was worried, wasn’t he? If she refused again he’d be hurt, he’d be angry, he’d leave—

Maybe… just this once? Let him take me just this—

Her small fists clenched in her lap. The yes was already at her lips when — without warning — her father’s face surfaced: the flat, indifferent set of it, the day he walked out. Pain lanced through her chest, sharp as glass, and something inside her twisted loose all at once.

“I SAID no thank you!”

“No! No! NO!”

The booth went silent as a dropped call. Every face turned. Choi Jae’s expression cracked — a flash of something dark — before the smile crawled back into place, thinner now.

Whatever else was true, Sulli was a company junior, and Tiffany was not going to sit on her hands while one got hurt in front of her. She straightened, face going serious, warmth pitched carefully underneath.

“Sulli. Hey — what’s wrong?”

“Nothing, eonni. I’m fine.” She was holding the tears back by main force now, voice wobbling on the rims of the words. “Oppa — I’m sorry — I need the restroom.”

“I’ll hit the restroom too.”

Choi Jae rose, tugging at his collar — and the moment the booth door shut behind him, the phone was out and at his ear, voice dropped to a flat mutter.

Outgoing call · muffled, hallway

“Where are you right now?”

“Knew it — you’re never far when there’s fun. Perfect: I’m at Whisper Bar, the one nearby. Bring me the stuff.”

“Move fast. I’m waiting.”

Sulli made it to the restroom on unsteady legs. From her bag she dug out a pill bottle; her hands shook so badly it took three tries to tap two pills into her palm. She swallowed them dry.

Braced on the sink, she watched the tears come anyway — unstoppable, infuriating. Her fists clenched on the porcelain. The temper she couldn’t govern anymore. The doses that kept growing. The illness that grew faster still, always one step ahead of the medicine.

The outer door swung — another guest coming in. She fled into a stall, drew her knees up, buried her face between them, and set about the nightly labor of forcing herself quiet.
