Chapter Twenty

The Joke

They sipped their coffee, and the warmth of it seemed to work some quiet magic, thawing the last of the strangeness between them. The mood loosened into something easy and bright — a stream unfreezing under spring sun.

Seul-gi clapped her hands once, crisp as a teacher calling class, and cleared her throat.

“Seung-wan, Ji-ho — you’re both new, so listen up. Mornings, there’s usually no group schedule; after the gym it’s all self-directed practice. Afternoons are group classes, about two hours each — the timetable’s pinned to the practice-room notice board. Today, if I remember right, it’s dance and vocal.”

“Group classes wrap around nine, half-hour break, then free practice — that’s when you attack your weak points. Choreo’s rough? Drill the details. Rap timing’s off? Live with the metronome.”

“Oh — and after practice, do NOT forget your daily log. Everything you worked on, written up properly. The team leader actually checks.”

“Got it. Thank you, eonni.” Seung-wan nodded along diligently, filing every word away.

Seung-wan was, by any measure, a certified ace. Scouted this March at SM’s global audition in Canada, she spoke English, Korean, French, and Spanish; played guitar, flute, and piano; and owned a voice so distinctive it printed itself on the memory in one listen — an instrument that could thread a song’s emotion through the eye of a needle. The company had rated her so highly at the audition that they’d signed her to a trainee contract on the spot. No two-week probation, no second evaluation. Unlike a certain someone.

“Thank you, sunbae.” Then, turning: “Seung-wan — trade contacts?”

“Of course!”

Numbers and KakaoTalk IDs changed hands, easy as that.

Truthfully, Ji-ho wanted Irene’s contact too — badly. But a hoobae didn’t just ask a sunbae. There were lines, and there would be better moments. He could wait.

The group split for thirty minutes of training, then drifted back together and strolled toward the SM building, chatting and laughing the whole way.

SM’s house aesthetic ran slim and refined; the company actively worried about artists bulking into silhouettes that broke the image. Hence: no gym anywhere in the SM building itself. The one they’d just left was simply the nearest — the unofficial company annex where SM artists and trainees all trained.

Five minutes’ walk returned them to headquarters. Seul-gi had entered SM as a trainee back in 2007; Joo-hyun in 2009 — deep-seniority elders both, and every trainee they passed offered greetings accordingly. Ji-ho, Seung-wan, and Soo-young — the three resident newborns — bowed dutifully to every senior in their path.

The strict etiquette of it all was ordinary Peninsula life, nothing more — but even after twelve years, some American part of Ji-ho quietly chafed at the endless ranked bowing. He’d long ago accepted it. He’d never quite gotten used to it.

“Tae-jun hyung — Joo-hyun sunbae’s here.”

The speaker’s target, Ryu Tae-jun, was a second-year trainee and a rap specialist — and a man carrying a torch. He’d confessed to Irene once already, been turned down flat, and remained entirely undiscouraged. At the sound of her name he assembled his face into what he privately considered his most devastating smile, turned—

—and found her walking beside a tall, straight-backed stranger. No helping it: at 182 centimeters, Ji-ho stood out of the Red Velvet cluster like a crane in a chicken yard. You couldn’t not see him.

At that particular moment, the crane was mid-performance, telling a joke with his whole body:

“Seung-wan, listen. A cannibal tribe catches a trainee, right? The whole tribe comes over, sniffs him, one by one — and every single one shakes their head and walks off. In the end the chief has no choice but to let the trainee go. Why?”

Seung-wan tipped her head, thought hard.

“Because… trainees are too skinny? No meat?”

“Wrong~” He was already losing it, shaking his head, cracking up at his own punchline before it landed. “Heh heh — because — hahaha — because the trainee — pfft — tasted too bitter! HAHAHA!”

The secret of joke-telling, as every bar veteran knows: the joke matters less than the laugh you deliver it with. Laughter is a contagion.

Seul-gi went first, loud and unguarded.

“HAHAHAHAHA!”

“Ah-hahahaha!” Joo-hyun’s threshold for laughter had always been criminally low — the full auntie-laugh burst out of her, face flushing red with it.

Soo-young doubled over completely. Two months of eleven-hour days — nobody at that gym felt this joke in their bones the way she did.

But mid-laugh, Seul-gi and Joo-hyun both faltered — the same memory ambushing them at the same moment: the worst-pressure days, the two of them fleeing to the rooftop to vent and cry together. A sourness rose under the laughter. Since when does a joke make you want to cry halfway through?

Ji-ho caught the shift in the air instantly and rolled straight into the sequel.

“So — the tribe lets the trainee go, right? But now they’ve got no food, and everyone’s starving. The chief goes hunting again — and this time he catches the trainee’s boss. Drags him back to camp. And the tribe is even MORE disgusted. Won’t touch him. They make the chief release the boss too.”

“And the chief loses it — ‘The trainee was too bitter, fine! But the boss?! What’s wrong with the boss?!’ What did the tribe say?”

“Oh! Oh! They said the boss is old — the meat’s too tough!” Seung-wan was fully invested now.

“Heh heh — wrong again — hahaha—” He was wheezing. “The tribe said — hee — ‘Chief, this one’s no good either — there’s not a shred of humanity in him!’ HAHAHAHA!”

“HAHAHAHAHA!”

“AHAHAHAHA!”

The girls went down like dominoes all over again. Joo-hyun laughed until actual tears spilled over, until her legs gave, until all she could do was hang off Seul-gi’s shoulder and dab at her eyes, helpless.
