Thursday 17:45 → Friday 13:10 · October 3–4, 2013 a practice room where the door just clicked shut · a roof garden with one bench and the whole sky for a ceiling · a conference corridor where a secretary opens her toolkit · and, one country and a sea away, a hotel ceiling, a van, a stage, and a corridor floor two women fencing over one man nobody will name · one question asked in a whisper and answered with another · one witness at a stair door · one secretary left holding the wreckage · and one fawn whose body finally votes

The door clicked behind Park Jin-young and the click went through the practice room like a starting pistol nobody had wanted.

Ten women were left in it, and the room reorganized itself around them in about four seconds: the five trainees at the mirror wall, the five of T-ara by the windows, and between the two camps one open expanse of polished wood, empty and neutral as a demilitarized zone that both governments pretend not to maintain.

Bae Joo-hyun packed her bag with her eyes down, unhurried, item by item, a woman tidying a room in which nothing whatever had occurred.

Park Ji-yeon leaned at the window with her arms crossed, letting her gaze cross the floor at intervals, casual as patrol.

The remaining eight did their best with the silence, and the silence did better.

Kang Seul-gi looked at her leader, then at the window, then back, and had the distinct sensation of something condensing out of the air, molecule by molecule, the way rain decides to happen. Son Seung-wan stood where she stood and said nothing, but her eyes had begun making their rounds, an air-traffic controller noticing two returns on a converging heading.

T-ara felt it too. Park So-yeon’s brow creased; she sent a look down the window wall, a leader’s shorthand question — do we intervene? — and Ji-yeon answered it with a small shake of the head and the first half-millimeter of a smile.

“Today’s practice went really well,” Ham Eun-jung offered, into the middle distance, with the brightness of a woman laying a plank over a hole. “Everyone’s syncing up. The teamwork is—”

“It is,” Seul-gi said, catching the plank with both hands, standard-issue politeness at full wattage. “The sunbaenims’ fundamentals are honestly incredible. We learned so much today.”

“Too kind.” Ji-yeon finally spoke, and the laziness in her voice was the lazy purr of something with its weight already shifting onto its back legs. Her gaze crossed the room and settled, precisely, on one person. “You’re not bad yourselves. Especially Bae Joo-hyun-ssi. She dances very well.”

Bae Joo-hyun raised her head, met the look, and produced a smile calibrated to the millimeter.

“Thank you, Ji-yeon sunbaenim. Your performance today was excellent as well. You can tell you’ve been at this for years and years.”

Been at this for years and years. Aish. That was a knife with a compliment wrapped around it. And the worst part: the woman really can dance.

“We have,” Ji-yeon agreed, mild as milk. And then she peeled herself off the window and came across the neutral zone on slow, ringing heels, unhurried, the tap of each step landing like a countdown, and stopped in front of Joo-hyun, and tilted her head to one side.

“Bae Joo-hyun-ssi dances like that — at her age — and still no debut? Whyever not?”

It went in under the ribs, where it was aimed. The oldest trainee in the building felt the old wound take the point and held her face still over it, and breathed.

“Well.” Even, pleasant. “The chairman prizes us. He took us out of SM personally.” A small pause, the kind that loads. “It’s just a pity he’s been so busy with his new album. No time to write our songs himself.” A second pause, the kind that fires. “Otherwise we’d have debuted already.”

A song he wrote himself. She gets songs he wrote himself. I want one. I want one so badly I could bite the mirror.

Ji-yeon blinked the envy away like a speck of light, and pivoted without a seam.

“Bae Joo-hyun-ssi. When you dance, there’s something strange about your eyes.”

“You overpraise me, sunbaenim.”

“It isn’t praise.” One step closer, and the voice came down to a register with exactly two seats in the audience. “You were angry, back there. Because I kissed him. Weren’t you?”

The lashes trembled once, a flicker on a still pond, and the face above them changed not at all.

“I don’t know what sunbaenim means.”

“Don’t you.” A soft laugh; she stepped back and let the volume return to the room. “Then forget it. Although —” and there it was again, that complicated light, passing through her eyes like weather through a window, “— some people, and some things, are more complicated than you’ve imagined.”

Joo-hyun’s fingers tightened on the strap of her bag, and eased. Her chin came up a degree. The smile stayed exactly where it had been, but something in the metal behind it changed grade.

“Sunbaenim may be right. But complicated things are often best left unexamined. It’s usually the people who examine them too closely who get hurt.”

The room’s temperature dropped a full season. Nobody watching could have transcribed the exchange (half of it had been in a frequency only two women were tuned to), but every girl in the room felt the two blades come out from under the courtesy, and go back under it, and understood the language of the scabbards.

Seul-gi’s lips pressed into a thin line; she glanced at Kim Ye-rim and received a blank, honest bafflement back. Soo-young’s eyes went corner to corner, corner to corner, a spectator at a match whose rules nobody had posted. By the windows, Hyo-min and Eun-jung traded one look, one raised eyebrow apiece, a whole committee meeting conducted in a third frequency.

Ji-yeon held the stare for three seconds. And then she laughed, genuinely, and let it go the way a cat lets go of a thing it has decided to bat around later.

“Bae Joo-hyun-ssi, you’re funny.” She turned, took two steps toward her group, and then looked back over her shoulder, because the evening was not done spending itself. “Oh, one thing you should know. He’s considerate, that man. Our first date, he booked the restaurant himself. He’d found out what I love to eat, and he chose the place because of it.”

Joo-hyun’s eyes flickered once, and leveled. She went back to her bag, hands moving at the same unhurried pace as before.

“Is that so. Sunbaenim is close with the chairman.”

“We are.” Ji-yeon’s head tipped; her eyes bent into crescents, and under the smugness, uninvited and unmistakable, ran a thread of actual sweetness. “He’s romantic. And attentive.”

The zipper paused for a quarter of a beat, and completed its run.

“That’s a good thing.” Joo-hyun raised her head, the standard smile back on its hook. “The chairman has always looked after his artists. He’s like that with everyone. Even trainees get his little gifts: he comes down to the practice room himself, and he’s endlessly patient with us.” Her voice softened into something almost fond. “That’s simply the kind of man he is. Gentle. Attentive. With everyone.”

With everyone. …Meaning what: that I’m a line item? That I’m one of many? Everyone.

“Is that so.” The crescents flattened. “Then Bae Joo-hyun-ssi is lucky. You get to see him.”

“I do.” Calm as a lake with a crane standing in it. “Not that he’s there every day; he’s a busy man. When he is, he asks after our training. Whether anything is hard for us. Last time—” the sentence strolled to its point, unhurried, “—last time I was unwell, he came himself. Brought the medicine with his own hands. He really is that attentive.”

“Very attentive.” A smile arrived and showed teeth. “Though, well, look at Bae Joo-hyun-ssi. A face like that. Who wouldn’t take extra care?”

“Sunbaenim is beautiful as well.” The bow that followed was small, correct, and weaponized. “T-ara’s visuals are famous. Everyone says so.”

“Mm. And yet, I think—” one step in, the voice dropping to a register meant for two again, “—there’s something particular about Bae Joo-hyun-ssi. An… air. It draws a person in. Especially—” the pause did its work, and the eyes over it did the rest, “—certain people.”

For the first time in the exchange, Bae Joo-hyun did not smile at all.

“What is sunbaenim trying to say?”

“Nothing at all.” Retreat, with the volume restored and the smog of innocence back over everything. “Only that you’re unusual. I hope we have many chances to get to know each other.”

“So do I.” A nod, the smile returned to its hook, sharpness safely racked. “There’s a great deal I could learn from sunbaenim.”

They held each other’s gaze, and the space between their two smiles filled up with a sound only the other girls could hear: a soft, invisible crackling, like a live wire lying in dry grass.

Then she went, and Soo-bin went with her, and T-ara filed after their senior in ones and twos. Passing the trainees, Park So-yeon caught Seul-gi’s eye and gave her a single nod (well fought, well lost, nothing happened here), and the door shut on all five of them.

The air moved again. You could nearly hear it.

“That was—” Kim Ye-rim began, at last, at a whisper.

“Don’t.” Seul-gi pulled her sleeve.

Bae Joo-hyun stood where she was for a few seconds more, the strap of her bag in her hand, and whatever had just happened behind her face stayed behind it. Then she picked the bag up properly.

“I’m going first.”

“Eonni—” Seung-wan got halfway into it, but the door was already closing on the leader’s back. She looked at Seul-gi, got no help, and made her own decision at speed. “I’ll go check on her.” And she was gone too, quick and quiet, in the direction of the stairwell.

Which left three of them, in a room that still smelled faintly of ozone.

“What was that?” Ye-rim finally exploded, at the volume of the deeply confused.

Seul-gi shook her head slowly.

“I don’t know. But I think there’s something between eonni and the chairman. Something real.”

“You mean—” Park Soo-young’s eyes had gone as round as saucers.

“Don’t guess.” Seul-gi cut it off at the ankles. “We know nothing. We’ve seen nothing. We’re going to be very stupid and very blind until somebody tells us otherwise.”

But there are conclusions that need nobody to say them aloud, because everyone in the room has already moved in and put the furniture where they like it. This was Bae Joo-hyun. Bae Joo-hyun, the composed one; Bae Joo-hyun, who did not lose form in front of other people, ever, whose face in a crisis was a product you could export.

And she had just spent ten minutes fencing with a senior over a man, blade for blade, hit for hit, with a room full of witnesses; and there was exactly one explanation any of them could construct that did not insult everything they knew about their leader.

So they put the furniture where it liked, and stood by it, and said nothing at all.

Memory · the temporary harbor

Because there had been terms, and she had agreed to them, and the terms had seemed simple at the time. She was already his by then; the thing was done. He was excellent (that was never in dispute), and his opening bid had not been flowers but an entire life, quietly furnished: a room of her own two doors from a stairwell, a mattress he had chosen in a shop with his own hands, a kettle, a small fridge with a drawer of labels on it.

A woman can call that many things. She called it a harbor, and told herself harbors are for stopping at, not staying in.

But feeling has never once honored a lease. Love deepened, and jurisdiction shrank: other women began to hurt to look at. His name in the same sentence with another woman’s turned to acid in the ear. She caught herself wanting sole tenancy — wanting it the way you want air — and being ashamed of the wanting, and wanting it anyway.

And under all of it, the question she could not afford: know, and then what? Know the number, know the names, know what his days and nights held in every place she couldn’t see. And then? Could she leave? Could she even want to?

So she chose the ostrich’s profession: what she didn’t see, she didn’t know; what she didn’t know, hadn’t happened. He was merely busy. Those women were merely colleagues. The rent would come due someday. Not tonight.

Not knowing is a room too. You can live in it for years. The rent is everything you didn’t hear.

The van ran south through the early streets with everyone in it at half-mast, nobody talking, the particular silence of a group of professionals before noon. Yoon-a leaned on the window and watched the city go by in pieces (shophouses, a bridge, a crane asleep over a half-built tower), and behind her eyes the ledger kept turning its pages on its own.

Beside her, Kim Tae-yeon looked over, and looked again, the second look a size longer than the first.

“Yoon-a-ah. Are you all right? Your color’s off.”

“I’m fine.” The smile came up from somewhere and did its job. “I just didn’t sleep well.”

The leader held the look for a moment, not believing it and not being able to afford not to, and filed the whole exchange under a tab she kept for later. The van went on south.

At the stadium she put the professional on like the rest of them, and went up the steps to the stage with her number on.

Singapore Indoor Stadium · Friday rehearsal · the autopilot, engaged The track came up and she hit her marks like a woman keeping appointments in another city: here, now here, now the turn; voice out on pitch; arms exact; the smile deployed at the angles the camera crew had asked for on Tuesday. It ran the way a house runs its heating: unwatched, automatic, warm at every register. And underneath the autopilot, three systems failing in sequence. The head first: a slow kaleidoscope with the room turning in it, patiently, half a beat behind the music. Then the throat, every swallow like a coin edge going the wrong way. Then the strength, draining out through the soles of her shoes by the hour, into the plywood, gone. And in the cockpit, on loop, the sergeant: you don’t fall. You don’t worry eight people who have enough to carry. You don’t hand one line to a single comment section that says you dragged. So: smile. Dance. Sing. Again.

The block ended. She came down off the stage on legs she was operating by wire, found a chair in the backstage corridor, and sat in it with her eyes closed, too far gone even to perform being fine for the dressing-room mirror.

Script, album… refuse, or accept? Team, or me? Head aching. Throat aching. Heart, also aching, for some reason. Filed under: everything.

The door beside her opened, and Lee Soon-kyu put her head in, and stopped.

“Yoon-a. You’re really okay?”

She opened her eyes and assembled the smile from available parts.

“I’m fine, eonni. Just tired.”

Sunny looked at her, started to say something, weighed it, and for once in a long life of saying things, put it back on the shelf. She held out a bottle of water instead.

“Drink. And don’t run yourself into the ground; there’s a limit even on you.”

“Thank you, eonni.”

She unscrewed the cap and drank. The water was cold, and it went down and found every place that hurt, and confirmed each one in writing. She said nothing, drank again, one more swallow, the evidence swallowed with it, capped the bottle, and stood up to go be somewhere on time.

Outside, the sky over the stadium had gone the color of old dishwater, low and gray and deciding something. She gave it one look, took nothing from it, and followed the others toward the next room, the next call sheet, the next hour with an owner.

Some choices, no one teaches you how to make. You just stand there holding both of them until one of them holds you.

Song Ji-ho. Tell me what to do.

You can’t, can you. You’re not here.

The corridor tilted, gently, like a boat accepting a swell. The gray window slid sideways. The sound went soft and close, as if the room had put on a coat.

And Im Yoon-a went down the way snow comes off a branch: all at once, quietly, with no cry at all: a body arriving on the corridor floor like a coat dropped from a height, one shoe still pointing, absurdly, at the next appointment.

A rustle. A pause. A soft, wrong sound.

“YAH! Yoon-a, are you all right?!”

“Taeyeon-ah! She’s fainted, somebody—”

“Yoon-a-ya! Yoon-a-ya, can you hear me?!”
