The Cabbage Misunderstanding

Thursday · 20:40 Seoul · Seongdong-gu · Seongsu-dong · the Jang apartment, third floor a recruitment page, a jug in a fridge, and a plan that has begun in the wrong room

It began with milk, and it began the way most of the trouble in this book has begun: correctly, on schedule, and in a building where somebody else’s children could see it.

There was a carton of milk standing on top of the Jang family’s refrigerator, in a paper bag with the top folded over twice, and it had been there for forty minutes, and it had no business being there at all. It was hers: the cycle had opened at midnight on the first of the month, ten days back, and there had been a small carton of it standing on a counter in a kitchen of his own that she had never been inside, in a box with a paper label on it, and somebody — Chae Soo-bin, in the end, who had found it and had been told to put it in a bag and had then forgotten which bag — had carried it up three flights of stairs in a raincoat.

He had no idea, on the way in, why he had brought it. He told himself it was because the girl it belonged to was eating in this house tonight and might reasonably want the last thing she had forgotten; and if he had been asked the question out loud, in a corridor, with a child watching, he would have said something about refrigeration.

That was the whole of the mechanism. A label, a carton, a date, and a thirteen-year-old with far too much access.

Ten days earlier, Jang Da-ah had filled in the LOEN Entertainment recruit training application form in her mother’s name, using a registration number she had copied off a document in a drawer, and had been turned away at the first screen for want of anything to put in the box that asks how long a person has been training, and had told exactly nobody, and had then spent the interval doing what a girl of that age does with a secret: nothing at all, loudly, in every room of the house, until the secret had stopped being hers and started being the family’s.

The reason the rejection had been so hard to carry was that the form had been filled in at the kitchen table while Chae Soo-bin — two doors down, nineteen, in a two-door annex with a kettle, and the only person any of them knew who worked in the building the form had gone to — sat on the floor of this living room with her shoes off, helping with the part about the guardian’s signature. And the reason the evening had gone the way it had gone was that Chae Soo-bin, who is a good girl, had mentioned at about the fourth attempt that the man she works for was in the building, and that he is the one who decides, and that he does not bite.

He had come up. That is the part that will be told at school for a year: that the Chairman came up to a third floor in Seongsu-dong in a raincoat and looked at the form and said, fourteen, next year, and you fill it in yourself, and I will not be here when you do — and then, because he is a man who has never in his life left a thing half-done, had sat down on the floor between a thirteen-year-old and a ten-year-old and gone through the recruitment page field by field, out loud, the way you explain a contract to somebody who is going to have to live inside it.

What she had, in the end, was a dance. She had learned it off a screen at half speed, and then at full speed, and then with the sound off, in this room, between homework and eleven at night, in front of a television that her mother believed was being used for school. She had never had a lesson of any kind in her life. That was the whole of her case, and it was a real one, and the two facts had never been reconciled by anybody in a position to do something about it.

“There is no version of this where you go on a list,” he was saying, at half past nine, with his sleeve on the linoleum. “There is a form, and there is a trial, and there is a room of people your own age who have been at it since they were nine. You have been at it since you were eleven, which is not nothing, and it is not a ticket. If you are still wanting this in April I will look at the name on the sheet.”

“You’re on the sheet?” said Jang Da-ah.

“I am not on the sheet.”

“You’re on the sheet,” said Jang Da-ah, to her sister, in the tone of a person filing evidence. “He says he isn’t on the sheet.”

And that was the moment the subject arrived, because the only other thing in the room that anybody had not been told about was a paper bag on top of a refrigerator, and the only other thing in the world that Jang Da-ah knew about this industry had been assembled out of eleven months of listening at a door.

“What’s the milk for?”

Song Ji-ho was standing at the counter with a water glass he had not filled, which is the sort of thing men do when a question has arrived before their answer has. The fridge hummed. Somewhere below the window a bicycle bell went, once, in the rain, and there was not one single useful sentence available in the room.

There is a version of this where he says: it helps her sleep, that is all, go back to your form. The sentence existed. It was short, it was accurate, and it was going to save him an enormous amount of trouble.

He let six seconds of it go by in silence, which was the first mistake; in a house where two sisters have spent an hour being told that the man in the room knows a great many things about idols, silence is not an answer. Silence is a curtain with something behind it.

“It is the first day of the month,” he said at last, in the tone of a man describing weather, “and the schedule is built around the growth curve of a girl between fourteen and nineteen. That is all it is.”

“That is so weird,” said Chae Soo-bin.

“It is not weird,” said Jang Da-ah, without looking up, in the flat voice of a thirteen-year-old who has just decided to be the adult in the room. “It is a thing that is happening to an idol. Wonyoung has got a list about it. She has got a list about everything.”

“What list?” said Chae Soo-bin. “What list — why does your sister have a list about an idol?”

“Because she is the oldest,” said Jang Wonyoung. She was ten, and had her knees up, and had been eating cut apple in a way that somehow made her look older than all of them. “She writes down who has to do what.”

“She writes down who has to do what,” said Chae Soo-bin, turning the words over like an object picked up in a shop that she does not know how to put back in somebody else’s display.

“I’ll get it,” said Da-ah, and went down the hall, and came back with a sheet of paper that had been written on, folded into quarters, then unfolded and rewritten on the other side, in the round hand of a child who is very proud of her letters — and there, near the top, under a heading that had plainly been added last week, was the line:

There is a particular sound an apartment makes when four people arrive at the same conclusion by four separate roads. It is not a sound at all. It is the second before one: Da-ah looked at the line in her own handwriting, then at her little sister, then at the man by the counter; Chae Soo-bin put her hand over her mouth; and the room filled up with the enormous, unstoppable, entirely inappropriate laughter of children who have understood something grown-ups would rather they had not.

“Milk,” said Chae Soo-bin, to nobody, with her eyes wet. “The great secret of the century is milk.”

The spatula came up. What followed was not violence; it was the two daughters of the house being marched along the corridor of a fifteen-pyeong flat at a speed neither of them had ever needed to move at in order to close a bedroom door, with a laptop under one arm and a folded sheet of rules in a hand that had been told to wash itself first, and a small crash from the dining table where a plate of apples was shoved clear of the fighting.

“We haven’t done our homework,” said Jang Da-ah, one last throw of the dice.

“You are not doing homework,” said their mother, “tonight, or tomorrow, in this house, in front of guests — you are going to bed, both of you, and when the Chairman has gone you are going to get up and write me an apology in a hand I can read.”

“Can we at least say goodnight?” said Jang Wonyoung.

“You may say goodnight,” said the woman, at the door, “and you may say nothing else.”

“Goodnight,” said Jang Da-ah, with enormous dignity, from the middle of the corridor, looking straight past the Chairman of LOEN Entertainment at the jug in the kitchen. “Sleep well after your milk.”

The door of the bedroom came to with the sound of a decision being made, and their mother said, to the room in general, “I am going to move the table. He is going to eat something.” And then, to Song Ji-ho, in the tone of a woman setting down a stone: “You are staying for that. And you can tell me on the way out what a schedule is.”

“What is that?” said Jang Da-ah, holding it up by one ear, at a dining table, in a house, with her mother four steps away.

“It has never been worn,” said Bae Joo-hyun, before anybody else could say anything at all, and the six words in her voice were the six words in the room, and the way she said them was the way she said everything she had decided not to be hurt by.

You people and your packages.”

Later — much later, when the table had been cleared and the dead man’s socks had been worn with total dignity through two kinds of kimchi and a plate of cut pear — the mother of the house stood at the sink and said, without turning round, to the man drying a bowl: “There was a thing on the television about your company and a girl in a group, and it was on for forty minutes, and my daughter came in from school and told me the company was ruined.”

“Yes,” said Song Ji-ho.

“It is not ruined,” said the woman. “Because there are two children in the corridor outside this kitchen who are supposed to be asleep, and one of them has got a list, and the way a child decides a company is ruined is by watching the adults in it. He did. That is the whole of what happened next, and it is worth writing down because it is the opposite of what everybody in the industry assumes a man in his position would do: he put the bowl in the rack, he wiped his hands, he called the two of them into the kitchen at full volume like they were being called in for a hiding, and he stood in front of a thirteen-year-old and a ten-year-old in a small kitchen in Seongsu-dong and told them what the growth schedule is, what the milk is for, what it is not for, and what the number means.
