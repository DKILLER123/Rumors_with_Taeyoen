A Walking Pictorial

Monday → Thursday · 08:20 → wrap the villa’s mirror · a studio running “Urban Lyric” · LOEN’s rollout desk · and a practice room where nobody can see the phone screen but her one trait paid out in shoulders, one studio that forgot to work, four days of the same gasp, one config sheet that emptied wallets in advance; and, in a practice room, a ringing phone and a narrowing pair of eyes

Back in his own bedroom, Monday morning, with the smell of perfect eggs still in the hallway, Song Ji-ho sat on the edge of the bed and opened the ledger.

Warmth rose out of his center like water finding a level it had only just been told about, ran out along invisible threads, and started renovations.

It did not hurt. It did not even itch. It felt like something deep in the machinery being woken, consulted, and improved against its own expectations.

He looked down at his hands first: the joints cleaner, the fingers longer in their proportions by some honest-seeming fraction. Then he was up and across the room to the full-length mirror, and the face that met him was his own, read a second time: the architecture a half-shade more certain of itself, the born-to-it quality in the brows given one more coat of varnish.

The shoulders had come out wider, enough to carry the outline of anything off any rack. The waist had gone the other way, obedient to a plan. Below the waist the legs ran long and straight as if drawn by someone being paid per centimeter. He turned a quarter and looked at the back of himself in the glass: the muscle there lay close and dense, the line from lats to waist closing into a V so tidy it looked typeset.

He rolled his shoulders once. The muscle moved under the skin like water under ice: smooth, packed, cooperative.

Perfect, said the mirror, and for once was not flattering him.

That current just went through me like a tailor with no appointment and re-cut everything I was already smug about, and left every single measurement at its most watchable. Well. Clothes are going to fit like rumors now. No clothes, better still. And with the roster I’m keeping, the option to sell the scenery shirtless is officially on the table.

He laughed at himself, once, and went to burn the observation off under a hot shower.

He came back down a changed climate. Black knit, soft and close, the color of nine at night. Dark casual trousers cut clean, hemmed to stop exactly above the ankle bone, and the ankle itself pale above the house shoes, presented like a signature.

Song Ji-ho · the villa, first floor, 08:40

Downstairs, the living room smelled of butter and eggs. Chae Soo-bin was just coming out of the kitchen with a plate in each hand, heard the stairs, and glanced up on autopilot.

The autopilot quit.

She stood there, plates level, eyes fixed on the man coming down the staircase, the whole apparatus of her paused mid-frame like a film that had lost its power.

Those legs go ALL the way up. Whose legs are those. They were not that length on Friday.

She blinked. Blinked again, in case the first blink had been political.

Same face. Same clothes. No, wait: new clothes, but the same man in them. So why does the picture look re-shot?

He came off the last step and glanced at her, the corner of his mouth moving a millimeter.

“Something on me?”

Her heartbeat filed a formal complaint and her whole system went numb from the neck down. The pause, she would later describe to herself, that the body takes after a beauty attack. Total, clinical, involuntary.

“You— you’re—” The plates stayed miraculously level while her eyes conducted their unauthorized survey, shoulders, waist, legs, shoulders again. “—different?”

“Different how?”

She opened her mouth, audited every available sentence for dignity, and spent the last of her balance on the truth.

“…Better-looking. Sir.”

That sounded insane. A man does not go upstairs to change and come down upgraded. That is not what changing is.

But he DID. Look at him.

He laughed out loud, and the sound of it in the morning kitchen was illegally warm.

“Soo-bin. So the raise landed and I got handsome overnight? That’s very honest of you. I’ll note your pricing.”

Color went up her face like a curtain.

Is it the money? Am I like this? No. No, absolutely not. It is not the salary. It is—

She risked one more sidelong audit, professional and doomed. The shoulders in the black knit. The waist under them. The stance, upright and easy as a young poplar in a courtyard with good feng shui. The legs, longer than her career prospects.

It is NOT the salary.

“Breakfast is served?” He was already walking to the table.

“S-served.”

She fell in behind him carrying the eggs, and her eyes, without submitting a request, attached themselves to his back. The breadth of the shoulders. The strictness of the waist. Below that—

The trouser cut is doing something criminal to the line of those legs. And below THAT—

Her gaze stopped, arrested, on a region of the tailoring she had no clearance for.

…Perked. VERY perked. NO!!! What am I LOOKING at?!

She ripped her eyes away with an audible effort, ears going red to the tips, deposited the eggs at speed, and retreated to her chair with her head down, reviewing her life choices at the ceiling for the rest of the meal.

He, noticing none of the war, picked up a fork and got to the eggs, which were, again, perfect.

“Still the shoot today?”

“Yes. The same magazine crew, photocards today.” She had her voice back to almost-level. “The theme’s ‘Urban Lyric.’ Casual wardrobe, apartment set and street set. Chairman Kim has the call sheet.”

He nodded and ate. The eggs’ reign of perfection continued unchallenged.

✦ ✦ ✦

An hour later he walked into the studio, and the studio’s Monday quietly lost its footing.

The sets had been built overnight: a penthouse corner with a wall of borrowed skyline, a desk-and-leather-chair study, and a street corner standing under its own synthetic night. Wardrobe racks stood in patient rows. Downlights were being trimmed. And by the door, the man who had shot him twice already was adjusting a strobe and looked up, from habit, to see who’d arrived.

The adjustment stopped mid-turn.

Same face. Same client. So why does today feel like a new booking?

“Hoejang-nim.” Kim Min-ho came across the floor, but slower than usual, his eyes walking the approach. “Have you… been training?”

“More movement than usual, lately.”

The photographer circled him once, entirely without shame, the way he circled props and lights: down the shoulders, across the back, along the leg line, and back up.

“The proportions have come in.” He clicked his tongue, half in disbelief. “Day one we shot the shirt. Day two the suit. Neither day did the geometry announce itself like this. Today—” a small gesture at all of him — “today it’s a ready-made tear sheet.”

“I put in some work for the album’s sake. I’m glad it reads.”

“It won’t just read. It will bully.” Kim Min-ho said it with the flat authority of a man appending a fact to a file. “Twenty years behind cameras. Proportions are a one-second read for me, and I’m telling you: measured against working models, this stands in the front row. Today we don’t take pictures. Today we take inventory.”

He said nothing to that, and smiled the smile of a man with a stat panel in his pocket.

At the racks, a wardrobe assistant looked up at the commotion, one look was the whole ambush, and the hanger in her hand swung free of her fingers and was caught, famously, three centimeters off the floor.

Who ALLOWED this. He’s been handsome since debut, that’s public infrastructure, but today? Today is a different department entirely. The shoulder-to-waist on him. The LEGS.

Her colleague leaned in along her shoulder, following the stare.

“What?”

“Look at the chairman.”

The colleague looked. The gasp she drew was long enough to be a mood.

“My god—”

The two of them stood with the hangers, eye to eye, sharing the exact same thought in the exact same font: what is the MATTER with this man today.

At the makeup mirror the artist met him with a powder puff already loaded, and stopped before her first stroke.

“Hoejang-nim, your skin today.” A slow blink at her own reflection in his. “This is better than it was two days ago, and two days ago it was good. At this state I hardly have a job. I’ll spot-correct and get out of the light’s way.”

“Then keep it simple.”

She kept it simple. It did not help. Her eyes kept slipping off their own work: down the shoulder line in the mirror, across the collarbone, along the neck; and twice she had to recount what she was doing with her own hands.

Get a hold of yourself. He is a client. He is a face. The face is being paid for. Breathe.

Her pulse declined the instruction, and the first setup was called.

✦ ✦ ✦

Set one: the borrowed penthouse, the wall of false skyline. He stood at the glass with his arms folded, a quarter-turn in, eyes on the middle distance as if the skyline owed him a favor.

The shutter went once, and Kim Min-ho made a sound photographers mostly save for weddings.

“Hold that body. Change the face only, give me the window again… there.”

He shifted, unfolded one arm, and set his hand along the window frame as if he owned the building behind it, and the pose opened his back like a chart of the year: shoulder into waist, the whole V of him drawn in one line.

The shutter went again, twice, and settled.

By the monitors, a rank of female staff had assembled itself without any order being given, at parade rest, eyes front.

“Are you seeing the back?” one of them whispered.

“I’m seeing the back.”

“And the waist on that.”

“The legs are frankly showing off.”

Four throats swallowed at once, in unison, like a section.

Set two: the study. A dark suit now, the shirt collar severe, him folded into the leather chair behind the desk with one hand flat on the blotter and the other loose at his side, head bent as if weighing a decision only chairmen get to weigh.

The shutter ran a small burst. Through the viewfinder the man seemed to sit inside a light that was being rationed somewhere else in the room; still, seated, doing nothing, and impossible to look away from. Gathered light, an engineer would have said, compounding with the watching. The watching was heavy.

“Now the lens. Head up, eyes to me.”

He raised his eyes straight down the barrel, and the front rank of the staff section inhaled as one organism. Amber, that look, and deep past all reasonable depth, the eyes of a man who has an ocean and knows where it keeps its weather.

“My heart—” one of them said, hand pressed flat to her own chest, conversational and dying. “Is going to lodge a complaint.”

The colleague beside her nodded with the violence of the fully agreed.

Set three: the synthetic night, the street corner. A trench cut clean as a headline, chin tipped up into the rigged lights, the blower throwing the hem of the coat out at an angle: one long shin stepping out of the silhouette like a rumor confirming itself.

Kim Min-ho went to the side, changed lenses without looking at his hands, and then the shutter stopped pretending to be punctuation. Burst. Burst. Roll. He fired until the buffer complained, muttering one long sentence in the language of men who have stopped photographing subjects and started documenting artifacts.

Every angle holds. Every posture survives three looks and asks for a fourth. The light lands on him and something chemical happens on the way back. I am not shooting a pictorial. I am cataloguing an object the museum doesn’t know it’s getting.

The rank of staff had given up all pretense of work. They tracked him across the set, shoulder, waist, leg, shoe, and back up the other side, on a loop that no one was even pretending to break.

“Motion carried,” one announced quietly. “The chairman is my new wall.”

“Last week you were fully devoted to that boy group’s visual.”

“Set that visual next to this man and try to keep a straight face. I’ll wait.”

No one could. The motion carried unanimously.

He changed the pose a final time; the coat swung; the pale ankle came out of the trouser hem for half a second like the last coin in a magic trick.

“Even the ANKLE—” A hand went over a mouth.

“Bone and flesh in perfect ratio, I’m— the ankle has a RATIO—”

Two hours had passed. Nobody had checked a clock once.

✦ ✦ ✦

At wrap Kim Min-ho lowered the camera and let out a breath he had apparently been holding since the morning, walked over, and stood in front of him for a second like a man drafting a toast.

“Hoejang-nim. Twenty years of this trade. Today is the set I’ll be smug about at retirements.”

“Thank you, seonsaengnim.”

“Not flattery: inventory.” He shook his head once. “A build like this, an air like this; a career goes by without either. When this album is done and the world has seen the cards, call me. I want the next ones.”

“I’ll call you.”

He came out of the changing room in the black knit again, and Chae Soo-bin materialized with a glass of warm water and an expression she had not fully cleared.

“Something still?” He took the water.

She opened her mouth, shelved three sentences, and filed the fourth at a murmur.

“You’re really… not the same today, Hoejang-nim.”

“In what way, not the same?”

She thought about it honestly, which was her mistake.

“It’s the… can’t-look-away kind. It’s still your face and still your clothes. But the eye keeps going back for one more, like a plate that refills itself.” She bowed slightly, appalled at herself. “That’s all. That was the whole report.”

“Soo-bin-ah.” He was enjoying this at a level he made no effort to hide. “Your review quality has gone vertical since breakfast. The raise is already effective, but noted.”

She went pink to the collarbone and studied the floor’s grain.

“It was TRUE—”

He laughed, set a hand briefly on her shoulder, and walked for the door with the gait of a man being followed by good news.

“Car’s out front. Home.”

The three days that followed kept the appointment. Same studio, same magazine crew, new batches of staff rotating through with the sets, and the reactions ran identically, like a distribution study. Stage one: the freeze. Stage two: the second look, taken without permission. Stage three: the failure to leave. Stage four: the silent, synchronized swallow.

Kim Min-ho closed each evening with the same verdict in different words, and on the Thursday he delivered it straight into the middle of the crew so it would be witnessed.

“Take the music away from this man and put him on a runway payroll. Heaven allotments like this are wasted on hobbyists.”

Heaven’s allotment, nothing. Heaven did not co-sign this. Eight girlfriends. EIGHT. Do you know what I spend on scheduling alone? On birthdays? On remembering who was told which version of the weekend? This body was negotiated. Clause by clause. I have EARNED this V.

The last card set of the last day wrapped at six, and Kim Min-ho crossed the floor with his hand already out, and the handshake went on a beat longer than business.

“Hoejang-nim. Four days. The most fun I’ve had at work in years. Whatever this project is: I’m in for the next one, name your date.”

“You’ll have it. Thank you for these four days.”

“Thank YOU. Shooting you is not labor.”

Around them the crew nodded as one body. It really wasn’t. It was, several would say on the way home, the best view in Seoul, and they had been paid to stand in it.

Five tags, one fandom at war with itself in every thread, and buying tickets to the same fifteenth of October either way.

Song Ji-ho read none of it twice. He had, that Thursday evening, scrolled past the whole campaign with the calm of a man reading his own name in a language he invented, and then stopped on a different contact card altogether: the one filed under a deer.

She played her move at that club table: the calendar, the five fingers, the Busan window. Manners say a move answered is a game alive. And which way the deer falls at the end of it, one question is left open.

The whole peninsula is going to get to watch it answered.

He tapped the card, and put the phone to his ear.

Across the river, in a practice room with the mirrors fogged at the corners, Girls’ Generation was dying politely in formation.

The set list for the next tour was on the wall, and the wall was unsympathetic. On the eighth count of the second chorus, Choi Soo-young threw her waist into the turn and her long black hair came around like a weather event, full across the face of Kwon Yuri.

“Aish, Choi Soo-young! Your hair! A tie! Exist!”

“No.” Pose reset, waist loaded, hair primed. “The hair is the charm. The hair STAYS.” And she threw the turn again, deliberately, and the weather landed a second time.

“Charm. CHARM, she says.” Yuri was on her like a closing argument, arm around the neck, knuckles reporting for duty. “I have endured this charm for a YEAR—”

“Kk! Kwon Yuri! You are DEAD—”

The formation dissolved into a two-man insurrection, and the rest of the members downed tools with the good conscience of veterans, arranged themselves at the edges, and watched.

Order, in theory, had an officer. Tiffany stepped into the light with one hand on her hip and the other holding the last shreds of the schedule.

“YA! Kwon Yuri! Choi Soo-young!” The disciplinary face assembled itself, ninety percent officer, ten percent leak. “Two weeks to tour opening! Missing counts, wrestling matches, is this the show? Shall we sell tickets to THIS?”

“Mm.”

“Mm-hm.”

“…Excuse me. Are you two MMM-ING me?” She advanced into grabbing range on pure principle, and was instantly, expertly captured, one arm each, and two sets of veteran hands went on the offensive, one to her waist, one to the neighborhood of her chest, with the precision of a trained unit.

“AAAH!!! PERVERTS! BOTH OF YOU!!!”

The room laughed the way only rooms that had seen this exact war many times could laugh. Kim Taeyeon clapped her hands twice, skipper to her bones.

“Ten, everybody. Water. And Soo-young, the hair goes UP for the tour, I am telling you now, not at dress rehearsal.”

Im Yoon-a breathed out, dragged herself to the corner, and sat down on the floor with her back to the mirror wall, hairline damp, chest working. The phone lying beside her bag lit up and rang: a doorbell chime of a ringtone, absurd in the war zone.

She looked at the screen.

And the tired left her face all at once, replaced by something quick and unreadable that a sharper colleague might have called recognition, with the safety off.

She was up and out the practice-room door in four strides, phone to her ear, the door swinging shut on the noise behind her, and none of the collapsing, gossiping, water-drinking members saw it happen.

Except one.

Lee Soon-kyu narrowed her eyes at the closing door, slowly, in the manner of a small observant radar dish, and said not one word to anybody.
