Chapter Five

How to Win Friends and Influence People

The first step of the dream — twelve years overdue — was finally on the board. Joy hammered inside Ji-ho’s chest, but he had long since learned to keep his face calm while his heart rioted.

“Thank you. Thank you all — you’ve worked hard today.”

Kim Mi-yeon had taken a real liking to him, and it showed in the extra care of her instructions.

“For overseas trainees, the company provides dormitories — you’re welcome to move in, or rent on your own if you’d rather.”

“Don’t worry about your visa. The company will handle it.”

“And if you need it, there’s a living stipend you can apply for.”

In the end, Ji-ho passed on the dorm. A man carrying a reality-bending game system in his head had no business sharing a bunk room with six teenagers. He finished the paperwork, sat through the physical, had his height and weight logged, and made his way back to the hotel, where a convenience-store haul of bread and bottled water stood in for dinner.

A quick shower, then flat on his back on the bed, staring at the ceiling and thinking about the road ahead.

SM Entertainment. Blessing and curse in one corporate logo. Their artist contracts were infamous — bloodsucking things, merciless down to the decimal point — but no company on the Peninsula manufactured stars with anything close to their efficiency. For Ji-ho, who carried his own cheat code, the trade skewed favorable: let the star factory do what it did best, ride the system’s rewards, rise fast. Fame first. Because in this industry, fame and fans were the only currency that ever bought an artist leverage.

He was deep in reconstructing his old in-game route when the phone lit up.

He huffed a laugh. Right — he was eighteen again, and this was the war chest: two years of bar tips and busking money, not even four thousand dollars. Seoul was not a cheap city, and rent was coming. The money would hold for a while, but a part-time gig needed finding, and soon.

The clock read 19:50. Ten minutes to eight — to the God of Learning’s golden hour. No chance he was wasting it. The only question was what to feed it.

The next threshold sat at 50, and threshold rewards were the best rewards in the game. Looks and voice were near their natural ceilings; the low-hanging fruit was physique and learning.

Iron for the body. Books for the mind.

He ran a quick search for “books everyone should read before 25.” One title kept surfacing: Dale Carnegie’s How to Win Friends and Influence People. He found a copy and dove in, fully absorbed from the first page.

Field Notes

Carnegie’s rules for dealing with people:

One — become genuinely interested in other people.

Two — smile.

Three — remember names. A person’s name is, to that person, the sweetest and most important sound in any language.

Four — be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.

Five — talk in terms of the other person’s interests, and make them feel important — sincerely.

“Fifteen percent of one’s success is due to technical knowledge — and eighty-five percent to the ability to express ideas, to assume leadership, and to arouse enthusiasm among people.”

The hour dissolved without him noticing. He stretched, closed his eyes, and tested himself — and found every passage standing in his memory exactly where he’d left it, sharp-edged and whole, ready for use.

God of Learning. Absolutely unfair. I love it. One hour a day was the only complaint. He remembered skills like this needed 100 proficiency for the first level-up — call it three months of daily use. Fine. He could do three months standing on his head.

Study hour spent, his thoughts snapped back to MeTube. How had the morning’s video landed?

The hate he skimmed past without a flicker; he couldn’t even be bothered to delete it. He knew this species too well — feed them once and they multiply. And anyway, they were the minority. Most of the comments were rough, good-hearted cheering from strangers. The world ran grey, sure. But when the grey caught the light, what showed through was white.

He got up and checked the mirror.

Eighteen. Skin still carrying that ridiculous collagen glow of real youth, stacked on top of an 8-point face — the kind of handsome that needed no filter and asked for none. Fresh from the shower, hair still damp, a loose tee hanging easy off his frame, collar dipping just enough. The whole effect was lazy, clean, and disarmingly real.

He brewed a cup of lavender tea, blocked out his shots in his head, and hit record. On screen, his hand raked back and forth through the wet hair — careless, unstyled, catastrophically effective.

“Guys. I’m back from the audition.”

“I sang ‘Bleeding Love.’ Leona Lewis. If you haven’t heard it, fix that tonight — it’s the kind of love song that ruins you a little. The good kind of ruin. The kind that starts with butterflies and ends with a heart monitor.”

“So here — a few lines, just for you.”

Bleeding Love · Leona Lewis · 2007

But I don’t care what they say,

I’m in love with you…

I keep bleeding, I keep, keep bleeding love.

“Goodnight, everyone.”

He was up before the sky finished paling. Washed, dressed — a simple light-toned hoodie set, canvas sneakers, clean and bright as the morning itself.

Seven a.m., practice room, SM basement level two. A scatter of trainees were already stretching along the mirrors.

Heads turned at the unfamiliar face — a flick of surprise, then a second look, then a third. New provisional trainees weren’t worth memorizing; ninety percent washed out inside two weeks. And in a building this oversupplied with beautiful people, an ordinary pretty face didn’t rate a glance. To make people look twice here, you had to be exceptional.

Like the trainee from Daegu everyone already knew by name — Bae Joo-hyun. By the system’s ruthless arithmetic, her face graded a 9.

A reserved Daegu-born trainee whose immaculate composure hides a thoughtful, quietly romantic nature.

Ji-ho’s 8 still put him squarely, undeniably, in the handsome column.

New environment, first day, lowest rung. Ji-ho kept his courtesy polished to a shine — a bow and a bright greeting for every person he passed, the smile never slipping. The Peninsula lived and breathed its seniority culture, and SM enforced etiquette like scripture. Sunbae and hoobae, greetings and bows: get that wrong, and talent wouldn’t save you.

Get it right, and the doors had a way of opening themselves.
