Chapter Two Hundred Thirty-Three

Ji-eun and the Lyrics That Could Not Be Cleared

August 4, 2013 · overcast LOEN Entertainment · the music production suite a magic pen, a sky-blue notebook — and a draft that gets rejected on arrival

August 4. Clouds over Seoul, and LOEN back at full working hum.

Acquiring a mature company had turned out less crushing than advertised — the machine ran on its own rails. Morning was meetings and signatures. For the afternoon, he planned to shut a door somewhere quiet and think properly about his own comeback album. So he walked to the production suite.

He had barely left when Ji-eun arrived at the chairman’s office wearing a smile that preceded her by a full meter, hand already raised to knock.

“IU-ssi — the chairman isn’t in his office.”

“Oh?” She turned to the secretary. “I had something to discuss with him. Would you know where he went, Lee-biseo?”

“He just headed to the music production suite.”

“The production suite?” A soft echo, then the smile again. “Thank you.”

“Ne. Take care.”

Floating floor, absorption and diffusion panels, double-glazed isolation, silent dedicated HVAC. You could scream in the hallway; the room would never know.

Ji-ho powered up the console with the ease of long habit, and drew THE CREATOR’S PEN from the system’s inventory — an unremarkable black fountain pen that was anything but.

He opened his notebook to a blank page, set the nib down, and was one breath from testing INSPIRATION SURGE when the door knocked.

“Come in.”

“Hoejang-nim~”

A young, sunlit face leaned around the door frame.

Not the kind of beauty that detonated on first sight — the kind that compounded on every viewing: small precise features, snow-clear skin, eyes that curved into crescents and made the whole face move.

“Am I interrupting?”

“Not at all.” He waved her in, smiling.

She came in with both hands behind her back, light on her feet as a sparrow, craned her neck at the blank page in front of him, and asked:

“Inspiration yet?”

“Not yet.” He hooked an arm around her shoulders and sat her down beside him. “You came looking for me specifically?”

“Mm!”

She nodded hard, and produced — with a magician’s flourish — a sky-blue notebook, holding it out with both hands.

“You said we’d do my title track together — my lyrics, your composition. Well, I FINISHED. Read it read it read it~”

“Already?” He took it, genuinely curious.

“The second I knew we were collaborating I couldn’t STOP. The inspiration just kept coming!” Her eyes were fixed on him, glittering with expectation. “Look at it — how is it? Honestly!”

“All right, all right.”

He began to read, properly.

There was craft in the question of sequence — music first, or words first. Track-first, the industry’s dominant mode: the producer builds the skeleton — melody, chords, drums, color — and the words come after, bending themselves to the rhythm’s shape. Hooky, stage-ready, fast to manufacture, easy to explode; also prone to lyrics that rhyme beautifully and mean nothing. Ninety percent of idol title tracks were built exactly that way. Lyric-first was the other church: the words come first and the melody follows their breath — slower, harder, unfriendly to dance tracks, and the natural home of ballads, OSTs, and singer-songwriters. Of artists, in other words, like the woman currently vibrating with anticipation at his elbow.

Their first collaboration had been a lyric-first song about morning glories and autumn — now a seasonal fixture, and privately, the song in which she’d first begun to fall. So this second collaboration meant more to her than she was saying. She’d spent two days on nothing else, drafting and redrafting around the album’s stated theme — twenty-one, adulthood, maturity — and this notebook held the version she believed in.

He read.

The opening verse: I’M TWENTY-ONE — a warning that she might bring pain, a plea not to pass her by regardless. Fine. Confident, age-forward, on-theme. He kept reading. The next stanza requested, in escalating detail, a single night — blooming in someone’s arms. His brow ticked. The verse after that described preparing the house in advance, lighting every candle… and specified a black-and-white outfit. And there his understanding arrived, fully formed and slightly sweating: this was not a lyric. This was a DIARY ENTRY. Of a very specific recent evening. The black-and-white “outfit” was the maid costume. And the stanzas that followed — the never-shown expressions, the asking what flavors and colors he preferred, the offer to take the initiative, the request to be led somewhere breathless — dispelled all remaining ambiguity about genre.

The nation’s little sister, owner of the cleanest face in Korean pop, had handed him a 19-rated confessional and called it a title track. Ssss. He scratched his head. Very on-brand, actually — the egg principle: pristine white outside, alarmingly golden within.

She was watching him like a puppy watches a plate.

“Weeeell? How is it?”

“Mm~” He looked at the transparently innocent face before him. “Ji-eun-ah. I asked you to write LYRICS. Not a diary. Why is that entire night in here?”

“What? Why not?” Genuine incomprehension. “Songs are supposed to come from real feeling! That night is a memory I’ll keep my whole life — the happiest I’ve ever been. And I’m twenty-one, an ADULT, who became a woman in your arms. That’s literally the album concept. It fits PERFECTLY.”

He set the notebook down, took her hand, and assumed the tone of a man defusing something.

“I’m not saying it’s badly written. The narrative, the feeling, the imagery — genuinely good. That’s not the problem.”

“The problem is that this song doesn’t fit twenty-one-year-old you. If you love it — hold it. Release it at twenty-five. Twenty-seven. It’ll land completely differently.”

“Ani — why doesn’t it fit me?” She dug in immediately. “I’m twenty-one! That’s EXACTLY dating age! And we ARE dating — that’s just a fact!”

He rubbed the back of his neck and laughed helplessly.

“Ya. Think it through. Your lyrics. My composition. Both names in the credits. That is a PUBLIC ANNOUNCEMENT, in liner notes, that Ji-ho slept with Ji-eun.”

She pushed her lips out and muttered at the floor.

“You DID sleep with me. It’s just true.”

His breath performed a brief system halt.

“Yes. It’s true. It does not follow that we ANNOUNCE it via album.” He exhaled. “At twenty-one you write perfume and high heels. Anticipating love. Dreaming about it. That’s where you are on paper. You don’t SKIP the entire relationship and open at the bedroom.”

“‘Don’t skip the relationship,’” she muttered, mostly to herself, volume dropping to grievance frequency. “YOU skipped the relationship. When did I get courted, exactly? Where were MY flowers—”

“What was that? Louder.”

“…Nothing.” One last attempt, eyes big: “So the draft really — REALLY — can’t be used?”

“It cannot. Not now, anyway.” Verdict, final. “Rewrite.”

“Hmph! Fine! REWRITE!”

She repossessed her notebook with maximum force and marched out, ponytail swinging with indignation.

He watched the door bang and shook his head, laughing. With him in this timeline, THE incident — the slip that had once scarred her career — would never happen. But the girl clearly had an appetite for walking close to edges; whether that was strategy, provocation, or just an honest taste for voltage, he genuinely could not tell. Probably all three. It usually was, with her.

✦ ✦ ✦

The mood for writing had left with her. He capped THE CREATOR’S PEN, returned it to inventory, and let the studio go dark.

Tomorrow was Tiffany’s birthday party — and Girls’ Generation’s sixth debut anniversary. He’d promised her something worth remembering, and the arrangements were nearly set.

And tomorrow held the other thing, the heavier thing: Taeyeon. Since learning about Tiffany she had neither raged nor wept nor asked a single question — just gone quiet, while the two of them were run ragged by promo schedules for the very songs he’d written. No scene, no fight. Which, with Taeyeon, was not peace. It was pressure, banking.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow he’d finally have the chance to reach her — and he intended to prepare for it like a man who understood exactly how much was riding on the answer.
