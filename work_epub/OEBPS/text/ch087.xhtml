Chapter Eighty-Seven

Main Quest — “Official Debut”

An SM rookie facing the company negotiated from the bottom of a well.

Field Notes

First: the penalty was crushing. Every debut-track trainee carried five to seven years of training on the company’s books. Call it eighteen million won a year in instruction alone — with everything else folded in, at least twenty-two million. Five years came to one hundred ten million won. Breach at treble damages meant three hundred thirty million — in the American math Ji-ho still ran reflexively in his head, somewhere north of a quarter-million dollars. A chain gauged precisely to hold a trainee still.

Second: the funnel was merciless. Across the industry, short-term academy students, audition entrants, and unsigned hopefuls numbered around half a million a year. By KOCCA’s own census — the big three and every mid-tier shop included — formally contracted trainees totaled just 1,170. SM at its absolute peak carried perhaps 150. Becoming a signed trainee was already a war.

Girls’ Generation averaged very nearly five full years of training apiece; the longest served seven. Monthly evaluations. Quarterly culls. By the time a trainee reached a debut offer, they had survived a decade’s worth of attrition compressed into adolescence — and nobody who crawled out of that funnel had the leverage, or the nerve, to argue percentages.

Ji-ho harbored no fantasy of extracting a superstar’s contract as a rookie. Most of the schedule he could live with. But the copyright — the copyright he would hold in his own hands, or not sign at all.

Even the five percent on songs he didn’t write, he could accept. SM’s splits were stingy, but no company on the peninsula spent harder on production and promotion. Everything in this business was a trade.

But fifteen percent on his own compositions, and perpetual automatic licensing of his catalog? Never.

He thought for a moment, then laid it out.

“First — copyright. Works I create independently, I hold. The company receives exclusive agency and operating rights for the promotion of my career, revenues split by industry standard.”

“But major copyright decisions — outright sales, special licensing — require my written consent. When the contract ends, the exclusive agency right ends with it; on renewal, it renews.”

“Second — album and digital revenue. On songs I both write and sing, my total share comes to no less than forty percent.”

Kim Ah-ju’s face darkened.

Jo Woo-chul’s smile finally, completely, went out. He stared at Ji-ho and spoke slowly.

“Ji-ho. Do you understand what you’re asking for? This is very nearly an overturning of the company’s standard framework. You’re not negotiating — you’re challenging the foundation the company operates on.”

“My terms aren’t excessive. I’m asking fair price for the value I create.” He gave no ground, not an inch. “Lee Soo-man seonsaeng-nim told me timing is everything. My songs and my judgment are the timing.”

“If the company can only see me through its rookie mold, then perhaps the ‘value’ and ‘timing’ we each mean are not the same thing. SM is a great ship — but this song of mine doesn’t have to ring out from this particular deck.”

He was not a trainee lashed to SM’s mast. Quest by quest, stat by stat, he had grown into someone who did not need this building to exist.

“And you think SM can’t live without you?” Jo Woo-chul’s laugh came out cold. “Talent? Skill?”

“This city is drowning in people who write well and sing well — and most of them are scraping by at the poverty line!”

His voice hardened further, weight leaning into every word, pressure applied like a hand on the back of the neck:

“You say your songs are good? Where are the receipts?”

“I have watched a hundred so-called great songs hit this market and sink without a single ripple!”

Ji-ho absorbed none of it. His answer came clean and ordered.

“I never said my songs were good. The company came to buy them.”

“As for whether they’ll chart — if SM can’t judge that much, it wouldn’t be sitting at the head of the big three. Would it?”

The air in the office went solid.

The pressure had failed to land — and pressure that fails to land recoils.

Jo Woo-chul settled back into his chair, silent for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed, and gathered up the draft.

“I’ll report your opinion — faithfully — to the director and the sajang-nim. But Ji-ho, I have to warn you. The company has principles, and it has bottom lines. Concessions run both directions, and what you’ve proposed today may read upstairs not as a concession but as… a threat. That will materially affect the company’s confidence in you, and the resources it had planned to tilt your way.”

“I can tell you this much. For ‘Some,’ the company intends to pair you with Taeyeon herself.”

“Think about Taeyeon’s popularity. Think about Girls’ Generation’s fandom. That song releases and it detonates on impact — can any other company on this peninsula hand you that?”

“I understand the risk, and I know exactly how rare the chance is.” Ji-ho rose to his feet. “But I believe my work deserves this respect. If the person who created it won’t defend its basic worth, then whatever glory it wins later — how much of it ever truly belongs to the creator? I’ll wait for the company’s answer.”

The negotiation broke without a handshake.

Ji-ho walked out with his spine straight, and did not look back.

Jo Woo-chul watched the door close, kneaded the bridge of his nose, and turned to the legal representative.

“Word for word. His statements, his ‘proposals’ — draft the report and send it up to Director Lee Soo-man and Sajang-nim Kim Young-min.”

“And notify the conduct-monitoring team. Their day-to-day attention to Ji-ho can become… somewhat more detailed. We need a fuller evaluation.”

Fuller evaluation. Or: a handle to bend him with. Or: one more chip for the table.

✦   ✦   ✦

Ji-ho stepped out of the SM building into a Seoul afternoon bright enough to sting.

He had just pushed away, with his own two hands, the opportunity he’d once have crawled for. He did not regret it.

“Some” and “A Boy” were not merely his door-knockers into this industry. They were the bedrock of his musical world.

If you surrender the bedrock, what exactly is the palace standing on?

And there would be more. Better. Song after song — he could not fold to SM now without folding forever.

His value had to sit in his own hands from the very first day. Even if all his hands held right now was a draft that might tear in half.

He looked back up at the tower — and thought, unbidden, of the blurred edges of last night’s goodbyes with Ji-eun and Jiyeon; of Irene and Wendy; of Taeyeon and Tiffany…

The warmth of all those tangled threads against the cold arithmetic he’d just walked out of — the split ran straight through him.

And yet, standing in it, he went calm.

There has never been only one road.

And there are always more methods than doors.

“Did you hear? Ji-ho might debut SOLO!”

“Impossible. He’s trained for three months. THREE. If he debuts, what does that make me? I’ve been here four YEARS.”

“Ji-ho sunbae is honestly incredible though… I heard the songs he writes are on another level.”

Envy. Jealousy. Awe. Grudges quietly filed for later. The practice-room air carried all of it at once.
