Chapter Two Hundred Seventy-Three

Which of Us Is Greedier

◍ United States · California NAPA VALLEY 26–29°C · warm, dry, harvest month — the one week of the year this valley is worth stealing

September in Napa belongs to the grapes.

The fruit hung in bunches so heavy the trellis wires bowed under them, and the leaves had started turning — gold laid over rust, row after row, right to the foot of the hills. The sky above all of it had been wiped clean of cloud and left that blue that looks expensive: sapphire, hard and bottomless.

Photographers came for it. Tasters came for it. Somewhere in the distance a harvester was already running, and the air had the faint sweet rot of split skin in the sun.

This was the season the whole calendar was built around, and it was one of the reasons he had picked it.

Five taxis came into the forecourt of the hotel in single file and stopped in a row, and before the first door had opened the lobby staff were out.

“Welcome to the Tanzana. How may we help you?”

One of the LOEN crew stepped in front of them with the ease of a man who had decided that tipping was a strategic instrument.

“Luggage to our rooms, please — we’ll follow you.”

“Of course. Thank you. Anything at all tonight, ask for me.”

The handshake of small talk completed itself. The crew member came back and bent slightly at the waist.

“Hoejang-nim, it’s settled. We just follow him.”

Ji-ho nodded and went in through the revolving door.

The Tanzana announced itself the way old money does — quietly, and in every surface at once. A chandelier let fall something like ten thousand small lights. The upholstery took the hand back warmly when you leaned on it. Sculpture stood where a lobby would normally put a plant, and the dome above the courtyard had been installed, presumably, by a man who had resented open sky.

Expensive. Not subtle about it.

Tanzana Hotel · lobby, then the long corridor two girls become friends in four minutes; one of them learns something she did not want to know

Jiyeon and Soo-bin had already become close.

Girls do it at the speed of gossip: one comment about sunscreen, one about the fit of a blazer, one about how a flat white in Seoul has never once in history arrived correctly, and by the lift they were sisters.

Soo-bin laughed at the right places. She had her notebook against her chest and her face arranged in the gentle, listening expression she used on sets, and over Jiyeon’s shoulder — past the cap, past the noise, over everything — her eyes kept finding the back of a man walking two meters ahead.

Then came the front desk.

She had the key cards in her hand and was turning to go when Jiyeon slid her arm through Ji-ho’s and went with him toward the lift.

Same direction. Same floor. Same door.

Soo-bin stopped.

It was not a blow, exactly — a blow would have had the decency to be sharp. It was heavier than that, a thick impact somewhere behind the breastbone, and after it the air in the lobby simply stopped being enough.

Her face went the color of paper.

She stood there and watched the doors close on two people standing side by side, with Jiyeon’s head tilted up, saying something to him, smiling so hard her eyes went into crescents.

So that’s what it is.

The plastic edge of a key card dug into her palm. She did not feel it.

She had always believed herself clear-eyed. She had been in the business long enough to see how men like that were built and unbuilt; she had heard the stories, done the arithmetic, kept the correct distance from famous faces. She should have been immune.

And still she had fallen for the man who walked into a corridor of red paint and picked up the neighbor.

His face. His music. His name. His money.

The fault was hers: she had stacked so many filters on top of Song Ji-ho that she had stopped seeing him and started revering him. Under the filters she had forgotten the obvious — that a face like his was a scarce resource; that his standing was a height most people never touch in a lifetime; and that all of it, piled together, is the most addictive drug on the market and also the most dangerous.

A man like that was never going to be short of women.

She breathed in, turned, and walked to her own room.

The corridor went on and on. The carpet took her footsteps and gave nothing back, and the silence was so complete it felt like the entire hotel had decided to stop involving her in its affairs.

She walked mechanically, and the picture walked with her: Jiyeon’s arm on his sleeve, and that smile — natural, unbothered, entirely entitled.

That is the face of someone who has him.

And what was she?

What gave her the right to feel any of this?

The warmth in the office, the pulse in her wrists, the way she had lain awake at two in the morning re-enacting four seconds of his profile — all of it had been hers alone. His side of it had never existed. What possible claim did the private half of a thing have on the public half?

Chae Soo-bin · the first file she refused to close

Samhaksa-ro. A corridor drowned in red paint and a smell she could not name. Her legs not working. Footsteps coming down the stairs two at a time, and a man who stopped, looked at what had been done to her, and — without a word, without a single wasted motion — took off his jacket, put it over her, and lifted her onto his back as if she weighed less than the trouble.

Later, in the emergency room, still half out of her head, she remembered the sound of that jacket being pulled off a stranger’s shoulders and thought, very clearly: so this is what it is when someone decides you matter.

She had kept the receipt for everything since.

The door shut behind her and she slid down it until she was sitting on the floor.

She did not turn on the light.

Napa’s afternoon came through the curtain gap in a sliver and laid a bright, uneven diamond on the carpet, and she sat and watched it travel — down the wall, across the rug, off the edge of things — until the room was only gray.

Tomorrow she had a job to do.

Minutes of the meeting. Shooting order. Which documents to have on which clipboard at which hour so that a man would never have to ask for them. That was the assignment, and it was going to be done properly, because she had finally obtained something worth keeping.

And everything else —

Don’t think about it. It isn’t yours to think about.

She folded forward over her knees and let the hurt keep its volume all the way down, and when the wet came to the corner of one eye she did not wipe it, because there was nobody in the room to report it to.

I came here for him. That was the whole reason.

Why was I too late?

Inside the suite, the door had barely closed before Jiyeon launched herself at him.

“I’m so happy!” Her arms locked behind his neck and she kissed him in a rush, several times, out of order. “This place is stupid— look at it, it’s stupid!”

Flower-sweet perfume, the heat of a body still warm from travel, thin travel clothes and everything soft about her pressed against him, breathing.

He took her weight with one hand under her thigh and her back, and the skin under his palm was fine-grained and hot, and the sheer aliveness of her came up through his fingers into his arm.

They stayed in the entryway for an unreasonable amount of time.

When she finally came up for air she was flushed and slightly out of breath, with nothing in her eyes but him.

“Darling.” Fingers in his collar, swinging a little. “On the plane, Soobin-eonni and I looked it up. It’s Wine Month here. All month. Every winery’s doing cellar tours, harvest experiences, tasting flights, food-pairing dinners— can we go? Can we?”

The last syllable stretched out long and got sticky at the end, the way sugar does.

He looked down at her, said nothing, and pinched a little of the softness at her hip.

“Not today. There’s a schedule to lock before tomorrow.”

“Ahh— darling, please. It’s four in the afternoon. It’s criminal to work at four in the afternoon. Do it tomorrow.”

She squirmed, all soft curves and bargaining, and lifted her face — that small, expensive face, expectant, eyes like stones at the bottom of a clear stream, clean and hot at the same time.

Youth. Beauty. Appetite. Any three of them would undo a man; she was wearing all three at once and knew it.

Ji-ho admitted, privately, that he had been moved for the better part of one second.

Then he lifted his hand.

One swat.

“Ow! Ow!”

She spun around on his arm and glared, eyes bright with the beginnings of genuinely wounded tears, lower lip doing its best to hold.

“Good.” His face gave nothing. “I brought you here to work.”

“…Okay.” The pout survived one and a half seconds. She burrowed her face into the hollow of his neck, muffled, sulking on principle.

He kissed the top of her head.

“When the shoot’s finished,” he said, letting his voice come down, “we’ll take a whole day. Every cellar in the valley.”

Her head came up so fast there was a risk of whiplash.

“I knew it! You’re the best, you’ve always been the best—”

“Shower. Something comfortable. I’m running the meeting.”

Both her feet found the floor. Something moved behind her eyes — a light coming on in a room she had left ready for this exact purpose — and then she lifted the hem of her shirt, showed a strip of flat pale stomach, and turned a fraction to one side with her hip out — a pose borrowed from the cover of every magazine in the Seoul terminal.

“Darling,” she dropped her voice into a lazy, nasal something and looked at him through her lashes. “Want to wash my back?”

He looked at the innocent face on top of the shameless body, at the leg going all the way down the room, and felt whatever had been holding his professional resolve quietly disintegrate into nothing at all.

He cleared his throat.

“Well. Since you invited—”

“Weren’t you working?” She blinked, stuck out her tongue, and skipped. “Go be busy. Go be professional.”

She got inside the bathroom, and stuck her head back through the closing gap with the air of a girl entirely satisfied with herself.

“I’m not washing your back with you!”

The door came to. The lock turned.

Ji-ho stood alone in the middle of a hotel suite in California, looking at a closed door with his mouth half open, wearing the expression of a man who had been played — publicly, professionally, and by someone whose entire career depended on looking like she had not.

A moment later he laughed at himself, gave up, and picked the script back up.

Work. Work, then work.

Suite · the script desk “Stay With Me” · three minutes thirty seconds · the plan he has been carrying since Seoul

The song itself was simple and mean about it: C major, a vocal line that climbs from E4 to E6, a melody with a bruise under the polish, and words about the morning after a one-night stand — about a man who has decided that pride is expensive and would rather beg.

As a record it was a quiet, grown-up reckoning with what love costs. In Ji-ho’s hands it was going to be something else: two hundred and ten seconds of visual poetry.

He had chosen Napa, and the middle of harvest month, for one reason only.

Ninety seconds of everybody’s joy. A hundred and twenty of his.

The story was not complicated.

He turned the pages and shot the whole thing in his head, take after take: where the light came over the ridge, how the frame would hold, how long to stay on a face before the feeling in it turned into a feeling in the audience.

The bathroom door opened.

Jiyeon came out in an oversized robe with a towel in one hand and wet ends of hair going down one shoulder, and the terrycloth was not lying about the shape of her collarbone.

Fresh-washed skin went pink at the edges. She looked like a peach that had just been peeled — wet, tender, and radiating a smell of heat and flowers.

Ji-ho put the script face-down.

He pulled his T-shirt over his head in one motion, dropped it on the bed without aiming, and walked at her.

Her eyes went down to his chest and stayed there.

Shoulders wide enough that the eye had to travel. Muscle with the definition cut clean into it, and the waist above it narrowing down hard — the whole of it carrying a kind of male heat that arrived before he did.

She stood exactly where she was, swallowed once, and turned red at a speed that could be measured.

He stopped in front of her and looked down.

“Never seen one?”

“I— no!” A denial with no structural integrity whatsoever.

“Want to?”

The question came out low and patient, and the tips of her ears lit up first, and then her cheeks, and then her neck, and then the open inch above her collar, and she went the color of something boiled.

“Who wants to!” She got one step back, then another, and her eyes started looking for somewhere else to be. “Hmph. Go and be busy.”

He laughed — the whole face, unguarded, pleased in a way he did not usually permit himself.

The next second she was off the floor, over his shoulder, robe coming loose and one bare leg kicking at nothing.

“Ah— what are you doing! Put me down!”

Her hands beat at his back. A slap that did more for him than it did for her.

“You’re washing my back.”

“I said no!”

“Objection heard and discarded.” He went down the corridor with her hitting his shoulder blades. “And be thorough about it.”

The bathroom door shut again.

Steam got to the mirror first. Jiyeon stood in the far corner with no robe left anywhere in the argument, arms around herself, head down, a loofah clutched like a permit.

Under the shower, Ji-ho stood with his back to her.

Water ran down him in lines his spine had decided: shoulder blades lifting and setting, the groove going all the way down and disappearing below the waist, every muscle working under the skin and half-visible through the fog.

Her eyes went down. Her eyes came back up at speed. The tips of her ears were the reddest things in the room.

“Are you going to stand there all night?”

“Huh? — coming, coming!”

She attacked his back with the sponge in a manner that had more enthusiasm than method.

The corner of his mouth lifted.

He turned around.

Water came off him in sheets, over his chest, along the lines of his stomach, down into the V at his hips. He stood there in the steam, unhurried, and looked at her.

Jiyeon discovered that she had, for the first time in her career, nowhere at all to put her eyes.

A woman can be kissed for three years and still be undone by a man standing in water.

And the last step has not been taken yet — which is the only reason her hands are shaking.

“Look up.”

She shook her head, and hair flew off the ends of it onto his chest.

He took her chin between his fingers and lifted.

Lashes wet. Eyes shining. Her mouth red from where she had been biting it — pitiful, and ridiculous, and the best thing on the floor of that bathroom.

“Scared of what?”

“I’m not scared.” A whisper of a whisper.

His thumb went once across her lower lip, and the laugh that came out of him was low and unrepentant.

“Liar.”

Then he put his mouth on hers.

Soft at the beginning — lips against lips, wet, warm, patient. Then the small trembling in her stopped, and her chin came up, and she answered, badly and sweetly.

He let go eventually.

Jiyeon stood breathing hard, eyes bright and damp at the corners, and glared at him with the full force of a person who is not remotely frightening.

“Well? Are you washing my back or not?”

One look. The sponge went back to work, furiously.

“Pervert.”

“You invited me to shower.”

“I was teasing you!”

“Teasing me.” He turned it over like a phrase in a language he was learning. “Then I’ll go out now.”

He made one move for the door.

Her hand shot out and caught his wrist.

She looked at what she had done, turned the deepest red of the day so far, and — chin up, eyes everywhere but on him — said it.

“Finish— finish washing you first.”

He laughed, drew her in against him, and rested his chin on her wet hair.

“All right. No more teasing you. Wash. We have a meeting.”

Against his chest, a muffled, unrepentant: “Mm.”

Steam came up around them and time, briefly, lost its job.

A while later, quietly:

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm?”

“I like you so much that it’s embarrassing.”

He said nothing to that.

He answered it by putting his arms around her and holding her hard enough that neither of them could have mistaken it for anything else.

Everyone stood when he came in.

Numbers came up. Crew, insurance, permits, drone clearance, two vineyards with hard availability windows, and a list of pickers who could be paid to stand still and look happy.

Chae Soo-bin sat to one side with the notebook open, head down, writing.

Her face was in exactly the right state: attentive, warm, professionally pleasant, the small smile parked at the corner of her mouth like a tool put down.

Only occasionally — twice, maybe three times — her eyes came up off the page, crossed the two people on the sofa, and went back down before anyone could have caught her.

“Here.” She turned the tablet and slid it across.

The location scout had sent shots from three vineyards: rows, a barn, a crush pad, and one long avenue of vines running dead at the camera.

Ji-ho went through them one by one, thumb flicking, nodding twice.

“This one.” He stopped on a frame with the trellis receding into soft focus and hills stepping up behind it. “Depth. The rows pull the eye where I want it, and the ridge gives me a second frame inside the first.”

“Understood. A-Unit and B-Unit split as discussed—”

The briefing ran on. Times, moves, backup windows, the sequence of shots, whose truck went where, how to move forty people through a working harvest without being thrown out.

Soo-bin wrote all of it down.

She wrote it down better than she had ever written anything down, and the thought made something close to laughter move through her.

So this is what professionalism is. Your hands still work. Your face still works. The pen still keeps up.

Turns out a person can be broken and still take legible notes.

She had, without deciding to, become two women.

One was in her chest, arriving in waves, unable to sit still.

The other was in a chair in a hotel room in California, smiling, writing, not missing a single word — and no one in the room had the slightest idea that either of them existed.
