Chapter Seventy-Six

The Rebuke

At her mother’s words, Sulli’s whole body shuddered. She pulled out of Ji-ho’s arms and lifted her head — tear tracks still shining on her face, and in her eyes, a fire that had never been permitted to exist before.

“EOMMA! What are you SAYING?!” Her voice came out hoarse from crying and fury, and it landed like a dropped stone anyway. “Ji-ho oppa is my FRIEND! How can you insult him like that?!”

“Friend?”

The mother’s snort curled her brightly painted lips; her gaze dragged over Ji-ho with open disdain before swinging back to her daughter.

“A LOVER, more like. You besotted little fool! You come home and scream at your own mother, demanding money for a CAR — all for some man?”

“YOUR money? That money is what I EARNED — raising you, managing your career! Without me, would there even BE a you?!”

Sulli stared at her as if she’d just heard the funniest joke in the world, and the tears spilled again — but this time they carried something new underneath the hurt: years of pressurized anger, finally finding the crack.

“MANAGING? EARNED?”

“Your ‘managing’ meant treating me as a money tree — taking every won of my income and handing me back an allowance! Your ‘hard work’ meant spending my entire savings — and then borrowing HALF A YEAR of my future salary from the company without even TELLING me!”

She released Ji-ho’s arm and took one small step forward. Her thin shoulders were shaking — and straightening at the same time.

“I’ve been debuted for YEARS, eomma! I don’t even know how much is in my own account!”

“I wanted to buy one car. I came home to BEG you for it — and there’s nothing left! Nothing! And you’d already advanced six months of my income from the company!”

“I entered the company at twelve. Debuted at sixteen. I’m nineteen now — every won I ever earned went into your keeping. And now there’s nothing left, and I’m HALF A YEAR IN DEBT to my own company!”

“My life. My work. Is that all I am to you — a printing press?!”

The barrage choked the older woman for exactly one second. Then humiliation curdled into rage; her voice climbed an octave, hand pressed to her own chest in the classic posture of the injured party.

“YAH! Sulli! How DARE you speak to your mother like this?! I gave birth to you, I raised you, I MADE you a star — do you know what that cost me?!”

“Without me, would SM have taken you? Would you have debuted? Acted? And now your wings are grown, you’re earning money, and you want to kick your mother away? You ungrateful child! Your money IS mine to spend — I only kept it FOR you, so you wouldn’t waste it, so bad people wouldn’t cheat you!”

“Bad people?”

Sulli’s laugh came out broken. The tears kept falling. The eyes above them kept getting colder.

“Then WHO, exactly, has been bleeding me and controlling me all these years? Oppa SAVED me. He worried about me enough to chase me from Seoul to Busan! And you? You shipped me off to a dorm as a child and turned me into an ATM as an adult! When you’re not demanding money, you’re policing every word out of my mouth — I can’t even tell the truth about my favorite COLOR unless it matches yours!”

She drew one deep breath, as if it took everything she had, and said the next words with terrible, deliberate clarity:

“I — Sulli — am an adult. From today, my income is mine to manage. No one — INCLUDING you — touches what I earn without my consent. I will speak to the company myself: every advance, every transfer I never approved — ends now.”

The sentence landed on her mother like a lightning strike. The color dropped out of the carefully maintained face — then flooded back as blind fury. Financial control was the lever she ruled her daughter with, the engine of her entire lifestyle. This was a blade at the root of it.

“YOU WOULDN’T DARE!”

The shriek came with the whole face contorting, every pretense of poise abandoned. She crossed the room in three fast steps, arm rising, palm swinging hard for her daughter’s face—

“I’ll beat the ingratitude out of you, you white-eyed little wolf—”

“EOMMA!” The brother lunged — a step too slow.

Sulli watched the familiar hand come down with wind behind it, and her body simply locked — eyes squeezing shut on instinct, braced.

The pain never arrived.

A long-fingered, unmistakably strong hand had closed around her mother’s wrist in midair — and held it there, immovable.

Ji-ho stood between them. No one had seen him move.

His face carried no expression at all. Only the eyes had temperature — and it was well below freezing.

“Ajumma.” The word came out flat and cold. “What exactly do you think you’re doing?”

“You— LET GO of me! This is FAMILY business — no outsider gets to—”

She thrashed. The grip did not acknowledge the attempt. Then his wrist turned — one small, economical motion — and the high-heeled, apoplectic woman lost her balance entirely, staggering backward into the living-room sofa, her knee cracking against the corner of the coffee table hard enough to double her over.

He didn’t watch her land. He turned to the brother instead, voice easing several degrees.

“Is her bag packed?”

The brother nodded quickly and handed it over. Ji-ho took it, drew the still-trembling Sulli half into the shelter of his arm — and only then turned the cold gaze back to the woman moaning and cursing on the sofa.

“Family business? If ‘family’ means endless bleeding, control, guilt-collars — and now raised hands — then that kind of family deserves losing.”

His tone never rose. Every word landed like a whip anyway.

“You birthed her and sent her away — where’s the devotion in that? You raised her for the returns — where’s the grace? You made your daughter a trophy to show and a machine to withdraw from. You drained her youth and her sweat, and when she needed support you gave her demands and abuse. And you want the word ‘mother’?”

“SHUT YOUR MOUTH! What would YOU know?! Do you know how hard it was raising them ALONE?!”

“Hardship is not a license to harm your children — and it is NOT a fig leaf for greed.” His voice finally lifted, contempt riding openly on it now. “Your hardship? She has repaid it — with every won she ever earned, with her childhood, with her freedom. Repaid it with INTEREST. What more do you want? Her marrow?”

“Look at yourself right now. Does anything about you resemble a mother? All I see is a parasite feeding on her own daughter — down to the bone.”

“Sulli is an adult. She is a PERSON — not your appendage, not your property. She has the right to her own income, her own life, and the right to walk out of a house she can’t breathe in.”

“You— you—!”

The woman’s face cycled gray and scarlet; her pointing hand shook; no full sentence survived the trip out. Every hypocritical justification she owned had just been shredded and held up to the light.

“Let’s go.” He didn’t look at her again. The voice that reached Sulli had already changed keys entirely — gentle, low, hers.
