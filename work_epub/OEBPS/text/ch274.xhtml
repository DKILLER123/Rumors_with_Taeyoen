Chapter Two Hundred Seventy-Four

You Never Know What Best Friends Talk About

Day one · 05:00 Napa Valley · hotel forecourt, then the vineyard a first day in the director’s chair, and one secretary deciding not to look

The convoy was lined up and waiting before the sky had committed to anything.

Ji-ho came down with Jiyeon half a step behind him, and the crew was already standing by the cars in the number they had been assigned, which was the first thing that day to go right.

Chae Soo-bin was at the door with a tablet, and she met them at a pace that used up exactly the distance she had and no more.

“Breakfast is in the car, Hoejang-nim. Hair and makeup are in vehicle two — base work on the way, finishing details at the winery. Clear tomorrow. Sunrise at six twenty-three; we roll in at seven and the light holds past nine.”

The pace was even. The clauses were in order. There was nothing in her voice that had not been in it every day she had worked.

Ji-ho looked at her.

Beneath the foundation there was a thin bruise of sleeplessness under each eye. He could see it only because he was looking for something he could not name.

“Bad night?”

For a fifth of a second the report stalled in her hands.

“I slept. Different pillow, that’s all. Please don’t worry about me — I’m fine.”

He did not push it. He nodded, and got in.

Jiyeon followed, and at the door she turned and gave Soo-bin a small bright crinkle of the eyes.

“Soo-bin-ah, thank you. You’ve had it rough.”

“It’s the job.”

Doors. Two of them closing like one sentence. The convoy pulled out into fog that the headlights could not see past.

Soo-bin rode shotgun with the run sheet open across both hands, checking today against yesterday’s corrections. Outside, the dark was thinning the way water thins: at the edges first. The rows came up out of the mist one fence post at a time.

She breathed in, deliberately, through her nose, and put every watt of her attention into the screen.

No thinking.

Not about the things that aren’t mine to think about.

I came here to work. Work is the only thing I came here for.

Only.

Ji-ho stood under a trellis with his storyboard in a fist and did not breathe for a while.

So this is what it is, standing on the other side of the monitor.

He had prepared for this. He had, in fact, prepared for it in a way nobody else in the business managed to.

“Hoejang-nim.” The DP came up close and dropped his voice. “Twenty minutes of light.”

Ji-ho nodded. His throat had closed.

Every rule he owned was in there — thirds, lead room, the ninety-degree line, the axis, how to cover a scene so an editor never has to invent a cut — and now, with a set leaning on him, all of it had packed itself into a single hard knot that he could reach for and not grab.

“Harvest first,” he said. He cleared his throat and said it again like a person who meant it. “Harvest first. Wide. Camera on that side.”

The DP did not move.

“That side is the sun, sir.”

Backlight. He had pointed twenty people at the sun.

Right. Breathe. Move it.

The reset took eleven minutes. Eleven minutes he would remember for the rest of his life.

The same rows, four takes later everything correct, something wrong

In the frame, Jiyeon walked between the vines carrying a picking basket with morning on the side of her face, and she was, objectively, beautiful enough to stop traffic.

Ji-ho stared at the monitor and kept feeling a splinter he could not locate.

She’s too big. She’s pinned to the top and bottom of the shot like she’s been glued to it.

“Again.”

“Loosen it.”

“No — that’s not it. Closer.”

His hand went to his chin. The knot would not come loose.

Out on the rows, the extras had begun to exchange the small look of people being asked to be photographed while somebody figures it out. A grip checked his watch with his whole head.

The set was going stiff, one degree at a time.

Jiyeon walked over and crouched down at his shoulder, and looked at the playback with him as if it were a normal Monday.

“Hoejang-nim.” Careful, official, and kind, in that order. “Do you shoot photographs? The kind where there’s a person in them?”

He looked at her.

“With people you do one of two things,” she said, tapping the screen with one finger, still in character, still gentle. “Half-body, or a lot of nothing. You can’t do both. When she’s glued to the ceiling of the frame it feels like holding your breath — look, see? Nowhere to go.”

He looked. Saw.

The knowledge arrived a half-second behind the image and clicked into place like it had been waiting for a doorknob: give the eyeline somewhere to travel. Don’t fill the frame. Leave the audience air.

Knowing it and doing it are two different skills. Somebody should have told me they were two different skills.

“And — the rows.” She turned sideways, squinting at nothing. “Trellises, the horizon line. If the horizon’s tilted you keep waiting for the whole vineyard to fall over. It’s distracting.”

Ji-ho stared at her.

Under that look she folded a little, and touched her own cheek the way people do when they suspect they have said something enormous.

“That was nonsense. I’m saying nonsense. I’ve just been in a few hundred videos and you start to feel it, that’s all—”

“It wasn’t nonsense.” He stood up, and his voice had come back level. “Again. Whole take.”

This time he watched the monitor and did not open his mouth.

Jiyeon walked the rows. She took up a third of the frame; the rest of it went off beside her in the direction she was looking, wide and pale and unhurried. The horizon lay dead level. The trellis lines ran parallel out of the shot without buckling, without leaning, without promising to fall.

Quiet. Open. Right.

“Good.” He let out the breath he had been holding since the sun. “Printing that one.”

The script supervisor’s pencil came down in a checkmark. Somebody down the row exhaled, including — though nobody would have quoted him on it — the DP.

Ji-ho bent over the storyboard and, with a red pen, drew three circles in the margin.

Not helpless. Unpracticed. And unpracticed in front of a crowd turns into panic, and panic turns into forgetting that you know anything at all.

The morning stayed difficult. They got the harvest coverage anyway — messy, half of it unusable, all of it in the can by the time the light went flat and white.

At lunch he did not eat. He sat behind the monitor with the dailies queued and watched himself learn.

Jiyeon came over with a tray and put it down in his peripheral vision, where it remained, untouched and cooling.

“Aren’t you starving?”

“Eat. I’m on the stomping.”

The grape stomp had been the day’s big setup: pickers tipping crates into a half-hogshead, bare feet going to work, skins bursting, juice throwing itself an arm’s length in every direction and everybody in shot screaming with it.

Watching the playback, Jiyeon had gone still in the way she goes still when she wants something, and thirty minutes later she had submitted to the sanitizing wash, kicked off her shoes, and joined the line of stomping pickers for a take nobody asked for.

Her dress took the juice and turned violet from the thigh down.

She laughed like someone who had never once in her life had a publicist.

In the frame, his character notices her — that was the whole business of the shot — and it worked. It was lovely. It was also missing something he could not put his thumb on.

“Feeling,” he said, to the screen. “We shot the action. We didn’t shoot the feeling.”

Jiyeon sat down next to him, ate a bite, ate another, watched him watch it.

“That one’s hard,” she said. “On my videos the director used to shout about subtraction — you can’t have everything in there. Either the frame is doing the feeling, or the face is. Pick one or you get postcard.”

He turned his head.

She chewed, looking back at him with absolute innocence.

“Did I talk too much again?”

“No.” He rewound the take. “You’re right. I wanted the whole thing in one frame, so I got nothing in it.”

15:20 Balloon launch field one lift-off, one take of it, and nothing about it forgiving

An ascent is a one-time event. Light, wind, the shape of the climb, and the way a camera moves inside a basket that is not yet moving — four variables that only get to meet once.

Ji-ho stood at the launch line and watched a balloon the size of a house remember its shape from the ground up, and his palms had gone wet inside his gloves.

Jiyeon, touched up, stood beside him. Wind kept taking her hair across her face and she kept not caring.

“Darling.” She said it quietly, with the crew close enough that she shouldn’t have, and she said it anyway. “Stop carrying all of it. Decide what you want in the shot — and then decide what you don’t.”

What he didn’t want.

He looked at the balloon. Then he laughed at himself, out loud, in front of forty people, and did not care either.

Subtraction. Not addition. A rookie tries to put everything in; a professional earns the right to throw most of it away.

“A-camera goes up with them, wide, the whole valley.” He turned, pointing as he spoke, and the sentences began arriving faster than he could assemble them, which was, he noticed dimly, new. “B-camera stays ground, tight — her face only, nothing else in the frame. C-camera—”

He stopped.

“Kill C. It’s crowded and it won’t read.”

The DP started walking people into place with the speed of a man relieved.

Ji-ho went and stood behind Jiyeon, in the basket, in the center of the frame he had just asked for.

“Rolling. Action.”

A music video does not demand acting. It demands a beautiful two minutes and a face willing to be looked at, and on that count both of them were dangerous.

The burner roared, the ground let go, and the DP at the monitor did not blink once.

In the frame: the valley opening under them like something being unfolded for a child. Jiyeon looking down at the vines with her whole face lit up by it. Ji-ho looking at her, which was the shot’s entire argument. Between them a wide, deliberate emptiness — the space an eye travels through, now spent on two people in a script who have only just met.

The horizon level. The violet dress against the gold of the envelope, one warm key, nothing in the palette fighting with anything else.

“That’s it. That’s the shot.” The DP punched the air once. “Hoejang-nim, the progress since breakfast is not— I don’t have a word for it.”

They came down. They walked to the monitor. They watched it together, and Jiyeon clapped both hands over her mouth.

“Daebak — it’s gorgeous! Oh, wait. How?” Her head came around to look at him with open worship in it. “This morning you were— and now it’s like this. How?”

From a rookie freezing at the first setup to running the floor in nine hours.

So this is the gap. Not talent. The mile between reading about water and getting in.

“Pay attention,” he said, with the flat dignity of a man who knows exactly how good it sounds, and walked off before she could answer.

Behind him, three departments watched him go with an expression that, translated, said the same thing in three accents.

Soo-bin had the tablet, and the tablet did not need her at that moment, and she stood a few feet away watching a room fall for him — and the ache in her chest came up so fast she had to spend it on a pen.

September 6 · evening V. Sattui · the harvest table a thousand feet of white cloth, fifty kitchens, and the noisiest night of the valley’s year

Napa’s one real night out ran from the winery gate into the field in a straight white line, a thousand feet of table, long enough that the far end was a rumor.

Kitchens from all over the valley had taken sections of it — this fifty feet rib, that fifty feet paella — and the smoke and the wine smell got into the crowd and stayed there. By the time they stepped out of the car the sky was still holding a last stripe of blue over the hills and the bonfire was already going, and the music shook the glasses on the tables.

“Waaaah—” Jiyeon grabbed his sleeve and stood on her toes to see past the crowd. “There are people.”

“Want to go?”

The nod she gave him could have been used to start an engine.

Ji-ho looked back at the camera van, where second unit was already loading down.

“Stay on us. If you lose me I will find you, and I will know things about you.”

“Rolling, Hoejang-nim. Set and rolling.”

That was not, in fairness, how a scripted shoot day went.

There were three thousand people holding plastic cups, and not one of them standing on a mark. Blocking, marks, the geometry of two lonely strangers — none of it survived the first twenty meters.

So he took the storyboard out of his own hands and put it into the AD’s.

“You’re the eyes,” he said. “Take what happens. I’m with her.”

Jiyeon’s face did something with the words take what happens and then lit up entirely.

“If it’s for the camera,” she announced, “I have no manners tonight.”

She dragged him into the crowd by one hand.

The first lap of the table was food.

Ribs glazed and sticky, paella in a pan the size of a wagon wheel, oysters on ice still cold enough to steam, duck liver on toast with salt on it that caught the light. She took one bite of each of them, and delivered the remainder to him with the assurance of a woman who had never once been told no at a buffet.

“This is the good one. Try it.”

“This is too salty. You finish it.”

“Oh — this wine is sweet! It’s like a mistake in the best way!”

He was towed the entire length of that white river from one end to the other with a glass that was always half full, always something she had decided about.

“You’re doing that on purpose.”

“Doing what on purpose?”

“Finishing my food before I’ve started it.”

She leaned in and kissed his cheek, satisfied, and put her mouth near his ear to be uncatchable about it.

“In Korea we say: if you share it, the luck comes.”

Nana said the same thing over the counter at the Koreatown market. Share it, bok comes to you. She raised two countries on that one sentence.

Dark came all the way down, and the bonfire got fed until it was too bright to look at, and somebody started a line of strangers linking arms, and the row of them swung around it like a rope being turned.

Jiyeon put him in it.

Her dancing was, technically, excellent: hips and shoulders and footwork that any of the three thousand would have paid to see on a stage. She used none of it. She did whatever the drum told her, hair coming loose across her face, laughing so hard she had to hold her ribs.

Ji-ho stood on the other side of her and watched.

Firelight went over her and the flush under it, and somebody near him whistled on pure instinct, and three people clapped at nothing in particular.

She didn’t notice any of them. She laughed until the laugh ran out, and then she fell into him.

“Tired.”

“Then sit.”

“Not sitting.” She came up on her toes, face on fire, a fine shine of sweat across the tip of her nose. “More.”

The music changed without permission and went slow.

The fire popped. Sparks went up in ones and twos and lost themselves in the stars, which was the sort of thing that only happens in valleys.

Her arms came around his neck. Her face tilted up.

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm.”

“Today was the best day.”

The fire jumped in her eyes and went out and came back.

He kissed her. Around them the crowd, which had been waiting for something to happen, approved of it loudly and kindly, and a woman shouted something in English that the AD’s camera caught every frame of.

Jiyeon did not care about any of the three thousand. She got up on her toes and held on harder.

When they came apart she was breathing badly, her forehead pressed under his chin.

“Home?”

“Do you want to go?”

She shook her head. Then, having thought about it, nodded. Then shook it again with more commitment.

He laughed, took her hand, and steered them both toward the gate.

She sat back against the door, entirely pleased with herself, and pulled his sleeve once so he would follow, and did not look back at the driver at any point and never, once, thought about it.

“Ji-yeon-ah.”

“Mm.”

She answered it by getting up on her toes and putting her mouth on his hard enough to move them both.

The same room, later for days they had shared a bed with a call time between them; tonight the call sheet had nothing on it

His hand came to her waist and found the truth about a life in practice rooms under the silk — thin, and not soft, with muscle lying flat and quick to answer when it was touched.

She went at him like somebody collecting a debt, everything at once, and he picked her up, and she made a small sound at being lifted and laughed at herself for making it, and then the lamp was out of the argument entirely.

The sheets were hotel sheets. The night was not.

What the script needed was two people who have decided to stop pretending they met by accident. What the room had was a slow season of almost, arriving, all at once, at somebody who was entirely willing and entirely there.

Afterwards the fire in the field had burned down without either of them noticing. Somewhere out past the rows an owl did its one job. The curtain made a gray rectangle of them both.

Her hand was still on his back, moving without purpose, and found a sheen of sweat and went back to where it had come from.

She drew herself closer, all the way, until there was no room left to describe.

A long while later she spoke, in the voice of a person filing a report she had no idea she owed anybody:

“Song Ji-ho.”

“Mm.”

“Oh. So that’s what it is.”

He looked down. Her eyes were shut, her lashes damp, and her mouth was doing something that in anyone else would have been called victory.

“What is?”

Her eyes opened, red-rimmed and shining, and she thought about it with the seriousness of an artist given a deadline.

“It’s—” A pause. “Good. It’s good. I take back— never mind.”

“Dare you.”

“…Mm?”

“She said two days ago that she could get up the next morning and do a whole schedule. I don’t believe she could have—” The sentence fell off the end of her.

Ji-ho lay there in the gray light with his brain completely, audibly offline.

Then he laughed so hard the bed took it badly.

“You three discuss this?”

She was already pulling the coverlet up over the top of her head.

“What is there that best friends can’t talk about?”

“Then what else did you talk about?”

“Want to know?”

He nodded. He was, to be clear about the man, genuinely eager.

“Hee hee. Never.”

Park Ji-yeon found a position against his ribs, adjusted twice, and settled into it with the finality of somebody closing a case.

Ji-yeon-ah. You’re mine, finally.
