Chapter Four

Provisional Trainee

Five judges sat behind the long table: vocal coach Kim Mi-yeon, dance coach Jung Woo-jin, rap coach Choi Woo-chul, and two staffers from the production center, Jung Jae-ho and Jung Hye-won.

Jung Woo-jin leafed through the boy’s file.

“Ji-yong-ssi, your form says dance is your strength. Show us something?”

“Ne! Of course.”

The boy was visibly nervous. His prepared piece was “Sorry, Sorry” — the Super Junior single that had set all of Asia on fire in 2009. Its signature hand-rubbing move was simple, instantly memorable, magnetic, locked tight to the beat; it had spawned imitation dances across half the globe and become one of the group’s defining moments.

The music kicked in. Ji-yong began to move.

The judges watched closely — Jung Woo-jin most of all, arms crossed, eyes narrowing by the bar. When the music cut, his brow had already knotted.

“Ji-yong-ssi. There’s no power in any of it. ‘Sorry, Sorry’ is American funk and contemporary R&B over a hard electronic beat — how do you dance that like overcooked noodles?”

“And your timing — you rushed four counts. Four. In the piece you prepared specifically for this audition. Did you actually take this seriously?”

“I— I’m sorry.” The blood drained out of the boy’s face, and he bent into a hasty bow.

“That’s enough. Go home and wait for our call.”

“Next.”

Wait for our call. In this industry, that sentence meant the call was never coming. The disappointment around the table was almost audible.

Ji-ho walked to center floor with an easy smile — unhurried, open. Years on the Peninsula had trained the bow into his body; the one he offered now was textbook. Then he straightened and began, in Korean polished to a mirror shine:

“Good afternoon, teachers. My name is Ji-ho — Korean-American, born and raised in Los Angeles. I’m eighteen, and my strongest skill is singing.”

He followed it with a brief self-introduction in English — relaxed, fluent, unmistakably native. Language skill mattered at these auditions; a candidate who could move between tongues cleanly was an instant mark in the plus column.

As he spoke, his gaze traveled slowly left to right across the five judges, resting a beat or two on each face before returning to center — omitting no one, pressing no one, radiating a calm and total sincerity. It was a habit stamped into him by years of working rooms full of strangers: every guest gets looked after. Every single one.

Before he’d sung a note, the panel had already formed three impressions.

One — the face. Handsome in exactly the clean, camera-ready way SM’s visual standard demanded; you could park him at the front of a group and call it a day.

Two — the manner. Courteous, composed, effortlessly poised.

Three — the languages. A gyopo kid whose English was pure Los Angeles and whose Korean carried not even a shadow of an accent. Involuntarily, all five judges thought of the same names: the Jung sisters — Jessica and Krystal — and Tiffany, the American-raised trio whose bilingual ease was company legend.

Jung Hye-won leaned forward, openly intrigued.

“Ji-ho-ssi, you grew up in the States, but your Korean is flawless — truly, there’s nothing to catch. How?”

Ji-ho let his eyes settle on her as she spoke — on the point just between her brows, the spot that reads as attentive without the pressure of a stare. I’m listening to you, and answering you.

“My grandmother only ever spoke Korean to me — she used to say a Song should never lose his own language. After she passed, keeping it felt like keeping her. And since music is what I love, I never stopped studying — the language, the songs, all of it.”

“Effort pays its debts, teachers. Results never betray the people who work for them.”

As he answered, his gaze moved naturally from judge to judge, two or three seconds each, arriving back at Jung Hye-won precisely as he finished — the questioner honored, no one neglected. It was the kind of craft you couldn’t teach in a classroom; the world had beaten it into him across a decade of hard living, and it worked in any interview, anywhere, every time.

Unshakable, articulate, warm. The judges found themselves nodding along, thoroughly disarmed. This kid is genuinely something. Except — the age. The age is a problem.

Which left the only question that mattered: could he actually sing?

Kim Mi-yeon smiled at him, gentle as a music box.

“Ji-ho-ssi. Singing is your weapon, you said. Show us.”

Trainee vocal tests ran a cappella, always — no track to hide behind, nothing between the judges and your pitch, tone, breath, and heart.

“Yes. I’ve prepared ‘Bleeding Love,’ by Leona Lewis.”

His song. The one that fit his voice like a key fits its lock.

He cleared his throat once, softly — and began.

Bleeding Love · Leona Lewis · 2007

Closed off from love,

I didn’t need the pain.

Once or twice was enough,

And it was all in vain.

Time starts to pass…

His voice had always been the gift. Now, backed by 74 points of vocal craft, the first phrase alone tilted the room.

The years poured into the melody as if they’d been written into it — every empty apartment, every last train, every stranger’s city. The timbre ran warm and dense, textured like heated spring water, sliding slow and settling deep, straight into the chest.

The five judges traded a single glance, and one identical thought passed between them: did we just strike gold?

The last time anyone in this room had felt this particular jolt, it came from a girl who had beaten out nine thousand others — the one now leading Girls’ Generation. Kim Taeyeon.

Bleeding Love · Leona Lewis · 2007

I keep bleeding, keep, keep bleeding love,

I keep bleeding, I keep, keep bleeding love,

Keep bleeding, keep, keep bleeding love…

Kim Mi-yeon was quietly elated. His support ran steady and long; the tails of his phrases decayed clean, no wobble, no clipped edges. Diction soft but precise. And the feeling — the feeling was surgical: a perfectly judged rasp shading the regret, a sudden brightness lifting the sweetness. A storyteller’s voice.

The final note faded, and the panel broke into applause.

One thought, and only one, occupied Kim Mi-yeon’s mind: this one does not walk out of this building.

Contracts didn’t happen at this stage — today tested only a candidate’s strongest weapon. Survive it, train in-house, pass the full formal evaluation, and only then came the trainee contract.

She rose from her seat.

“Ji-ho-ssi — have you had professional vocal training?”

“No, never formally. But I love singing more than anything, so I’ve practiced on my own — every day, for years.”

“Incredible. That was truly outstanding.”

“Thank you.” He bowed, unhurried and exact.

She sat, conferred with the other four in an undertone, then looked up at him with a smile that answered the question before her words did.

“Congratulations. As of today, you’re a provisional trainee of SM Entertainment.”

“You’ll train here at the company while we evaluate your progress, and we’ll schedule your formal assessment. Pass that — and you sign with SM as an official trainee.”
