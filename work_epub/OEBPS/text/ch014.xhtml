Chapter Fourteen

That Outrageous Trainee Contract

The composure on Taeyeon’s face lasted exactly one phrase. Her eyes widened by degrees; her lips parted without her permission.

By the system’s own ledger, Girls’ Generation had already completed their climb to the summit — and as the main vocal of the strongest girl group alive, Taeyeon’s vocal rating stood at 79. Four points above the boy currently standing at center floor. Four.

“Yesterday Once More” needed no introduction — an evergreen since the day it was pressed, warm melody wound around homesick lyrics, the kind of ballad that outlives the radios that play it.

Yesterday Once More · Carpenters · 1973

Every sha-la-la-la, every wo-o-wo-o still shines,

Every shing-a-ling-a-ling that they’re starting to sing’s so fine…

Eyes half-closed, fingertips ticking the beat against the air, wholly submerged — his English rang out native and unforced, every turn and ornament placed with a jeweler’s touch. Watching him sway gently through the bridge, Taeyeon finally understood why the company had convened a special evaluation for a complete unknown.

Yesterday Once More · Carpenters · 1973

Every shing-a-ling-a-ling that they’re starting to sing’s so fine…

The last note set down soft as a closing book. He steadied the tail of it, let the silence breathe once, and bowed to the panel.

“Thank you.”

For a moment the room simply forgot to react. Then scattered applause — swelling fast into the real thing.

Taeyeon surfaced from the song like a swimmer coming up for air. In the boy at center floor she was seeing, with uncomfortable clarity, a ghost: herself, years ago, walking into this company and silencing a room with one opening line — carrying that same raw, undiluted hunger for the stage.

“Ji-ho-ssi.” The admiration in her voice refused to be hidden. “The texture of your voice is exceptional. That full chest resonance gives everything a rich, grounded floor, and your breath support carries every phrase — long, steady, effortless.”

She paused, choosing her words with a specialist’s care.

“Technically — and I’m speaking at my professional standard — I can find almost nothing to correct. Your phrase endings especially: closing on a controlled soft mix, not a tremor in the airflow, nothing abrupt. Honestly? Near perfect.”

“But what’s rarer is this — your diction serves the emotion. Completely.” She was leaning forward now without realizing it, words gathering speed. “Between clean breaths, you’re doing this fine-grained emotional work purely through color — that rasp when the line confides, that sudden brightness when it turns joyful. That’s not technique. That’s storytelling.”

Anyone who knew Taeyeon professionally would have been staring by now. At work she was rigor itself — praise this unguarded, at an evaluation, from her, was very nearly unprecedented.

A humble bow.

“Thank you for your guidance, Taeyeon sunbae-nim.”

Without a flicker of hesitation, she marked the vocal column: a perfect 10. Beside her, the recruitment manager watched the pen move, looked thoughtful, and quietly amended a few figures of his own.

Evaluation concluded, Taeyeon rose and gathered her things at once — Girls’ Generation’s schedule was wall-to-wall these days, and carving out even this single hour had taken doing. But passing Ji-ho on her way out, she stopped, raised two small fists, and pumped them once in the universal gesture.

“Hwaiting!”

This close, he could see the fine soft down along her cheek, features small and exact as a hand-finished porcelain doll’s. Her lips, glossed to a crystal shine, caught the practice-room lights with a delicate wet gleam. And as she turned, the small breeze of the motion carried over a scent — grapefruit, clean and cold and bright.

Ji-ho breathed it in without moving a muscle. Scent tells on people. Citrus-lovers, in his long field experience, kept a vein of mischief running under whatever face they showed the world — allergic to heavy air, built for play. So beneath all that top-tier polish, the 2012-model Taeyeon was still hiding a girl’s quicksilver after all.

The judges filed out until only Ryu Seok-min remained, squaring his scoring sheets, the satisfaction on his face doing no work at all to hide itself.

“Ji-ho-ssi. With me, please.”

“Ne.”

Following him toward the office, Ji-ho already had a fair guess what was coming.

“Sit.” A gesture at the sofa; then, from a cabinet behind the desk, two copies of a document, laid squarely in front of him. “The trainee contract. Read it carefully before anything else.”

“Thank you.”

He took it with both hands and began to read — genuinely read. SM’s trainee contracts were territory he had studied like an examination subject, twice over in two lives. They came in two flavors: the standard full-time training agreement, which made you a de facto SM employee — and the looser “phantom contract” for students and low-priority signings. What sat in front of him was, unmistakably, the former. And the clauses matched his memory almost line for line.

SM Entertainment · Trainee Agreement — Key Terms
Article · Term

Base contract period calculated as seven (7) years from debut. From the day you debut, the next seven years of your career belong to the company.

Article · Training Costs

All training expenses incurred during the trainee period — vocal, dance, and language instruction; body management; nutritional provision — are itemized monthly and deducted from post-debut income in installments.

Article · Management & Conduct

Unconditional compliance with company arrangements in training and daily life. Exclusive management rights vest solely in the company.

Article · Breach

Breach of contract incurs liquidated damages of three (3) times total training costs.

In sum: absolute control, priced in years and multiples.

Translated into plain arithmetic: the first two years after debut went to repaying the company’s investment, and with revenue splits this thin, the remaining five didn’t put much in an artist’s pocket either. Even Girls’ Generation — at this very moment the biggest girl group on the planet — wouldn’t begin building real personal wealth until the 2014 renegotiation, save for the two names popular enough to bargain early: Taeyeon and Im Yoon-a. Only after that second contract would fans get to joke about “the rich-lady generation.” It was also, not coincidentally, why so many of the Chinese artists who debuted under SM eventually terminated their contracts — some across a courtroom table.

Im Yoon-a
Center · Visual · Actress
Girls’ Generation · Yoon-a
Born: May 30, 1990

The nation’s beloved center: graceful before a camera, mischievous among friends, and far more perceptive than her gentle image implies.

“Questions?” Ryu Seok-min had been watching him read — actually read — with growing interest.

Ji-ho looked up, level and calm.

“The terms are clear. One thing I’d like to confirm — the exact accounting method for training costs.”

Something like approval flickered behind the black frames. Most trainees receiving this document either vibrated with too much joy to read it, or went pale at the clauses. Cold attention to detail was… rare.

“Training costs cover lesson fees — vocal, dance, language — plus body management, meal plans, and related expenses. The company provides an itemized statement monthly, and deductions come out of your income after debut, in installments.”

A nod; no surprise there. A moment’s pause, then the second question, delivered just as evenly:

“Understood. Then — the post-debut revenue split. Is there any room for adjustment?”

Ryu Seok-min’s eyebrow climbed. Direct, this one. He leaned back, fingers lacing on the desk.

“The split is standardized company-wide. We don’t adjust it for individual trainees. Although…”
