Chapter Ninety

The Ninety-Degree Bow

Ji-ho tracked the footsteps by ear — and released her at the last possible second.

“You—!”

Cheeks flaming, a fine sheen of sweat on the bridge of her nose, Taeyeon shot him one furious glare, snatched up her bag, and bolted into the recording booth, where she scrabbled through it for lipstick and a compact with shaking hands.

The mirror reported the damage: eyes still warm and unfocused, fair cheeks glowing pink straight through, lipstick thoroughly, criminally ruined.

“Bad boy!”

She hissed it at her own reflection and began repairs.

Outside, Ji-ho calmly wiped the color from the corner of his mouth.

The door opened.

He greeted them like a man with the world’s clearest conscience.

“Seohyun nuna. Good evening.”

“Good evening.”

Tiffany scanned the room.

“Where’s DaeDae?”

The booth door banged open and Taeyeon stormed out in a magnificent huff.

“Yah, Ji-ho! What kind of song did you write?! It’s IMPOSSIBLE to sing!”

“Pfft—” He strangled the laugh mid-birth. “Ahem. Ahem.”

“DaeDae, were you just trying ‘I Got a Boy’?”

“Mm.” Taeyeon’s hands sketched enormous, outraged shapes in the air. “Impossibly hard. Criminally hard.”

“‘I Got a Boy’… ‘I’ve fallen for a boy’? What is this about?” Seohyun’s face was pure curiosity.

Fallen for a boy. Taeyeon’s face flashed hot all over again.

“It’s LATE. Let’s go. Home. Now.”

“Wait.” Tiffany caught her arm, and turned to Ji-ho. “Are you heading back? Seohyun drove today — we can drop you.”

His usual route home was a long detour by bus. A car was a car.

“Sure — then I’ll trouble you, nunas.”

Tiffany linked arms with Taeyeon and swept her around.

“Then let’s go~”

Seohyun’s lips pushed out into a small, dignified pout. Am I invisible? Nobody answered my question! And now I’ve been assigned chauffeur duty on top of it! Do maknaes have no human rights whatsoever?

“Seohyun-ah, what are you standing there for?”

She deployed the obligatory sweet smile.

“Coming, eonni.”

“We’ll split here — we’ve got to get the car. Ji-ho, wait for us at the main entrance.”

“Ne, nuna.”

He parted from the three of them and walked alone to the front of the building.

And ran into the last person he expected.

Ryu Tae-jun.

“Well — if it isn’t Tae-jun sunbae. What brings you here at this hour?”

“Running… an errand. Dropping off… materials. For a sunbae.” Ryu Tae-jun had not planned on meeting Ji-ho tonight; his eyes kept sliding away to the pavement.

“Dropping off materials.” Ji-ho checked the time — 11:11 PM. “At this hour. Huh? Errands for a sunbae.”

“Twenty minutes each way. Hard work, truly.”

The mockery in it was light, and it landed anyway. Ryu Tae-jun’s fists curled.

Once, he had used a sunbae’s rank as a boot on Ji-ho’s neck.

Now, in front of his sunbaes, he had become the errand boy who fetched and carried at eleven at night. The reversal sat in his gut like swallowed glass.

And under the humiliation: fear. He was a B-rank trainee. Ji-ho stood inside the pre-debut team — with rumors of an imminent debut trailing him like a spotlight.

Does he hold grudges? Will he come for me?

Ji-ho had no such spare time. Revenge on the Ryu Tae-juns of the world wasn’t worth the calendar space.

But men like Ryu Tae-jun could only read others through the smeared lens of themselves. He had been stepped on by his sunbaes, so he had clawed the dignity back out of his hoobaes. By that arithmetic, what would a genius who’d trained three months and was already debuting do with an old enemy?

He wavered. Ground his teeth. Then bent — sharply, fully, a proper ninety degrees.

“Ji-ho-ssi. I’m sorry.”

Ji-ho blinked, mildly.

“Sunbae. What’s this?”

The bow held, textbook-rigid.

“Back then — using seniority against you, singling you out. I’m truly sorry.”

Ji-ho regarded the deeply folded spine before him, face registering very little. Contempt going in, courtesy coming out. Almost funny.

Time seemed to congeal around the bow.

Ji-ho said nothing. He tilted his head a few degrees — examining, or remembering.

“Heh.”

The bowed body flinched, near-invisibly, and the head sank lower.

Ji-ho shook his head.

“Tae-jun sunbae, a bow this heavy — I’m not sure I can accept it.”

“After all, when I first arrived, you taught me the rule yourself: in this industry, a rookie’s back stays bent until the sunbae is satisfied.”

His own words, returned to him verbatim — each one landing like a slap with no sound.

Ryu Tae-jun held the bow. His face burned crimson; shame and fear braided down his neck until the muscles screamed.

Ji-ho took one small step forward, stopping beside him, not looking at him. His voice stayed level.

“But as it happens, I’m the type who looks forward.”

“What’s past can stay past.”

The bent shoulders loosened a fraction — the pardon almost in hand.

Then the next words dropped him through the ice.

“Although — Tae-jun sunbae, one thing does make me curious. Back when you made me bow: what were you hoping to get? A display of authority? Or was it just that you couldn’t stand how close I was with Irene sunbae?”

A pause, as if he were genuinely weighing it.

“And now — this bow. What’s it for? Buying yourself a quiet conscience? Or insurance, in case I decide to use what I’ve become… on you?”

No raised voice. No heat. Just a scalpel, opening the whole shabby mechanism to the air.

Ryu Tae-jun’s throat produced a dry clicking sound and nothing that resembled language.

“Stand up, sunbae.” A faint, tired distaste finally surfaced in Ji-ho’s voice. “Doesn’t your back ache, bent all this time?”

Ryu Tae-jun straightened as if granted amnesty — face cycling red and white, eyes skittering anywhere but forward, forehead beaded with fine sweat.

Ji-ho looked at him directly now. Calm as still water.

“I don’t need your apology. An apology changes nothing that happened. And I’m not going to do anything to you — because there’d be no point.”

He watched the flinching uncertainty in the man’s eyes, and continued.

“One suggestion, that’s all. Take every unit of energy you once spent on me, and spend it in the practice room. B-rank is a very long way from a debut.”

“As for me—” the corner of his mouth curved, “—I have a great deal to do, and very little time.”

“Train hard, Ryu Tae-jun. Maybe someday we’ll meet again — on a stage.”

Beep-beep—

Seohyun’s car pulled up to the curb.

Ji-ho didn’t spare the frozen figure another glance. He walked straight to the car, hand lifting in a wave.

“Nuna — coming!”

Three seniors of Girls’ Generation waited inside: Taeyeon, Tiffany, Seo Ju-hyun.

Ryu Tae-jun stood rooted where the conversation had left him, watching that straight back recede, and understood with total clarity how ridiculous he was.

Ji-ho hadn’t touched him. Hadn’t even insulted him. And it hurt worse than any beating.

Train hard. Not advice — a verdict:

The person you schemed to crush has already risen to an altitude you can only crane your neck at. Your fear, your calculations, the seniority you propped your whole self-respect on — from up there, it’s all dust.

The people in that car. The future in front of him. None of it will ever share a sentence with yours again.

“Heh. Hehehe. Hahahaha.”

The laugh came out of him wrong, cracked down the middle.

“Ryu Tae-jun-ah, Ryu Tae-jun… you really are the joke.”
