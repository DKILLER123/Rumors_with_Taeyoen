Chae Soo-bin did not say anything for a long way yet.

That was the trouble: she knew he had told her the truth. She had been in this car with all of it happening in the seat behind her, and she had seen the faces, and there was no version of the morning in which any of them looked deceived.

They all know. And not one of them has said a word, or made a scene, or handed in anything.

What is that?

She was the sort of person who had been brought up on a set of rules about this, laid out flat and in order, the way a mother lays out a uniform: a feeling between two people is one-to-one, it is loyal, it does not have room in it for a third. Two people love each other and there is nobody else, and the door is shut, and a person who leaves the door open is not a person, she was told, that a decent woman needs to explain herself to.

Everything she had been taught, and everything in this car, did not fit in the same room.

“But it isn’t right,” she said, quietly, to the windscreen.

He did not argue with her. He laughed once, the smallest amount.

“Right and wrong,” he said. “Who is defining them?”

Chae Soo-bin opened her mouth and discovered that there was nothing standing behind it.

“Soo-bin-ah.” His voice was kind, which was somehow worse than the alternative: the tone of a man explaining a thing to somebody he expects to understand it eventually.

“The world is more complicated than the version of it you have been given. When people have money and power, men and women alike, they stop lying about what they want. I am only a little more honest about mine than most men are about theirs.”

He turned his head toward the window and let the last of it land with no weight on it at all.

“You’ll understand later.”

Her hands went tighter on the wheel, both of them, at the same moment.

She did not want to understand.

She had the unpleasant, quiet suspicion that she had begun to.

The LOEN building took the morning at nine minutes to eleven, and the car came up its own drive, and the driver did the thing she always does, which is to open the rear door and stand back.

Song Ji-ho got out. Before he let the door go he turned back on the seat, because he had thought of something.

“By the way.”

Chae Soo-bin looked up at him from the other side of the door.

His eyes were not on her face. They were on the bottom of her ear, where a pair of small studs sat, catching the morning and throwing back a light broken into a great many pieces.

“The earrings I sent you,” he said, and the corner of his mouth went, and there was something in his eyes entirely at peace with itself. “Do you still like them?”

Something in her chest gave one hard pull.

Her hand came up before she told it to and her fingertip found the stud on her lobe: the small cool weight of it, and the smoothness, and a finish so fine that skin does not know it is metal.

Did she like them?

She had liked them the first time she saw them, which was in a car, in the rain, in a box she had been told to look at for two minutes and had then put back down out of her own reach.

She had liked them enough to wear them every day, and to be unable to take them off, and to have stopped counting the mornings she had gone past a mirror and stopped and gone back for a second look at the small broken light on her own ear.

Except.

The gift had not been only a gift to her.

She had been the person who compiled the record of them — a sheet in a spreadsheet, because she keeps everything in a spreadsheet, listing everything he has sent to anybody, with dates. Apart from the cases of wine from Napa, which were the same for everyone out of a simple economy of ordering, not one present on that list repeats another: different, one from the next, in every single line, and considered. Bags from houses with a long wait. Jewellery at a carat that a person does not buy for a colleague. Limited dresses, the sort with a number on the label.

To her he had sent a pair of earrings.

Chae Soo-bin opened her mouth with a sentence in it and could not find the sentence.

Outside, above the building and above the whole of the city’s Monday, the moon had gone and the sun had not, and it does not matter, because the thing she was thinking about is not a fact about the sky.

A bright moon hangs high, and shines on everybody underneath it.

What is hateful about a moon that high is that it will not shine on one person only.

He did not wait for the answer, and got out of the doorway, and went in at a pace with something light in it, and the revolving door turned twice and the long back of him was gone behind the glass.

Chae Soo-bin sat in the driver’s seat looking at that door for as long as a person can do that before it becomes a thing somebody would notice.

The sun came through the windscreen and laid a warm square across her hands and the top of the wheel.

She lifted her hand again and touched the earring.

Cool. Smooth.

Like the inside of her chest, this morning.

Taeyeon was waiting for him.

He knew it in the way he knows most things, which is not by asking, and the car went along streets he had driven ten thousand times and stopped under a building with lights in five of its windows and one of them on at a brightness that could be identified from the pavement.

There were violets in his arms. He had gone round the long way for them, to a shop in a place that has no reason to sell flowers at that hour, and stood there while a woman who had clearly been closing up chose a bunch of them with an eye for what would last until Friday.

She likes the purple ones. Not showy, and there is a coldness in the smell of them, and it takes a person a second to notice it, and she is, in that respect, a plant.

Up the stairs with the bouquet held at his side, and his shoes being too loud in the stairwell, and the sound of himself going up a floor of somebody’s building on a Monday with flowers in his hand and forty-one years of being a man who does not ask anybody’s permission.

The pink in her was not only in her face. It went from the cheeks to the neck and then into the top of the vest, where it stopped being visible, and there was a warmth under all of it, the mark left by steam, and a peach that has been left on the table too long in a warm room is the only comparison worth making.
