Chapter Twenty-One

The Bully

Ryu Tae-jun watched his goddess — his Irene — laughing until she shook, laughing at some tall stranger’s joke, and the smile he’d assembled for her evaporated like foam off a beer.

His face went dark as a storm front.

“Who the hell is that guy?”

“Tae-jun hyung, I’ve… never seen him before either.” The answer came out careful, the speaker’s eyes flicking sideways — nobody wanted to be standing closest when Tae-jun was in this mood.

“Ssibal!”

The curse hissed out low. Then, in the space of a breath, he manufactured a fresh smile and strode toward the group.

“Seul-gi! Joo-hyun! Morning, you two.” And without even a decent pause: “Joo-hyun — are you free this Saturday?”

The temperature of Irene’s expression dropped ten degrees on contact. Plenty of people had confessed to her over the years; nearly all of them had taken the rejection and kept their distance afterward, like adults. Only this one clung like a plaster you couldn’t peel off. If the harassment kept up, she was going to have to report it and let the company deal with him.

Seulgi was not about to stand there and watch her best friend get cornered. She stepped bodily between them, and her voice came out with edges.

“Yah, Ryu Tae-jun. ‘Seul-gi’? ‘Joo-hyun’? Since when do YOU get to use our bare names? You’ve trained here what — two years?”

The rebuke hit him like a slap. His breath snagged; his face cycled red, then white, then settled on a sullen mumble.

“…Seul-gi sunbae-nim. Joo-hyun sunbae-nim.”

“Hmph. Eonni, let’s go.”

Seul-gi took Joo-hyun’s arm and swept past him without the courtesy of a glance. Soo-young and Seung-wan hurried after — and just before rounding the corner, Seung-wan turned back and mimed a phone at her ear toward Ji-ho. He caught it, smiled, nodded.

When the girls had gone, Ryu Tae-jun sauntered over, hands buried in his pockets. He stood a full head shorter than Ji-ho and compensated by tilting his chin up, contempt dripping from every syllable.

“Yah. Kid. New here, right?”

Inwardly, Ji-ho sighed. The Peninsula’s seniority culture, that beautiful and rotten ladder — how many hearts had it crushed flat? Swallow humiliation from those above you, then collect it back with interest from those below. A perpetual-motion machine of small cruelties.

Outwardly, the standard smile snapped into place, the bow arrived at the regulation angle.

“Hello, sunbae-nim. Ji-ho, new trainee — I just joined. If you’ll excuse me, I was on my way to practice.”

“Yah — who said you could straighten up?”

Tae-jun stepped sideways, planting himself across Ji-ho’s path, voice swelling with borrowed authority.

“Rookies these days — is this what passes for manners? Bend that back down. You hold it until I tell you otherwise. Now!”

Something behind Ji-ho’s eyes went abruptly, dangerously cold. This isn’t etiquette. This is a target on my back. Because of Joo-hyun sunbae?

When Ji-ho didn’t move, Tae-jun’s face flushed crimson, a bull pawing at the dirt, and he bellowed it out one syllable at a time:

“Yah! Are you DEAF? Can you not understand me, or can you not HEAR me? I. Said. BEND. YOUR. BACK!”

And he drove a finger hard into Ji-ho’s chest.

Pain flared white where the finger landed. Ji-ho’s jaw locked — but his face didn’t so much as flicker, and his feet didn’t yield a centimeter.

He pressed the fury down — an ocean of it, boiling in silence. His shoulders, drawn tight as cables, eased by careful degrees. The flash of violence in his eyes was caught and caged before it reached his face. His knuckles whitened at his sides… then, slowly, opened. What finally surfaced was a smile with absolutely nothing in it.

“Sunbae-nim. My apologies — I’m American. Fresh off the plane, you understand, still catching up on this magnificent cultural specialty of yours. Thank you for the ‘instruction.’ I’ll engrave it right here.” A light tap over his heart, exactly where the finger had jabbed. “Now — my vocal coach is waiting. If you’ll excuse me.”

He walked straight past and did not look back.

“Yah — STOP! YAH!”

Tae-jun’s face twisted; he lunged to follow —

“Leave it, Tae-jun hyung.” His companion grabbed his arm, urgent and low. “Half the floor is watching. If the company hears about this, it gets ugly for you. Let it go — there’ll be plenty of chances to sort him out later.”

SM preached etiquette, but it did not tolerate bullying — not officially, not in view of witnesses. Tae-jun stopped. His eyes stayed nailed to Ji-ho’s retreating back, and they were not the eyes of a man letting anything go.

✦   ✦   ✦

Around the corner, out of sight, Ji-ho yanked at his collar and strode fast to the corridor railing. His hands clamped down on the steel. Jawline rigid. Throat working through half a swallow. Lips pressed into one cold, hard line — and in his eyes, just for an instant, something honed and glittering that he did not usually let anyone see.

Swallow the small insult, or forfeit the long game. Swallow it. Swallow it.

He ran the cold arithmetic. On the Peninsula, seniority culture wasn’t a rule — it was gravity. Collide with it head-on and you lost, always, no matter who threw the first stone. Today had been naked bullying, and it wouldn’t matter: the room would read it as a rookie disrespecting a senior. That was simply the operating system this country ran on.

So: no frontal collisions. This needed a cleaner solution — and not just for one Ryu Tae-jun, but for every future someone who looked at the new kid and saw a punching bag.

Whew. One long breath out. The smile reassembled itself, and he walked on to the practice room.

Thirty minutes of warm-up, joint by joint. Then core work, then total immersion.

The afternoon brought the big group classes — dance, then vocals. The room was quietly stuffed with names the future would learn by heart: Nakamoto Yuta, Lee Tae-yong, Jung Jae-hyun. A couple of Chinese trainees too — Hou Minghao, Park Mae-ryun. Ji-ho kept his head down and worked, hour after hour, until the group schedule released them at nine.

No free practice tonight. Tonight was for the job hunt.

He packed up and hauled his tired body out of the practice room. Trainees specialized; not everyone attended every group session — which is why, minutes after he left, Ryu Tae-jun came prowling through the doorway, scanned the room, and found nothing.

He grabbed the nearest newish trainee by the shoulder.

“Yah — Ji-ho. You see him?”

“S-sunbae, he just left.”

“Ssibal.” He let go. “Ji-ho! Lucky little rat. You bought yourself one day!”
